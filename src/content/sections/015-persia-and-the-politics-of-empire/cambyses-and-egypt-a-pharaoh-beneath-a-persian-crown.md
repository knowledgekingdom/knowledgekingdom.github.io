Cyrus died around 530 BCE; the circumstances of his death are uncertain, despite vivid later stories. His son Cambyses II expanded Persian power into Egypt, defeating the Egyptian ruler Psamtik III in 525 BCE. The conquest joined two major ancient political traditions under one imperial dynasty. In Egyptian settings, foreign rulers had to confront the enduring symbolic power of the pharaonic institution, temple economies, and Nile-centered administration.

Greek authors sometimes portray Cambyses as mad, impious or deliberately insulting Egyptian religion. Such stories deserve scrutiny. They may reflect genuine local conflicts, later political propaganda, Greek literary patterns, or combinations of all three. Egyptian evidence shows more complexity than a simple picture of relentless anti-Egyptian policy. The Persian king and his officials had practical reasons to use established Egyptian offices and forms of legitimacy, even if their demands could provoke resentment.

Egypt also reveals the cost of distance. The Nile valley was agriculturally valuable, but its own political traditions and international links made it difficult to govern as an unremarkable district. Persian authority there was challenged repeatedly over the following centuries. The issue was not that Egypt was incapable of being ruled by outsiders—many powers ruled it over time—but that resources, elite loyalties, taxation, garrisons and claims of legitimacy had to be managed continually.
