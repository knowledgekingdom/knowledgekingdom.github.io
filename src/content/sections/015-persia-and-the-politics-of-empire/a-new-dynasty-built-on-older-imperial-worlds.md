Centuries before Cyrus the Great expanded Persian power, the Neo-Assyrian kings had demonstrated what large-scale conquest could achieve: tribute collection, provincial governors, deportations, military garrisons, royal roads and a language of irresistible kingship. After Assyria's fall, the Neo-Babylonian Empire governed much of Mesopotamia and the Levant. Meanwhile Lydia was wealthy in western Anatolia, Media was a major regional force in Iranian and adjacent territories, and Egypt remained a sovereign kingdom. These were not empty spaces waiting for Persia to arrive. They were functioning societies with archives, officials, temples, fortified cities and diplomatic networks.

The Persians did not invent the idea of empire. They inherited and recombined institutions from conquered territories. Babylonian scribes continued to keep accounts in cuneiform; Egyptian administrations could continue to use familiar titles and local laws; Anatolian elites could remain influential under new rulers. This continuity was politically useful. The alternative—replacing every local official with a Persian stranger—would have required information and personnel a sixth-century state did not possess.

Yet continuity does not equal lack of change. An empire can retain a city's tax office while redirecting its revenue to another dynasty. It can respect a temple's rituals while requiring loyalty to a foreign king. A merchant may experience the new peace as an opportunity while a household sent to build a palace experiences the same imperial system as coercion. The question 'Was Persian rule good or bad?' is therefore too blunt. Better questions specify: for whom, in which province, under which king, and measured by what evidence?
