The Persian army was not simply a uniform force of identical soldiers. Imperial mobilization drew on the Persian and Median elite as well as contingents from different subject territories. Infantry, cavalry, archers, engineers and naval forces could be assembled for different campaigns. Some Greek sources describe a select group conventionally called the 'Immortals,' but the army's actual organization and figures are harder to reconstruct than popular retellings imply. Ancient historians frequently give enormous troop totals that modern scholars treat cautiously.

Supplying an army could matter as much as winning a battle. Thousands of people and animals required food, water, fodder, transport and routes that remained passable. A sea crossing or mountain campaign added further constraints. Military campaigns also relied on provincial cooperation: local rulers could provide ships, guides and logistical resources. A king who commanded a very large empire might still find it difficult to bring all available resources to one location at one time.

The great strength of imperial scale—mobilizing from diverse lands—was therefore paired with a problem of coordination. A contingent's training, language, motivation and equipment could differ from that of its neighbors. A successful commander had to translate diverse resources into a functioning operation. Failure in a distant battle did not necessarily mean that the empire was weak everywhere; it could mean that local tactical conditions overcame a strategic advantage in manpower or wealth.
