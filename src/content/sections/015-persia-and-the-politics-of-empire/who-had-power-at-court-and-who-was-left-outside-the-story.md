Kings, royal women, aristocratic families, administrators, soldiers, scribes and provincial elites formed the social infrastructure of Achaemenid government. The court was not a modern cabinet. Authority operated through kinship, personal access, favors, commands, estates and posts. Royal women such as Atossa—well known from Greek literary accounts and genealogical traditions—were visible in succession politics, although later dramatic stories about palace intrigue cannot be taken uncritically. Other women appear less spectacularly but more concretely in administrative records.

An important distinction is between <strong>visibility</strong> and <strong>typicality</strong>. If a princess controlled wealth or a woman supervised workers, that demonstrates a possibility within specific social contexts. It does not mean that ordinary women enjoyed the same opportunities, or that gender hierarchy disappeared. Nor should Greek stories of scheming queens be accepted as an unbiased mirror of Persian female political life. Literary traditions often exaggerate palace sexuality, luxury and intrigue as ways to explain an enemy's supposedly weak government.

Most imperial subjects were not royal. Farmers maintained irrigation, craftspeople produced textiles and vessels, merchants moved goods, and households met local obligations. Some people were enslaved or otherwise compelled to work; forms of labor dependency differed regionally. Our evidence is better for administrative arrangements than for the complete interior lives of families. A careful reconstruction should make ordinary people visible without pretending to know how every individual felt. The best questions ask which groups supplied labor, who controlled land, which institutions allocated food and where direct evidence survives.
