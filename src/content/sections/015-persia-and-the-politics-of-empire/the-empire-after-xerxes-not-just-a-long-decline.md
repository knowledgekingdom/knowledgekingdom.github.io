Popular narratives often end the great Persian story when Xerxes withdrew from Greece. Yet the Achaemenid Empire continued for roughly another century and a half. Under Artaxerxes I and his successors, Persian rulers remained formidable participants in eastern Mediterranean diplomacy and regional politics. Greek cities sometimes sought Persian money or alliances against other Greeks. The Greek world was rarely united, and Persian influence could be exerted through negotiations and subsidies as well as invasion.

Succession contests exposed the potential power of royal relatives and provincial armies. In 401 BCE, Cyrus the Younger challenged his brother Artaxerxes II; the battle at Cunaxa and the subsequent march of Greek mercenaries became famous through Xenophon's <em>Anabasis</em>. This is another reminder that Greek eyewitness narratives tell important parts of Persian history but are shaped by an author's particular experience. The mercenaries' extraordinary return journey does not establish that every Persian army was incapable of effective organization.

The fourth century witnessed periods of revolt, administrative strength, negotiation and reconquest. Labeling the whole period 'decadence' tells us too little. Some central difficulties were real: the immense dynasty had to manage competing aristocratic interests and ambitious governors. Yet it retained major resources and political influence. Historians can identify mechanisms of vulnerability without pretending that a defeat in 490 BCE made the overthrow in 330 BCE inevitable. Political outcomes become clearer when we distinguish what contemporaries could know from what later readers know happened next.
