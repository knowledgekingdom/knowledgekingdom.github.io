Persian rule in Egypt was repeatedly contested, offering a useful counterexample to any claim that Cyrus's dynasty maintained effortless harmony. Cambyses conquered the Nile kingdom in 525 BCE, but later governors and royal commanders had to negotiate or suppress challenges. Egypt regained effective independence during much of the fourth century BCE and was reconquered by Artaxerxes III around 343/342 BCE. The dates reveal a difficult truth of empire: an initial battlefield victory does not establish permanently accepted sovereignty.

Why might a province rebel? Heavy assessments, unpopular officials, local dynastic ambitions, religious tensions, interference from rival powers or a crisis at the center could all matter. We should not presume that every rebellion had one identical cause, or that ancient rebels expressed modern nationalism. Egypt had a deep local political tradition, and elites could seek autonomy, protect existing privileges or establish their own dynasties without using present-day concepts of national self-determination.

The imperial government faced choices with no perfect solution. A large garrison cost resources and might provoke resentment. A powerful local governor could reduce immediate friction but become politically ambitious. Lowering taxes might stabilize the region while leaving the court with less revenue. The system resembles a balancing feedback loop: excessive extraction increases resistance, resistance raises military expenditure, and increased expenditure may pressure the state to extract even more. Whether a particular revolt followed that sequence must be demonstrated rather than assumed.
