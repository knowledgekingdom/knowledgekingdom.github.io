The Achaemenid Empire did not speak with one voice. Old Persian appeared in some royal inscriptions; Elamite and Babylonian cuneiform continued in important contexts; Aramaic served as a widely used administrative and communicative language; Egyptian and numerous local languages remained vital. Greek, Lycian, Lydian and other languages mattered in western provinces, while different linguistic communities lived across Central Asia and the eastern imperial world. The precise distribution of languages changed by place and period.

A practical ruler had little reason to demand that every villager learn Old Persian. What mattered was whether instructions, taxes, legal arrangements and diplomatic correspondence could be understood by those who needed them. Translation and bilingual officials therefore possessed political power. A local petition could change meaning when moved between scripts or summarized for someone at court. Language policy was both an instrument of inclusion—allowing local record keeping to continue—and a potential barrier to those who lacked access to translators and scribes.

The three languages of the Behistun inscription and the Elamite administrative texts of Persepolis expose a crucial distinction: <strong>royal identity is not the same thing as administrative language</strong>. We must also avoid imagining Aramaic as the only language used by all imperial officials. A state can be cohesive without linguistic uniformity, provided its communication network can handle difference. This is a valuable comparison with early Egypt and Mesopotamia, where particular writing systems held central political roles under very different administrative conditions.
