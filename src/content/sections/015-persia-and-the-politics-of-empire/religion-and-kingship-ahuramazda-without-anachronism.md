Darius and some of his successors invoked Ahuramazda, a major Iranian deity, as the source of royal favor and the protector of truth and order. This connection is central to Achaemenid kingship. It does not automatically answer every question about the development of Zoroastrianism, the historical Zarathustra, or the beliefs of the empire's varied populations. Scholars debate how closely the royal religious language corresponds to later Zoroastrian ritual and doctrine. The dates and early history of Zoroastrian traditions themselves are contested.

Royal ideology made 'truth' a political term. At Behistun, Darius associates his opponents with lies and himself with legitimate authority. This can illuminate genuine values attached to justice and order while also serving a partisan purpose: the king claims the authority to decide who is a liar. Such language is especially powerful when inscribed in stone for future generations. The existence of divine language does not mean that every Persian decision followed one uniform theological code.

Across the empire, local cults continued. Egyptian temples, Babylonian divine images and religious communities in the Levant were embedded in political relationships. Imperial policy depended on specific circumstances. The simple assertion 'Persia practiced religious freedom' fails to capture this variety, just as 'Persia suppressed all foreign religion' is plainly inaccurate. A better historical description is that rulers frequently worked through local religious institutions while retaining the power to intervene, redistribute resources or punish opposition.
