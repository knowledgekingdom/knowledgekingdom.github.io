Put Persia beside Mesopotamia. Sumerian city-states developed administrative writing and mechanisms for mobilizing labor, and later Mesopotamian kingdoms built imperial traditions. The Persians inherited sophisticated Mesopotamian systems while integrating regions far beyond the Tigris and Euphrates. Put Persia beside Egypt: pharaonic monarchy had a long-standing cultural and territorial core, whereas Persian kings needed to govern Egypt as one part of a larger multilingual realm. Put Persia beside early China: the Zhou and Shang demonstrate other models of dynastic legitimacy and regional delegation, but their political worlds and surviving evidence differed.

The useful comparison is not 'which civilization was most advanced?' It is a set of precise questions. Did central authority depend on direct appointments or hereditary intermediaries? How was agricultural surplus collected? Which languages were used in records? How did kings justify their positions? How often did political authority outlast a succession crisis? How did governments use transportation and official information? Those questions produce historical understanding rather than a scoreboard.

There is also a warning about scale. The Persian Empire was unusually large by the standards of its era, but geography alone cannot measure state capacity. The ability to command soldiers at a frontier depends on supply and compliance, not merely on a colored area on a modern map. A province shown as imperial may have retained considerable local discretion; an independent city may have been economically dependent on imperial trade. Maps are essential and still incomplete.
