A price index takes many changing prices and turns them into a single measure of change under a specified weighting method. Imagine a household buys, in one fictional year, 10 loaves of bread at €2 and five bottles of oil at €4, for a total basket cost of €40. A year later bread costs €2.20 and oil €4.40. The same basket costs €44: an increase of 10 percent. A <strong>fixed-basket price index</strong> with the first year set at 100 would read 110 in the second year. That figure does not mean every individual's cost of living increased exactly 10 percent; spending patterns differ, and an official index uses many more goods, methodological rules and sometimes changing weights.

A <strong>base year</strong> supplies the reference value, usually set to 100. If a productivity index rises from 100 to 125, the measured quantity increased 25 percent relative to that base. If the index rises from 125 to 150 the following year, that second move is 25 index points but only 20 percent relative growth from 125. Confusing percentage change with index-point change can exaggerate a trend. The same arithmetic appears in stock-market indices, economic forecasts, scientific measurement and environmental monitoring.

A <strong>nominal</strong> amount is measured in current money; a <strong>real</strong> amount accounts for changes in an appropriate price index. Suppose an annual salary rises from €20,000 to €22,000 while the relevant price index rises from 100 to 110. The nominal salary rose 10 percent, but purchasing power measured against that particular basket is unchanged: €22,000/1.10 = €20,000 in base-year purchasing-power terms. This is a simplified illustration. In practice, household consumption patterns and tax circumstances complicate the meaning of “real income.”

An index is a practical compression device, not a complete description of lived experience. If rent rises much faster than food, renters and homeowners may experience the same headline inflation differently. Ask which basket, whose spending, which year, and which adjustment. These questions are essential to the economics chapters ahead.
