A raw count tells you how many events occurred. A <strong>rate</strong> connects events to a relevant amount of exposure. Suppose Hospital A reports 120 complications after 4,000 procedures while Hospital B reports 75 after 1,500. Hospital A has more recorded complications, but its crude rate is 120/4,000 = 3 percent, compared with 75/1,500 = 5 percent for Hospital B. If the patients and procedures differ in difficulty, however, even these crude rates are not a fair comparison of clinical quality. Denominators make comparisons possible; they do not solve every comparability problem.

Different questions call for different denominators. For traffic injuries, exposure might mean residents, kilometres travelled, or vehicle journeys. For infectious disease, rates can be calculated per population at risk over a specified time. For shop theft, possibilities include transactions, store visits, or items displayed. For election results, there is a major difference between 40 percent of eligible voters and 40 percent of votes actually cast. The phrase “half of people” hides more than it reveals if you cannot say which people.

A rate also needs a <strong>time period</strong>. Ten incidents per thousand residents per year cannot be compared directly with ten incidents per thousand over an entire decade. Other measurement choices matter: one organization might record a complaint as soon as it arrives, while another records it only after investigation. A change in record-keeping can resemble a change in behavior. Whenever a number suddenly rises, ask whether the world changed, the counting changed, or both.

For readability, rates are often expressed per 1,000, per 10,000, or per 100,000. The choice of scale does not alter the underlying ratio. Fifteen incidents among 30,000 people is 0.0005 per person, 0.5 per thousand, or 50 per 100,000. The larger units simply make a small fraction easier to see. Such calculations appear constantly in demography, public health, crime reporting, transport and economics.
