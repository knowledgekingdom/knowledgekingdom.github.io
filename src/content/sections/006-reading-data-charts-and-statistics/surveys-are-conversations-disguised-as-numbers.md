Imagine asking, “Do you support protecting hard-working families from unfair increases in public transport fares?” Now imagine asking, “Should fares be frozen even if that requires higher taxes or reduced service?” Respondents may hold the same underlying preferences, yet the wording directs attention to different trade-offs. Neither question is automatically dishonest, but responses are not interchangeable. Good surveys avoid loaded language, vague terms and forced choices that hide meaningful alternatives.

Order matters. Asking people whether crime has worsened before asking about trust in institutions may produce different answers than reversing the questions. An interviewer can influence results through tone, presence or perceived expectations. People sometimes report socially desirable behavior rather than what they actually do. “How often do you exercise?” may elicit aspirations rather than precise records. Anonymous questionnaires may reduce some pressures, but they create other limitations.

A survey report should explain who was eligible, who was contacted, how contact occurred, when the interviews took place, the exact wording of important questions, the sample size, whether responses were weighted, and any uncertainty measures. <strong>Weighting</strong> may correct known imbalances. For example, if a sample is 70 percent urban but the target population is 50 percent urban, analysts may assign different weights to urban and rural respondents. But weight does not conjure missing information about unobserved attitudes. Weights themselves can increase the variability of estimates, reducing the effective sample size.

An instructive example is the difference between a poll of viewers who voluntarily click a button and a survey designed to estimate national opinion. Both produce percentages. Only one may have a defensible route from measured respondents to the target population. <strong>The representativeness of the route, not the visual confidence of the pie chart, is what matters.</strong>
