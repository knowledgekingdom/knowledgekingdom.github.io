<strong>Case A: The struggling shop.</strong> A family shop notices that daily revenue fell by 8 percent from €500 to €460. Management concludes that fewer people are visiting. But a check of transaction counts shows 50 transactions yesterday and 46 today; average basket value remained €10. That is evidence compatible with fewer transactions, though a two-day comparison is too short to establish a trend. Next, management discovers that one day was a public holiday, a competitor offered a promotion, and card payments briefly failed. The meaningful analysis compares suitable days, checks total customers and margins, and separates temporary events from lasting changes. The lesson is that good operational statistics depend on the decision being made.

<strong>Case B: Two schools.</strong> School North has an overall pass rate of 85 percent and School South 75 percent. A headline declares North “better.” But suppose North enrolls a larger share of students who entered with stronger preparation. Without accounting for prior attainment and student selection, raw rates do not isolate teaching quality. Even an adjusted comparison would need to consider how preparation was measured, which students left, and whether tests assessed the same skills. The ethical lesson is not to refuse all comparisons; it is to avoid assigning blame or praise based on a statistic unable to answer the question posed.

<strong>Case C: The city and its crime chart.</strong> A municipal report finds theft complaints rose from 800 to 960 over four years, an increase of 20 percent. During that period, city population grew from 100,000 to 120,000. The complaints per 100,000 residents stayed at 800 in both years: 800/100,000 × 100,000 = 800; 960/120,000 × 100,000 = 800. That does not prove individual victimization risk stayed exactly constant. Population may be a poor exposure proxy if commuters, tourists or reporting behavior changed. Still, the rate prevents an easy error: equating a rise in total reports with a rise in reports per resident.

In each case, the decisive skill is not advanced algebra. It is a sequence of questions: What exactly happened? Compared with what? Measured how? Among whom? What alternatives could produce the same pattern? Which additional data would actually change the decision? Learning to ask these questions will be more useful than memorizing dozens of statistical tests without understanding their purpose.
