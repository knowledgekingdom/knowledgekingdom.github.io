Suppose a party's share of the vote rises from 20 percent to 25 percent. Its share rose by <strong>five percentage points</strong>, but the relative increase was (25 − 20)/20 = 25 percent. These are different statements. If the share falls from 25 percent back to 20 percent, that is a fall of five points, or a 20 percent relative decline. Percentages do not reverse symmetrically. A 50 percent loss requires a 100 percent gain to return to the original quantity.

Other numerical statements hide a baseline. “Sales increased by 200 percent” means the new level is three times the initial level, not twice. A rise from 10 to 30 is a 200 percent increase. “Sales are 200 percent of last year's level” means the new value is twice the original. Words such as “increased <em>to</em>” and “increased <em>by</em>” are not interchangeable. Before repeating a dramatic percentage, translate it back into an absolute before-and-after pair.

Risk reduction creates a similar trap. A fictional treatment reduces an event rate from 2 per 100 people to 1 per 100. The <strong>relative risk reduction</strong> is 50 percent. The <strong>absolute risk reduction</strong> is 1 percentage point, or one fewer event per hundred treated under the stated conditions. Both are useful, but they answer different questions. Anyone discussing policy, medicine or public safety should learn to present both when relevant. A vivid relative figure without the baseline can inflate perceived impact.

A strong statistical explanation may therefore use three sentences rather than one: <em>The rate fell from 2 percent to 1 percent. That is a one-percentage-point absolute reduction and a 50 percent relative reduction. The interpretation depends on the study design, follow-up period, and whom it measured.</em> The added context is not pedantry; it is a safeguard against accidental exaggeration.
