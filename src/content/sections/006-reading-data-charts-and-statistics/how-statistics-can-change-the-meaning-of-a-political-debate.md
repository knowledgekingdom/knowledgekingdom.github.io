Take the claim, “Living standards improved.” It could refer to higher average income, higher median income, longer life expectancy, lower poverty, better housing, or a composite index. Each is a legitimate dimension, but none captures well-being in its entirety. A country might enjoy rising GDP while housing affordability deteriorates. Another might experience improving health outcomes despite weak income growth. The choice of indicator shapes the story that follows.

International comparisons add exchange rates and <strong>purchasing-power parity</strong> issues. Converting local income to euros at market exchange rates answers one question about international purchasing power and financial transactions. Adjusting for local prices can better approximate how much local goods and services a household can buy. Neither method perfectly compares quality of life, informal activity, public services or distribution. A table that ranks countries to three decimal places may still contain wide conceptual uncertainty.

Political claims about crime, migration, education and public spending often rely on <strong>ecological</strong> or <strong>compositional</strong> effects. A city may change because its population changed, because individuals' outcomes changed, because reporting changed, or because all three occurred. A calm argument distinguishes the change being measured from the causal explanation being proposed. Someone can agree on a reliable statistic and still reasonably disagree about its social importance or appropriate policy response.

Statistics should therefore be understood as a language of public reasoning rather than a rhetorical weapon. Their best role is to make competing descriptions testable, reveal uncertainty, and locate real disagreement. The aim is not to “win with numbers” but to ask better questions of the world.
