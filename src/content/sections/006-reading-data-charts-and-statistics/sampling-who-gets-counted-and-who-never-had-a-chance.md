Imagine interviewing 800 visitors outside an expensive shopping mall and announcing that 74 percent of the country's adults favor a new luxury tax. Your sample is large, your arithmetic may be immaculate, and your conclusion can still be deeply misleading. You did not sample the country's adults; you sampled people available at one particular location and time. Some kinds of people may have been much more likely to appear than others. An impressive number of answers does not repair a distorted <strong>sampling frame</strong>.

A <strong>probability sample</strong> is selected using a design with known selection probabilities, allowing principled estimates of sampling uncertainty under the design's assumptions. A <strong>simple random sample</strong> chooses units so that every possible sample of a given size is equally likely. In practice, researchers often use <strong>stratified sampling</strong>, selecting from subgroups such as regions or age ranges; <strong>cluster sampling</strong>, selecting groups such as schools or neighborhoods; or multistage mixtures. These designs can improve feasibility and coverage, although clustering can also make results less precise than a simple random sample of the same size.

A <strong>convenience sample</strong> simply measures whoever is easy to reach. A large online poll can produce interesting information about its respondents without representing the population of a nation. A popular social-media account attracts followers with particular interests; its audience is not automatically the public at large. <strong>Selection bias</strong> occurs when the selection mechanism is associated with what is being measured. If night-shift workers cannot answer a midday telephone survey and their views differ from daytime workers, the missing group matters.

<strong>Nonresponse bias</strong> is a related danger. Even a carefully selected probability sample can become unrepresentative if the people who refuse to respond differ systematically from respondents. A response rate alone cannot quantify the direction of the bias, and statistical weighting cannot guarantee that unmeasured differences have disappeared. The essential distinction is between <em>random uncertainty</em>—the variation expected from sampling—and <em>systematic error</em>—the way a flawed design or measurement process pushes estimates away from reality. Making a biased sample bigger usually makes the wrong estimate more stable, not more correct.
