A dataset often contains blanks. Perhaps a household declined to report income; a temperature sensor failed; a shop's point-of-sale terminal was offline. It is tempting to discard incomplete rows and proceed. But if missingness is associated with the values you want to understand, deletion can systematically distort results. People with very high or very low incomes may be less willing to report them. Remote stations may fail more often during severe weather—the very conditions an analyst wishes to measure.

Some missing values are plausibly unrelated to the quantity being studied; others depend on recorded information; still others depend on the missing value itself. Statistical methods for handling these situations differ and rely on assumptions that are not always testable. Simple <strong>imputation</strong>—replacing blanks with a mean—can make a spreadsheet appear tidy while underestimating variation or obscuring correlations. An honest dataset may need a warning label rather than an artificial sense of completeness.

Official statistics also get revised. An initial economic estimate may use incomplete business reports; later figures incorporate additional data or improved methods. A revision does not necessarily signal deception. Scientists and statistical agencies update measurements as evidence improves. Yet quiet changes of definition, boundary, or methodology can make historical comparisons hazardous. Good data practice keeps a record of when each figure was obtained, how it was defined, and which version was used.

<strong>Data provenance</strong> is the journey from reality to a public number. Trace the measurement device or questionnaire, the collection institution, exclusions, cleaning rules, transformations, aggregations, and publication. If a headline cites a chart that cites a report that cites a survey, the survey's methods—not the elegance of the latest infographic—must carry the evidential weight. Traceback is the numerical equivalent of checking a historian's primary source.
