In 27 BCE, Octavian received the honorific name <strong>Augustus</strong>, a conventional marker for the beginning of the Roman imperial period. He preferred to present himself as <em>princeps</em>, or first citizen, rather than king. The Senate continued to meet; magistracies and assemblies still existed. But the decisive military commands, financial resources and political influence were concentrated in the emperor's hands. The arrangement we call the <strong>Principate</strong> rested on old republican language combined with a fundamentally new distribution of power.

Augustus ended cycles of civil war and expanded administrative capacity. He reorganized military service, supported veteran settlements, patronized major building projects and cultivated images of moral restoration. His famous <em>Res Gestae</em>, an account of his achievements displayed after his death, is valuable evidence for the message he wanted remembered. It should not be taken as a neutral audit of his reign. The Ara Pacis and imperial coins likewise reveal a program of legitimacy: Rome was told that personal authority brought peace, prosperity and stability.

The phrase <strong>Pax Romana</strong>, or Roman peace, can obscure who experienced peace and at whose expense. Many communities enjoyed comparatively secure long-distance travel and regular administration; frontier campaigns and provincial resistance did not disappear. An emperor could reduce civil war in the center while ordering conquest on the edges. Augustus devised a durable political settlement, but he left a difficult problem unresolved: how could a regime that denied being a monarchy reliably select the next monarch?
