Roman imperial government was not a single unchanging machine. Augustus's immediate successors included Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius and Nero. After Nero's death in 68 CE, civil war demonstrated that the armies could help determine who ruled. The so-called <strong>Year of the Four Emperors</strong> ended with Vespasian's victory and the establishment of the Flavian dynasty. The Senate retained prestige, yet imperial authority depended crucially on military support, court relationships, provincial networks and the perceived ability to govern.

Later rulers such as Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius are sometimes grouped as the 'Five Good Emperors.' That label reflects a selective evaluation, not an objective ranking applying equally to every province or enslaved person. Trajan's campaigns expanded the empire, including into Dacia north of the Danube; Hadrian consolidated frontiers and spent considerable time traveling. Marcus Aurelius governed amid war and epidemic. Imperial personality could affect administration greatly, but institutions and structural problems shaped what any individual could accomplish.

Adoption sometimes facilitated succession, but a system based on family power, army backing and elite acceptance never had a fully secure mechanism for peaceful transfer. Praetorian guards, provincial armies and rival claimants could intervene. Some emperors reigned for decades; others for months. Measuring stability requires looking beyond the number of rulers to the security of roads, taxes, settlements and institutions in different provinces. An empire could have turmoil at court while particular cities continued ordinary life, and the reverse was also possible.
