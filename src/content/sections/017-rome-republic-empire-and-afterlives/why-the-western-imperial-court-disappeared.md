The traditional date <strong>476 CE</strong> marks the deposition of Romulus Augustulus in Italy by Odoacer, usually described as the end of the western Roman imperial office. The western empire's political authority had already changed considerably, and there were other rival claimants; Julius Nepos, recognized by the eastern court, remained alive until 480. Treating 476 as the instant when all Roman civilization vanished is therefore misleading.

The western court faced overlapping strains: recurring civil wars, difficulties maintaining armies and tax revenue, negotiations and conflicts with federated military groups, the loss of North African revenues to the Vandals, and changing regional power. Groups often labeled 'barbarians' were not one homogeneous people attacking from a mysterious outside. Gothic, Vandal, Frankish and other leaders negotiated with Romans, served in imperial armies, settled within imperial territories and established successor kingdoms. Some claimed to preserve Roman administrative forms while changing rulers and military loyalties.

Historians debate the relative importance of fiscal structures, political instability, military shifts, migrations, environmental shocks, demographic change and contingent decisions. No single cause explains every region or every decade. The western imperial center disappeared, but cities, roads, estates, Christian institutions, Latin writing and elements of Roman law survived in varied forms. The process was often violent and disruptive, but 'collapse' must identify <strong>what</strong> collapsed: a court, a taxation system, long-distance networks, urban life, military command, or something else. These did not all end at the same time.
