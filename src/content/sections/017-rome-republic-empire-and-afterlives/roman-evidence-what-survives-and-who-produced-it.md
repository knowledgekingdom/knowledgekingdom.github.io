Roman historians and writers include <strong>Polybius</strong>, <strong>Cicero</strong>, <strong>Livy</strong>, <strong>Sallust</strong>, <strong>Tacitus</strong>, <strong>Suetonius</strong>, <strong>Cassius Dio</strong> and <strong>Ammianus Marcellinus</strong>. Their works differ in genre, dates, political sympathies and access to records. A senatorial historian describing an emperor may express the interests of an elite frustrated by imperial interference. A panegyric praises a patron by design. A legal text defines rules, not necessarily everyday compliance. The most cautious historian asks what a source was created to accomplish.

Evidence goes well beyond books. Inscriptions commemorate offices, public honors, soldiers and private individuals. Coins advertise rulers' images, victories and ideals. Pottery and shipwrecks illuminate trade. Housing archaeology reveals wealth and crowding. Human remains can reveal health and diet, although interpretation requires care. The destruction of Pompeii and Herculaneum in 79 CE preserved extraordinary urban material; it did not freeze a representative sample of the entire empire. The Vindolanda tablets preserve details of everyday communication on a frontier that elite literary narratives often ignore.

A productive <strong>source triangulation</strong> exercise compares different perspectives on the same question. To understand Augustus, put his <em>Res Gestae</em> beside the evidence of provincial administration and a later historian. To study imperial food supply, combine public statements about generosity with grain-storage remains and shipping evidence. To study legal equality, read a citizenship edict and ask what changed for a poor resident in a specific province. The sources are rich enough for complicated answers; simplistic slogans are often a choice rather than a necessity.
