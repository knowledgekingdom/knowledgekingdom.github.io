Later Roman chronology placed the expulsion of the last king, Tarquinius Superbus, around 509 BCE and regarded the event as the beginning of the Republic. The early details are difficult to reconstruct securely. The familiar tale includes the assault on Lucretia and the leadership of Lucius Junius Brutus. These accounts became potent moral stories about abuses of monarchical power, family honor and public liberty; they should not be read as transparent eyewitness accounts of the transition.

In the republican system that developed, citizens elected senior magistrates to hold office for limited periods. Two consuls commonly shared the highest ordinary civil and military authority. Other officers and assemblies gradually acquired recognizable functions. Competition among aristocratic households was intense, but ideals of rotation, collegiality and accountability discouraged a permanent royal office. The system changed considerably over time; there was no single constitution published in 509 BCE in the modern sense.

The Roman hatred of <em>regnum</em>, or kingship, became part of political rhetoric. Roman leaders could attack opponents as would-be kings while seeking exceptional commands themselves. That made accusations of tyranny politically effective but not automatically accurate. The Republic's distinctive achievement was a durable language of public magistracy, law and shared government. Its central weakness was that social power remained unequal and its institutions assumed that ambitious elites would accept common limits when defeated.
