Set Rome beside the Achaemenid Empire of Chapter 15. Both used provincial officials, communication routes, local elites, taxation and military force. Each made claims about legitimate rule. Yet their institutions differed in historical circumstances, language, citizenship arrangements and the relation between capital and territories. Neither can be reduced to the claim that 'good roads make strong empires.' Roads help only if the state can finance them, protect travelers and mobilize useful resources through them.

Set Rome beside Classical Athens. Both celebrated civic freedom among recognized citizens, yet Rome's formal politics relied more strongly on aristocratic patronage, hierarchically weighted assemblies and long-term magistrate competition. Roman citizenship eventually spread much more widely across imperial territory, while political control simultaneously became concentrated in emperors. Wider membership did not guarantee greater political participation. This contrast helps us separate the scale of inclusion from the distribution of governing power.

Finally, compare the western court with the eastern imperial state after 476. The history resists the notion that civilizations have fixed life cycles like organisms. Institutions endure, fail, transform, fragment and recombine under particular conditions. Some Roman achievements—including jurisprudence, administration, engineering and literature—were extraordinary. Other features—including slavery, conquest, spectacular violence and severe social inequality—must be equally visible. <strong>Admiration for an achievement is not an obligation to excuse its costs.</strong>
