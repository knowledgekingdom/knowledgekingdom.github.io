Ancient Rome was not only an Italian or western European story. The lands of today's Bulgaria, Romania, Greece, Serbia, North Macedonia and neighboring areas belonged to different political and cultural worlds before, during and after Roman expansion. Roman authorities established provinces including <strong>Moesia</strong>, <strong>Thracia</strong> and later <strong>Dacia</strong>; names, borders and administrative divisions changed. Greek remained influential in much of the eastern Mediterranean, while Latin military and administrative practices spread in different degrees. Roman roads connected Serdica (modern Sofia), Philippopolis (Plovdiv), the Danube and routes toward Byzantium.

Local Thracian traditions did not disappear on the day provincial authority arrived. Settlements, languages, cults and social practices interacted with military bases, towns, markets and imperial administration. Some inhabitants gained wealth or civic status through service and trade; others endured taxation, conscription, land loss or warfare. Roman citizenship and provincial life were layered identities, not a demand that everyone become culturally identical to a resident of Rome.

Several later Roman emperors came from the wider Balkan and Danubian regions, reflecting the military and administrative importance of the area in the third and fourth centuries. This regional connection is useful for readers of southeastern European history: Rome is visible not only in distant monuments but in archaeological sites, roads, urban layouts, inscriptions and the historical development of local religious institutions. Yet it would be anachronistic to label ancient provincial populations by modern national identities.
