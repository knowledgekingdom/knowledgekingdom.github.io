Christianity originated in the eastern Roman world in the first century CE within a Jewish historical and religious context. Jesus of Nazareth was executed under Roman authority; the movement that formed around his followers spread through cities, households and trade networks. Early Christian communities were diverse in theology and practice. Paul's letters and other early Christian writings are important evidence, but they are religious texts created for particular communities, not neutral demographic surveys.

Roman authorities did not pursue one constant empire-wide policy toward Christians across three centuries. Persecution varied by time and place. Some rulers and governors took violent measures; at other times communities developed with less direct interference. Under <strong>Diocletian</strong>, a severe persecution began in 303 CE. In <strong>313 CE</strong>, Constantine and Licinius issued measures associated with the <strong>Edict of Milan</strong>, establishing toleration for Christianity and addressing confiscated property. Constantine's support helped transform Christianity's public position, but the empire did not become instantly or uniformly Christian.

Later fourth-century imperial patronage, councils and legislation shaped Christian institutions. Theodosius I promoted Nicene Christianity and restricted certain traditional cult practices. The transition was contested and regionally uneven; pagan religious life did not end everywhere on a single date. Christian bishops acquired growing social and sometimes political roles, while doctrinal disputes divided communities. This history is essential for understanding medieval Europe and the eastern Mediterranean, but it should not be written as a simple replacement story in which one belief system automatically caused the demise of Roman government.
