Roman tradition assigned the founding of Rome to 753 BCE. One famous legend describes the twins Romulus and Remus, their miraculous survival, and a bitter contest that ends with Romulus founding the city. Other traditions connect Rome to the Trojan hero Aeneas and thereby to the heroic world of Greek epic. These stories told later Romans who they thought they were. They do not supply a reliable diary of the eighth century BCE. Archaeologists do find evidence of settlements in and around Rome during the early first millennium BCE, but the exact act of 'foundation' is a much less precise historical category than the legend suggests.

Ancient authors such as Livy wrote hundreds of years after many of the events in their earliest books. They drew on annals, family traditions, earlier histories and stories shaped by civic pride. Their accounts are invaluable evidence for Roman memory and moral ideals, but they must be compared with inscriptions, archaeology and the wider Italian context. A dramatic speech in Livy's narrative is not a recorded transcript; the historian has constructed a persuasive scene for readers.

This is a recurring lesson from Chapter 4: <strong>a source can be genuine yet unreliable for one particular claim</strong>. A coin made under Augustus may accurately reveal the message that Augustus wanted circulated without showing that every resident accepted it. A triumphal monument proves that someone celebrated a victory, not that the opponent suffered exactly the losses claimed. Roman history is unusually rich in evidence, and the abundance makes source criticism more—not less—important.
