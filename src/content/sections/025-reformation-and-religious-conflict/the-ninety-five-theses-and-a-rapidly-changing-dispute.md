On 31 October 1517, Luther sent his Ninety-Five Theses, written in Latin for academic disputation, to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz. The famous story that he nailed them to a church door has a long tradition but is not supported with the same certainty as the circulation of his text. Within weeks printed editions appeared in different towns. Readers who did not understand university Latin encountered summaries, translations, sermons and arguments, sometimes accompanied by images. A local controversy entered a much broader political and religious public sphere.

The Ninety-Five Theses questioned the preaching and effects of indulgences. They did not contain every doctrine later associated with Lutheran churches. The process was interactive: critics challenged Luther, readers circulated pamphlets, patrons shielded him, and the arguments sharpened. One reason print was transformative was not simply its speed but its ability to create many roughly similar copies of a claim, making replies, citations and counterattacks easier. At the same time, typographical errors, selective quotations and inflammatory illustrations could make controversies less rather than more accurate.

Avoid a common hindsight error: treating Luther's break with Rome as inevitable the moment he wrote the theses. Many contemporaries expected disputes to end in academic correction or negotiated reform. Contingency mattered. A different papal response, a different political patron, or different imperial priorities might have changed the immediate sequence, although the wider sources of discontent would have remained.
