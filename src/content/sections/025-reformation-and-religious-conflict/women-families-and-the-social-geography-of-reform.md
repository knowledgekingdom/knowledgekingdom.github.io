Religious reform changed family life. Clerical marriage in many Protestant territories created new pastoral households. Sermons and printed guides debated marriage, child-rearing, household discipline and the obligations of husbands and wives. Protestant opponents of monastic life argued that marriage and ordinary work were genuine Christian vocations. Catholic reformers continued to defend religious orders while also emphasizing disciplined family devotion. Neither side produced an uncomplicated advance toward gender equality.

Some women published religious writing, participated in networks of correspondence and acted as patrons or defenders of reform. Argula von Grumbach publicly defended reformist positions; Katharina Schütz Zell helped minister to refugees and supported Protestant causes in Strasbourg. Catholic women such as Teresa of Ávila shaped spiritual renewal and institutions. Their opportunities were constrained by censorship, legal status, family resources and the assumption among many male leaders that public preaching belonged primarily to men. An educated woman could be celebrated for piety and silenced when she challenged authority.

The burdens of change were also unevenly distributed. The closure of convents removed some routes to female learning and community life while enabling others to leave institutions they had not freely chosen. Marriage reform affected widows, property inheritance and household work. Women who maintained prohibited worship often sustained religious traditions through domestic networks. A chapter concerned only with formal church decrees would miss these relationships.
