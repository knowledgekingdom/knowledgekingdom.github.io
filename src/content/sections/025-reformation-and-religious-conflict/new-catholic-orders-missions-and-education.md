Ignatius of Loyola and companions founded the Society of Jesus, formally approved in 1540. Jesuits became especially known for education, spiritual exercises, scholarship, missions and service within Catholic political and religious institutions. Their institutions could train elite students, conduct overseas missions and participate in confessional controversy. They were neither the sole force behind Catholic reform nor a uniform secret political organization. Like other institutions, they had competing local priorities and sometimes disagreed with rulers or fellow churchmen.

Renewal also involved women. Teresa of Ávila promoted reform of Carmelite life in Spain, combining demanding religious practices with influential writing; other women sustained convents, charity, devotional movements and education. Yet reform often tightened enclosure rules and subjected female communities to male ecclesiastical supervision. The story therefore combines opportunities for religious authorship and institutional leadership with new restrictions. Religious life cannot be measured by the biographies of famous men alone.

Catholic missions expanded in connection with Iberian maritime empires and other trading networks. Jesuits such as Francis Xavier worked across the Indian Ocean and East Asia; missionaries engaged in translation and cultural negotiation in India, Japan and China. These efforts took place amid colonial domination, commerce, coercion, voluntary conversion and local adaptation. Some missionaries criticized abuses; others participated in systems that benefited from imperial power. Saying religion spread with empire is a starting observation, not a complete judgment about the motives and experiences of people who encountered it.
