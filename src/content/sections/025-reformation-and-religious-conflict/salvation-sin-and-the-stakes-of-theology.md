Many Reformation disputes sound abstract until we appreciate what was at stake for their participants. Medieval Latin theology described a relationship among divine grace, faith, repentance, Christian works and sacramental life. Believers worried not only about wrongdoing in daily life but about judgment and purgatory, understood in Catholic teaching as purification after death for those destined for salvation. Masses, prayers, confession, penance and works of charity formed part of the religious landscape. Different theologians disagreed about how to express these relationships, and religious practice was not identical to textbook theology.

Indulgences were especially sensitive. In Catholic teaching, an indulgence concerned remission of temporal punishment due to sin after guilt had been forgiven; it was not legitimately a license to commit sin or a straightforward purchase of divine forgiveness. Yet the funding of some indulgence campaigns through contributions, and the dramatic language of popular promotion, blurred these distinctions. A parishioner hearing that a contribution could help a deceased relative might understand the message differently from a university theologian. When an official explanation requires fine distinctions but the commercial system rewards persuasive slogans, conflict becomes likely.

The religious seriousness of ordinary participants matters. Someone who challenged a preacher might believe that souls were being misled. A church official defending the system might believe that attacks threatened confession, sacramental authority and the unity of Christian communities. We do not need to accept either view as theological truth to understand why these controversies provoked intense responses. Historical explanation begins by reconstructing the actors' beliefs accurately, then examining the institutional incentives and practical consequences attached to those beliefs. \[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]
