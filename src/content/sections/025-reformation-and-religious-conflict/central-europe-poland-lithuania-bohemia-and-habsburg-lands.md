Central and eastern European religious change defies a simple Catholic-Protestant divide. In the Polish-Lithuanian world, Catholic, Orthodox, Jewish and several Protestant communities lived within a politically plural Commonwealth after the Union of Lublin in 1569. The Warsaw Confederation of 1573 offered significant guarantees of religious peace among the political nobility, although it did not establish universal equality or erase later coercion. Religious freedom there had a particular legal and social scope: privileges of estates were not identical to personal rights held by every inhabitant.

Bohemian and Moravian traditions included descendants of earlier Hussite reforms. The Habsburg monarchy ruled over lands with varied institutional histories, and negotiations over estates, worship and political allegiance repeatedly produced conflict. In Hungary and Transylvania, Lutheran, Reformed, Catholic and Unitarian currents intersected with Ottoman pressure and competing political authorities. Transylvania's 1568 Diet of Torda is often remembered for a comparatively broad religious settlement, though the terms applied to recognized religious communities and did not create modern universal tolerance.

These examples are important because confessional history cannot be told only through Luther, Calvin, Henry VIII and the pope. Some regions created practical coexistence through legal privileges, mixed local jurisdictions or political bargains. Yet compromise could weaken or disappear when rulers, estates or external powers changed.
