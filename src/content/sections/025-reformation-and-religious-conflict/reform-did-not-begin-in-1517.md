Criticism of church practice long predated Luther. Medieval monastic reforms tried to enforce religious discipline; mendicant movements emphasized preaching and poverty; church councils debated the limits of papal authority. John Wycliffe in late fourteenth-century England challenged aspects of ecclesiastical wealth and teaching. In Bohemia, Jan Hus criticized clerical abuses and became a focus of a broad religious and national-political movement. His execution for heresy in 1415 did not end the Hussite challenge; wars, negotiations and distinct worship traditions followed. Historians must avoid treating these movements as merely unsuccessful early versions of later Lutheranism: they arose in different settings and pursued different goals.

Fifteenth-century reform currents also operated within Catholic institutions. Calls for better episcopal residence, stricter monastic rules and more educated clergy could attract sincere supporters who never embraced Protestant doctrines. Humanists such as Erasmus compared manuscripts, promoted learning and criticized empty ritual and abusive conduct, while remaining committed to church unity. The Renaissance methods considered in Chapter 24 made inherited texts newly accessible to critical examination, but close reading alone did not dictate which religious conclusions a scholar would reach.

These older histories make clear why a single-cause explanation fails. Printing helped Luther's controversy expand, but printing did not invent the dissatisfaction. Disputes about authority preceded the press, and some rulers benefited from church institutions too much to welcome sudden change. A better model is a combustible environment: existing reform expectations, distinctive theological claims, urban debates, printing businesses and powerful political patrons began to reinforce one another after 1517. \[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]
