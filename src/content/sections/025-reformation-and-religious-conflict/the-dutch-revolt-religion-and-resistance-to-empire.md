The Low Countries were economically dynamic territories of the Habsburg monarchy, containing important cities, ports and trading networks. Reformed Protestantism gained ground in some communities, while many inhabitants remained Catholic. Philip II's policies, the authority of royal officials, taxation, religious prosecutions and local privileges together shaped growing opposition. The wave of iconoclasm in 1566, when religious images were destroyed in various locations, reflected both theological hostility to imagery and wider political tensions.

Conflict escalated into the Dutch Revolt, conventionally dated from 1568, and became a long struggle over government, warfare and religious authority. William of Orange and other leaders assembled coalitions that did not always share the same confessional priorities. The northern provinces moved toward independent republican institutions, while much of the south remained under Habsburg rule. The divisions that emerged helped shape the later histories of the Netherlands and Belgium, but those present-day categories should not be projected neatly backward onto every town and household.

The Dutch Republic developed comparatively wide possibilities for publishing and some minority communities, yet its public church and civic rules retained restrictions. Merchants, refugees and printers took advantage of a commercial network that crossed confessional lines. The outcome thus combined religious conflict, state formation, trade and selective coexistence.
