Western European Christianity around 1500 was dominated by the Latin Church in communion with the pope at Rome, but it was never a perfectly uniform society. Bishops, monasteries, cathedral chapters, parish priests, universities, confraternities and lay devotional movements pursued overlapping purposes. Some believers participated primarily through the local parish; others undertook pilgrimages, donated to hospitals, joined religious fraternities, or supported scholars and preachers. The Orthodox churches of eastern and southeastern Europe had their own theological and institutional trajectories following the long estrangement from Rome; they should not be folded into a story of Western Catholics simply turning Protestant.

The late medieval Church was also a major property holder and political actor. Bishops might govern territories; monasteries owned land; some princes influenced appointments to church offices; papal courts dispensed legal judgments. Supporters viewed many of these institutions as necessary to education, charity, worship and public order. Critics complained about absentee officeholders, overlapping benefices, financial demands, favoritism and the gap between Christian teaching and elite behavior. Local conditions varied enormously. A skilled parish priest could sustain religious life even while an expensive ecclesiastical court made nearby residents resentful.

It is tempting to explain reform by assuming that everybody had stopped trusting the Church. Evidence points instead to intense popular religion: donations for altars, processions, devotional images, and anxiety about sin and salvation. People could be deeply devoted to inherited practices and simultaneously call for moral correction. Historians therefore ask what a specific community wanted to reform and who would control the result.
