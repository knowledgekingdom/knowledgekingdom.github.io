The Reformation emerged from European Christian disputes, but its consequences traveled beyond Europe through trade, migration and empire. Catholic missions developed alongside Portuguese and Spanish overseas expansion, and Protestant networks later followed Dutch, English and other commercial routes. Religious ideas crossed oceans through clergy, sailors, enslaved and free people, merchants, diplomatic contacts and printed books. Local societies interpreted Christianity through their own political and intellectual histories; conversion cannot be reduced to Europeans broadcasting a fixed message to passive recipients.

Power was unequal. Colonial authorities sometimes used coercion and destroyed local sacred places; missionaries could promote schools and translation while participating in imperial institutions. Other missionaries criticized enslavement or abuses, and Indigenous communities shaped religious practices in ways that colonial reports imperfectly described. Jesuit accommodation strategies in parts of Asia differed from other missionary approaches. Religious debate also traveled back to Europe as stories about non-European societies complicated inherited assumptions about language, faith and governance.

This global angle prevents an artificial separation between chapters. Chapter 26 will discuss Indian Ocean commerce and Asian powers; Chapter 27 will follow European maritime expansion; Chapter 28 will examine exchanges of plants, pathogens and peoples. The religious history here is part of those transformations, but not their sole cause. Economic networks carried religious texts, while religious institutions altered political legitimacy and education.
