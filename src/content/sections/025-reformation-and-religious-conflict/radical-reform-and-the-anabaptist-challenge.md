Some reformers thought Luther and civic Protestant churches had not gone far enough. Groups later labeled Anabaptists rejected infant baptism, arguing that baptism should follow a conscious profession of faith. This placed them at odds with societies that linked baptism to parish membership, legal identity and public order. Many also sought more autonomous congregations, although their practices and beliefs varied by region. The label covered diverse movements rather than one centrally governed church.

Authorities saw these practices as dangerous because religious membership and civic belonging were intertwined. An unbaptized infant, a community declining oaths, or believers refusing military service could appear to challenge the foundations of government. Both Protestant and Catholic rulers persecuted Anabaptists. Some communities upheld nonviolence and disciplined separation from politics. Others embraced apocalyptic upheaval; the Anabaptist regime established at Münster in 1534–35 became a notorious exception that opponents used to condemn the wider movement. It is poor reasoning to treat the most sensational episode as representative of every believer who rejected infant baptism.

Menno Simons later helped organize nonviolent currents whose descendants include Mennonites. Their story illustrates a question often hidden by histories focused on rulers: when a community insists that faith should be voluntary, can a confessional state tolerate it? In much of sixteenth-century Europe, governments answered no. Yet minority communities persisted through migration, mutual support and changes in local policy, gradually broadening the repertoire of religious organization beyond established territorial churches. \[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]
