Reform became real when local worship changed. In some territories, Latin services were replaced or supplemented by the vernacular; preaching gained prominence; images were removed or retained according to local theology; monasteries closed or were repurposed; poor relief and schooling were reorganized. Marriage and clerical families became visible signs of difference. Catherine von Bora, a former nun who married Luther, is often presented as a symbol of these changes, but her household also reminds us that domestic labor, money management and hospitality sustained the public careers of famous reformers.

The experience of reform depended on one's place in the parish. Some people welcomed preaching in a familiar language and resented older financial dues. Others grieved the end of masses for deceased relatives or the destruction of images tied to community memory. Religious buildings accumulated donations, family obligations and political claims over generations; changing their use could unsettle property rights. A new catechism might bring opportunities for schooling while prescribing stricter expectations for children and servants.

Historians recover these responses from visitation reports, parish accounts, wills, legal disputes and occasionally personal writing. Such records have biases: officials often documented disorder more thoroughly than peaceful compliance. An inspection describing 'ignorant villagers' may reveal an administrator's ideals as much as the villagers' understanding. A Reformation imposed by rulers and one negotiated within a city could have very different local histories, even when both adopted the same confession. \[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]
