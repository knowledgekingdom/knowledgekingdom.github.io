Theological disagreements cannot be reduced to a slogan about 'faith versus good deeds.' Luther's account of justification emphasized that sinners are made right before God through divine grace received by faith, not by earning salvation through meritorious works. This did not imply that compassion and ethical conduct were worthless; Luther argued that good works should flow from faith rather than purchase divine favor. Catholic theologians stressed grace as well, while disputing Luther's account of justification, authority and sacramental life. The debate was about the structure of salvation, not a crude choice between being moral and being immoral.

Luther also emphasized the authority of scripture over competing human traditions, though his approach to interpretation operated within preaching communities and theological argument rather than an expectation that every individual would read without teachers. His position on the sacraments differed from late medieval Catholic practice, retaining baptism and the Lord's Supper as central and defending Christ's real presence in the Eucharist. This last point would later divide him from other Protestants. The shorthand sola scriptura, sola fide and sola gratia is useful only when we explain what its advocates meant and how interpretations varied.

The word Protestant itself emerged from political-religious protest in the imperial context rather than naming a single doctrine shared by every dissenter. Lutherans, Reformed Christians, Anabaptists and later Anglicans disagreed sharply with each other. When a historical map colors them all the same shade, it conceals their conflicts over baptism, worship, government and authority.
