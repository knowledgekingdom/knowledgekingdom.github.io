Early modern religious conflict did not concern Christians alone. Jewish communities experienced alternating expulsions, restrictions, invited settlement and local protections. Antisemitism remained deeply rooted in Christian theology and social practices, and Luther's later anti-Jewish writings were violently hostile; they must not be hidden behind his early calls for reform. Jewish life developed across different political spaces, including cities and regions of central and eastern Europe, while expulsions from Iberia had earlier contributed to new Sephardic communities around the Mediterranean and beyond.

Muslims appeared in Western European religious rhetoric in ways often shaped by fear of Ottoman military power. Yet actual encounters varied: diplomats negotiated, merchants traded, captives crossed borders, and scholars and rulers discussed Ottoman affairs as part of broader international politics. Muslim subjects of the Ottoman Empire, and Christian and Jewish populations under Ottoman authority, had experiences that cannot be reduced to European polemical caricatures. Confessional boundaries were as much political and social categories as theological ones.

The expansion of public preaching and printed debate could sharpen hostility to those labeled religious outsiders. Catholic, Protestant and Orthodox communities all held traditions of inclusion and exclusion; no group should be described as uniformly tolerant or uniformly persecuting. A responsible historical comparison asks what legal rights different people actually possessed, how policies changed, what protections could be purchased or negotiated, and whose voices survive in the archive. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum's historical overview is particularly valuable for locating early modern antisemitism within a longer development rather than treating it as an unexpected twentieth-century invention. \[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-26" class="cite">26</a>\]\[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]
