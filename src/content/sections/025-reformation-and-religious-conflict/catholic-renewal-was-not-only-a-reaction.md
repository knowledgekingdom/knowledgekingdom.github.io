The term Counter-Reformation emphasizes Catholic responses to Protestant expansion. Catholic Reformation is often preferable when discussing movements that began earlier or had goals beyond opposing Protestants. Catholic reformers supported stricter clerical education, better preaching, monastic renewal, more effective pastoral supervision and charitable foundations. Some criticized corruption while defending papal authority, the Mass and sacramental traditions. Internal reform and confessional conflict developed together, not as entirely separate histories.

The Catholic Church possessed resources that supported adaptation: universities, religious orders, diplomatic networks, patronage, printed publishing and religious art. But these resources were unevenly distributed, and reform agendas confronted existing privileges. Bishops who lacked residence in their dioceses could not easily supervise clergy; ambitious princes resisted interference; monasteries and cathedral chapters defended their assets. Reform required training, money, regulation and the cooperation of local societies, not simply a declaration that abuses should end.

A useful comparison is between two bishops who agreed with the same reform decree. One might establish a seminary, inspect parishes and recruit teachers; the other could face war, poverty and resistance that delayed implementation. A council's text is evidence of official ideals. Its historical effects must be traced through budgets, records of visits, clergy appointments and the daily practice of worship.
