Huldrych Zwingli began preaching reforms in Zurich before and during the years when Luther's movement expanded. In the Swiss cantons, politics worked differently from German principalities: municipal councils, confederate alliances, rural communities and mercenary service were central concerns. In Zurich, civic leaders supervised reforms to worship and religious institutions, including changes involving images, fasting and preaching. Debate about whether the Mass should continue in its inherited form became a question for urban government as well as for theology.

Zwingli and Luther agreed on much criticism of Rome but disagreed deeply about the Eucharist. At the Marburg Colloquy of 1529 they failed to reach complete agreement on Christ's presence in the Lord's Supper. This mattered because participants viewed the sacrament not as an optional symbol but as a central practice of Christian life. It also shaped the possibility of religious-political alliance. In 1531 Zwingli died during warfare among Swiss cantons at Kappel, illustrating how religious division and older confederate rivalries could become entangled.

Following Zwingli, figures including Heinrich Bullinger consolidated Reformed traditions. Swiss reform was not merely German Lutheranism applied in a neighboring country. Cities and cantons formed their own political compromises, confessions and refugee networks. The story reminds us that a shared opponent does not guarantee unity. People may identify the same institutional problem yet divide over what should replace it, who should decide, and what compromises conscience permits. \[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]\[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]
