The English Reformation followed a route unlike Luther's. Henry VIII's desire to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, amid concerns about succession and royal authority, brought him into conflict with the papacy. Parliament's legislation, especially the 1534 Act of Supremacy, recognized the king as supreme head of the Church of England. This rupture with Rome did not mean that Henry immediately adopted Lutheran theology. Many inherited teachings and practices remained, and different policies emerged under successive monarchs.

The dissolution of monasteries from the 1530s dramatically changed land ownership, poor relief, religious practice and the built environment. Some benefited from the redistribution of property; others lost institutions that had structured local worship and charity. Under Edward VI, reformers advanced more explicitly Protestant policies, including the English Book of Common Prayer. Mary I restored papal allegiance and persecuted Protestants. Elizabeth I established a new settlement after 1558, blending Protestant doctrine with institutional continuity, though neither dissenting Catholics nor Puritan critics were fully satisfied.

A succession of royal policies meant a single lifetime could contain several officially sanctioned religions. People navigated changes in prayer books, royal supremacy, parish decoration and the danger of identifying with disfavored worship. The English case demonstrates that state-building and doctrinal reform can proceed on different schedules. To call it 'the same Reformation' as Saxony would miss Parliament, dynastic marriage politics, changes of monarch and the distinctive institutions that shaped what ordinary believers experienced. \[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]\[<a href="/025-reformation-and-religious-conflict/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]
