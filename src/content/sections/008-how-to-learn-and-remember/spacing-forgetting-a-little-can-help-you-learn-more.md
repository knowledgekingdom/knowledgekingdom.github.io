You could study a topic for three consecutive hours on one evening, or distribute three hours across several days. For many forms of long-term retention, <strong>spaced practice</strong> generally performs better than the same amount of study massed into one short period. Returning after some forgetting forces you to reconstruct knowledge rather than simply recognize it.

There is no magic universal timetable. A short review the next day, another after several days, and another after a week or two is a practical starting schedule, not a scientifically mandated interval for every learner and topic. Difficult material needs more frequent attention; material you recall easily can be revisited less often. Review intervals should depend on your goal: knowledge needed next week is different from knowledge you wish to retain for years.

Imagine reading about the causes of World War I on Monday. On Tuesday, you spend two minutes naming three long-term conditions and the immediate trigger. On Friday, you explain why the assassination in Sarajevo was not by itself a complete causal explanation. The following week, you connect this to alliances and the security dilemma from Chapter 7. Each retrieval strengthens not just a date or definition but the links between ideas.

A small amount of review can change the value of an entire chapter. Without revisiting it, the text may become a pleasant memory of having read something. With retrieval and spacing, the same chapter may become a set of concepts you can actually use.
