Facts form networks. To understand the Industrial Revolution, you need more than a list of inventors. Geography affects access to coal and waterways; economic incentives influence investment; scientific and technical change alter production; political institutions and labor relationships shape who benefits; and population change transforms cities. Understanding grows as you can explain these connections.

One useful method is <strong>elaboration</strong>: ask why a fact is true or how it relates to something already known. Another is <strong>self-explanation</strong>: explain the reasoning behind a paragraph or worked problem in your own words. These methods require care. A confident explanation can be wrong. Check your reasoning against trustworthy sources and note where you are making an inference rather than reporting a documented fact.

An effective question is 'What would I expect to see if this explanation were true?' If mountain ranges influence rainfall, where should wetter and drier areas tend to appear under appropriate wind conditions? If a tax increases the price paid by consumers, how might buyers and sellers adapt? If a country's population ages, what might happen to its workforce, healthcare needs, and public budgets? These are starting hypotheses, not automatic predictions. They invite you to connect concepts with evidence.
