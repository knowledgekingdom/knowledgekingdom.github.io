<strong>Attention</strong> determines which information receives enough processing to become a candidate for memory. <strong>Encoding</strong> refers to the processes by which experiences and new information are represented in memory. <strong>Retrieval</strong> means bringing that information back when the original material is not fully in front of you. <strong>Transfer</strong> means applying knowledge to a different task or situation.

These are not four sealed compartments. They interact, and psychologists debate the details of learning and memory systems. But as a practical model they are helpful. If your phone interrupts every two minutes, sustained attention becomes harder. If you memorize a definition without understanding how it relates to an example, you may retrieve the words but fail to transfer the concept. If you understand a subject today but never retrieve it again, future recall may fade.

Consider the term <strong>sovereignty</strong>. First you notice it in a chapter. Then you understand its relationship to states and authority. Later, without looking, you explain that it concerns ultimate political authority rather than shared ethnic identity. Finally, when hearing about an autonomy dispute, you identify which arguments concern sovereignty and which concern identity. That last step is far more useful than repeating a single sentence from memory.
