A large stock can buffer a system against short disturbances. A full reservoir can supply water during a dry spell; a company with savings can survive a bad month; a household with emergency funds can absorb a repair bill. But stocks also make change slow. A country cannot instantly replace its buildings, cars, electricity grid or population age structure. Today's choices become tomorrow's constraints.

Think of a ship that continues moving after the engine stops. Its momentum is an analogy for accumulation, though the physical mechanism differs from social ones. If a town has been losing young residents for decades, one successful recruitment campaign may improve the annual migration flow without instantly reversing the age distribution. The existing population structure still influences schools, housing demand and healthcare needs. Likewise, a government can reduce its annual deficit while total public debt continues to rise if borrowing remains positive; and debt may fall as a fraction of national output even while its nominal amount grows. These are different measures of different stocks and flows.

A buffer has costs. Large inventories tie up money and can spoil; excessive reserves can mean unused capacity; a very large emergency fund can limit investment. The right question is not “Are buffers good?” but “How much resilience do they provide relative to their costs and the range of disturbances we expect?” This becomes particularly important for electricity grids, hospital staffing, food supply chains and military logistics.

Suppose two fictional businesses both sell 100 units daily. One holds a 10-day inventory buffer; the other holds a two-day buffer and receives rapid deliveries. The second may use less storage and cash in normal times. But a three-day supplier interruption could halt its sales while the first continues. You cannot say one model is always superior without knowing delivery reliability, storage cost, demand variability and how damaging a stockout would be.

<strong>The systems lesson:</strong> what looks inefficient in ordinary circumstances can be valuable insurance against rare shocks. The reverse also holds: a large buffer that serves no plausible need may waste resources. Resilience is contextual, not a decorative slogan.
