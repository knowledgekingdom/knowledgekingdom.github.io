A <strong>balancing loop</strong>, marked <strong>B</strong>, tends to counter a deviation or move a system toward a goal or constraint. A thermostat is a classic example. If a room is cooler than the desired temperature, the heating system works harder; as temperature rises toward the setting, the demand for additional heat falls. The feedback compares a measured condition with a target and adjusts an action. If the thermostat is broken or too slow, the loop may perform badly, but the logic remains balancing.

Inventory management has the same structure. When shelf stock falls below a target, an ordering rule increases purchases; new deliveries raise inventory; as stock recovers, the need for extra orders declines. In a healthy system, this feedback helps resist shortages. In a badly calibrated system, orders can overshoot because replenishment takes days and the manager has already placed several overlapping orders. The mechanism moves us directly toward the next major concept: <strong>delay</strong>.

Balancing does not always mean “healthy.” A social hierarchy may use repression, rewards and institutional rules to resist change; a harmful business practice may persist because attempts to improve it are neutralized by entrenched incentives. The term simply says that the loop opposes a deviation. To decide whether the outcome is desirable, we need values, evidence and attention to whose interests the target serves.

In almost every important real system, reinforcing and balancing processes operate together. A city's population may rise partly because jobs attract workers and workers attract firms, while housing costs, congestion and service constraints may eventually limit further growth. A disease can spread through contact-based amplification while immunity, behavior and intervention alter transmission. The observed path is the result of interacting forces, not a choice between one loop and the other.
