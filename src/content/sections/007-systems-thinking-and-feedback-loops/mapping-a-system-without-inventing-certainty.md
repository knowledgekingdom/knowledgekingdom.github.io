A causal diagram is a hypothesis about relationships. It is not proof. A neat arrow from “poverty” to “crime,” for example, can conceal different crimes, institutions, locations, time periods and confounding variables. Different mechanisms may operate in different settings. Some correlations are partly reversed by selection effects or measurement changes. The map should help you ask better questions rather than intimidate others with scientific-looking graphics.

Start with a tightly defined outcome: <em>Why does a shop repeatedly run out of popular products?</em> List measurable stocks and flows, candidate delays, decision rules and relevant actors. Draw only relationships you can explain in words. Label positive and negative effects as <strong>other-things-equal tendencies</strong> rather than universal laws. Add possible alternative causes. Identify what observations would make you reject a proposed link.

Then move from diagram to testing. Could delivery invoices show variable lead times? Could sales records reveal demand patterns? Do shortages happen only on promotional days? Is the supplier constrained, or is the reorder level too low? The better your measurements and comparisons, the less your map is merely a story. Sometimes a spreadsheet model or a simulation clarifies assumptions, but exact-looking outputs should not be confused with exact knowledge of the world.

Models should also be checked for <strong>sensitivity</strong>. If a small change in the assumed delivery delay reverses your policy recommendation, the decision is fragile. If three reasonable assumptions produce the same conclusion, confidence may be greater. Scenario analysis can compare what happens under different plausible conditions. Especially in politics and climate, uncertainty does not mean all stories are equally credible; it means the scope and strength of evidence should determine how decisive your conclusions are.
