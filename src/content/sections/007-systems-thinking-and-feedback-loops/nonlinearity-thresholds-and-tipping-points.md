Many everyday models assume that doubling an input roughly doubles the output. That is a <strong>linear</strong> approximation. But systems often behave <strong>nonlinearly</strong>: a small change can have little effect in one range and a dramatic effect in another. A road near free-flow conditions may absorb a handful of additional vehicles easily; close to its effective capacity, a modest increase can trigger queues that interact with junctions and sharply increase delay. The precise response depends on the network, not an iron law that every road has the same tipping point.

Consider a fictional hospital with 90 staffed beds and an average of 80 occupied. Ten extra patients might be absorbed with difficulty but without crisis. If occupancy remains near 90 and admissions fluctuate, the hospital may encounter queues, cancelled procedures and transfers more frequently. Even if average demand is below capacity, random peaks can cause congestion. It is an error to plan capacity solely by comparing two averages while ignoring variability and peak demand.

Some ecosystems have thresholds and <strong>hysteresis</strong>: after a system shifts from one state to another, simply reversing the original pressure may not restore the old state. For example, degraded land may need both reduced grazing and active restoration before vegetation returns. The precise conditions differ by ecosystem. We should not describe every change as an irreversible “tipping point,” nor conclude that smooth trends are guaranteed to continue.

A more subtle kind of path dependence appears in technology. People adopt software because other people use it; training, files and complementary products accumulate around the dominant choice. Even when a technically attractive alternative appears, switching costs and compatibility can preserve the established standard. This does not prove that the standard was originally inferior or that lock-in is inevitable. It explains why <strong>history can matter to present choices even when the immediate situation looks similar</strong>.
