Why do empires weaken when conquest stretches their supply networks? Why can a tax rule change behavior beyond the money it collects? Why can scientific advances create new risks as well as solutions? Why can a peace treaty end fighting without resolving the institutions that generate future conflict? These questions will return throughout the Knowledge Kingdom.

A system is not a destiny. It is a structured set of possibilities shaped by resources, institutions, incentives, information and human action. Some loops are powerful, some weak, some only temporary. Some feedback is visible in a graph; some requires decades of archival or scientific work to detect. Systems thinking cannot tell you what is morally right, but it can reveal the consequences your preferred policy might have overlooked.

Return once more to the grocery shop. The most important improvement may be a new delivery schedule, an employee's ability to report stockouts, a redesign of the storeroom, or a change in the owner's goal from maximum sales to reliable profit and customer trust. You cannot identify the answer merely by describing the shop as a “system.” You identify it by <strong>understanding the system well enough to make a prediction and checking whether that prediction survives contact with reality</strong>.

That is the central habit of this chapter: when an outcome keeps surprising you, investigate the rules of the game, the accumulated stocks, the information people receive and the feedback created by their decisions. The world is not a simple line of causes. It is a moving conversation among causes, and we are participants in it.
