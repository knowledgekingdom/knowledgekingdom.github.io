NATO was founded in 1949. The North Atlantic Treaty includes consultation in Article 4 and collective defence in Article 5. The latter requires assistance in response to a covered armed attack, but each party determines the action it considers necessary, which may include armed force. It is not a clause requiring identical military action from every member. Article 6 helps define the geographical scope. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]

The Warsaw Pact, established in 1955, brought the Soviet Union and Eastern European states into a formal security arrangement. Soviet political domination over much of Eastern Europe and the interventions in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968 show why formal membership and meaningful autonomy need to be analysed separately. The pact dissolved in 1991. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-39" class="cite">39</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-40" class="cite">40</a>\] A comparison of the two organisations should consider their texts, decision practices and the actual freedom of members, rather than assuming symmetry because both were called alliances.

The end of the Cold War transformed the circumstances in which NATO operated, but did not automatically remove every reason its members saw for maintaining it. New members sought accession for their own reasons; critics and supporters disputed its changing roles and implications. A balanced historical analysis must separate documented policy decisions, security perceptions, legal claims and contested causal interpretations. Membership itself does not settle every question about security or foreign policy.
