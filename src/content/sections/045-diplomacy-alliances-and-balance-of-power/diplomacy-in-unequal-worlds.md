Diplomatic encounters have rarely involved perfectly equal parties. Imperial courts might expect gifts, ritual deference or recognition of their superior rank. Other rulers might participate for trading opportunities or pragmatic reasons while interpreting the ritual differently. In the Chinese imperial world, for example, relations described through tribute could combine ceremonial hierarchy with commerce and political bargaining. It would be misleading to treat every neighbouring polity as directly governed by China simply because its envoys participated in such exchanges. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-35" class="cite">35</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-36" class="cite">36</a>\]

Medieval and early modern rulers also used merchants, religious institutions, marriage networks and temporary missions. An envoy might depend on linguistic intermediaries and local knowledge; a misunderstanding about titles could convey a political insult even where the speaker intended none. The Ottoman Empire negotiated with European and Asian powers long before adopting permanent embassies in European capitals in the late eighteenth century. A permanent mission represented a change in diplomatic infrastructure, not the discovery of diplomacy itself. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-43" class="cite">43</a>\]

Modern international law formally recognises the sovereign equality of states, but legal equality does not erase differences in wealth, coercive power or bargaining alternatives. A poor island state and a continental great power may each possess one vote in a particular institution while having vastly different resources to influence negotiations. Diplomacy is the practice of acting within that tension, not a guarantee of equality.
