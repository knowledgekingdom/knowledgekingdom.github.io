An embassy is not simply a building with a flag. It represents the sending state, maintains contact with the receiving government, reports developments, supports nationals within legal limits and fosters relationships. Under the 1961 Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, permanent missions are established by consent. The receiving state is not obliged to accept every proposed diplomatic representative; it can declare a diplomat persona non grata. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]

Diplomatic immunity is a functional legal protection, not an assertion that diplomats are above all law. Accredited diplomatic agents generally enjoy immunity from the receiving state's criminal jurisdiction and qualified immunity from its civil and administrative jurisdiction. The sending state can waive immunity; the diplomat remains subject to its jurisdiction. The duty to respect local law continues. The premises of a mission are inviolable, but they do not thereby become sovereign territory of the sending state. This last distinction corrects a surprisingly common popular misconception.

Consulates concentrate on services such as documentation and assistance to nationals, although diplomatic missions may perform consular functions too. The 1963 Vienna Convention on Consular Relations provides a separate legal framework. If a traveller loses a passport, a consular official is likely to be more relevant than the ambassador. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-37" class="cite">37</a>\] Ordinary diplomacy is often administrative, and its success is measured by problems that never become headlines.
