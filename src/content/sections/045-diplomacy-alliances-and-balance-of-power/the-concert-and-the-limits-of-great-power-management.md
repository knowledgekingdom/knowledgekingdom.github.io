Concert diplomacy rested partly on the idea that the strongest governments had a shared interest in preventing a renewed general conflict. But they did not share identical views of revolutions, national independence or intervention. Britain resisted certain conservative interventionist policies; the Eastern Question generated competing interests in the future of Ottoman territories. The Crimean War in the 1850s demonstrated that great-power cooperation could break down. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]

It is tempting to claim that the Congress of Vienna produced a century of peace. That formulation ignores European wars after 1815 and the violence of imperial expansion outside Europe. A narrower claim is that there was no Europe-wide war on the Napoleonic or First World War scale between 1815 and 1914. Even that observation does not establish that the Concert was the sole cause; economic changes, military institutions and particular political decisions also shaped outcomes.

The Concert offers a useful comparison with modern institutions. Consultation can make disagreements visible before they escalate, but a great-power club can sacrifice the interests of people who lack representation. A diplomatic settlement should be assessed in terms of security, participation and distribution of costs, not simply the absence of war among its most powerful members.
