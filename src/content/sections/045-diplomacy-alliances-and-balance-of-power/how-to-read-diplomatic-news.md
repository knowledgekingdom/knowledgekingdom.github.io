When a headline states that two countries have signed a historic agreement, first identify the document. Is it a treaty, political declaration, memorandum or expression of intent? Who signed it and with what authority? Has it entered into force? What language specifies the obligations, exceptions and geographical scope? Does the text differ from what the speakers claim in public? These questions prevent confusion between political symbolism and legal effect.

Next examine context. Compare the agreement with previous commitments and actual behaviour. Look for sources from more than one party, including affected communities and independent institutions. Government records are valuable evidence of official positions but can reflect political interests. Scholarly arguments should be checked against the evidence they use; a secondary summary is not the same as direct examination of an archive.

Finally separate four judgments: what happened, why it happened, whether it will endure and whether it is desirable. The answers can differ. An agreement may be effective but unfair; a lawful undertaking may be difficult to implement; a failed negotiation may still have reduced uncertainty. Good geopolitical reasoning resists the urge to turn every event into proof of a preferred theory. Confidence should rise with evidence, not with the forcefulness of the headline.
