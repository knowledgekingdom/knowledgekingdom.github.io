In April 1955, representatives of twenty-nine Asian and African governments met at Bandung, Indonesia. Many had recently emerged from colonial rule. Their concerns included political independence, economic development, racial discrimination and peaceful coexistence. The gathering was an important antecedent of the Non-Aligned Movement, whose first summit took place in Belgrade in 1961. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-22" class="cite">22</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-42" class="cite">42</a>\]

Nonalignment did not mean identical policies or the absence of relationships with major powers. Member governments differed over domestic systems, economic interests and conflicts. Some received assistance from rival blocs. What united the project was an aspiration to pursue foreign policy without accepting that the world must be organised exclusively around the preferences of the two Cold War superpowers.

Bandung changes the perspective from which we tell twentieth-century history. A conventional map of two rival camps may leave recently independent states shaded according to their presumed allegiance. Their own documents reveal other priorities: sovereignty, the end of empire, development and international equality. Neither the movement's diversity nor its internal disagreements erase its significance. It illustrates how diplomatic institutions can also contest the definition of the international order itself.
