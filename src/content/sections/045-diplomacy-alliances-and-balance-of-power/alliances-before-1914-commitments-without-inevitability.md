By the early twentieth century European politics included overlapping agreements and increasingly rival groupings. Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy formed the Triple Alliance in 1882. France and Russia developed an alliance in the 1890s; Britain reached understandings with France in 1904 and Russia in 1907. The Triple Entente was not a single equivalent mutual-defence treaty. These distinctions matter when explaining the decisions made in 1914.

The Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902 illustrates that European alliances were embedded in wider competition involving East Asia. Its initial provisions distinguished a war with one power from circumstances involving additional powers. Its implications were linked to the interests of both governments in the region. Alliances were not simply expressions of common nationality or ideology. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

When the First World War began, Italy did not enter alongside Germany and Austria-Hungary, and later joined the opposing side in 1915. That alone disproves the notion that treaty labels mechanically determined every government's choice. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-44" class="cite">44</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-45" class="cite">45</a>\] Alliances affected expectations and decisions, but leaders still interpreted obligations, pursued political objectives and made choices in a rapidly changing crisis. Refer back to Chapter 38 for the broader causes; no single diagram of alliance blocs can replace historical explanation.
