In 1814 and 1815, European powers negotiated a settlement after the Napoleonic wars. Austria, Britain, Prussia and Russia had been major opponents of Napoleon; France, represented by Talleyrand, also participated in the negotiations. The settlement combined territorial adjustments, restoration of dynasties and efforts to prevent renewed general war. France's subsequent inclusion among the major powers at Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818 illustrates the difference between permanently excluding a defeated state and incorporating it into a new political order. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]

The arrangement is often understood through the balance of power, but consultation and rules of behaviour also mattered. Governments were not simply adding up military resources. They were trying to establish expectations about when they would meet and how disputes would be addressed. The resulting Concert of Europe was an evolving practice, not a single organisation with a modern permanent secretariat.

The settlement was neither a liberal democracy conference nor a universal peace. Its decisions often privileged monarchs and great powers over national movements and smaller communities. Chapters 36 and 37 showed the consequences of those exclusions. A system may succeed at limiting certain kinds of great-power confrontation while failing to deliver political rights or justice. The criteria for judging success must therefore be explicit.
