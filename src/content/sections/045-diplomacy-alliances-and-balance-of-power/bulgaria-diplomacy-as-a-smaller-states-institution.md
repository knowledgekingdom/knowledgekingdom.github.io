Bulgaria's modern diplomatic history includes the search for recognition, alliances and security amid the Eastern Question, the Balkan wars, the world wars and Cold War institutions. Those historical choices were conditioned by the country's geography and by international decisions over which Bulgarian governments had limited control. They also involved domestic disagreements and agency; geography did not dictate a single inevitable policy. Recall Chapters 37, 38 and 41.

Following the political changes of 1989–1990, Bulgaria pursued accession to Euro-Atlantic institutions. It joined NATO on 29 March 2004 and the European Union on 1 January 2007. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-41" class="cite">41</a>\] These memberships involve different obligations and opportunities. The EU's Article 42(7) contains a mutual assistance provision in the event of armed aggression on a member state's territory, with qualifications concerning national security policies and NATO commitments. EU membership also entails extensive legal and economic integration far beyond defence. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-32" class="cite">32</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-33" class="cite">33</a>\]

For a smaller country, diplomacy includes participation in common decision-making, negotiation with neighbours, protection of nationals and representation of economic interests. A representative in Brussels or at the United Nations may be working on regulations, transport, education or environmental agreements rather than a dramatic security crisis. Effective diplomacy depends on expertise and continuity as much as high-profile speeches. The evaluation of any particular policy remains a matter of evidence and democratic debate.
