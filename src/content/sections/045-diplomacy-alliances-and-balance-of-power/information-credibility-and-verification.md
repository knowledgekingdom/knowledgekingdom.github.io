A promise is easier to utter than to fulfil. Governments may disagree about what happened, doubt an opponent's intentions or lack reliable knowledge of its future choices. Some uncertainty is unavoidable: a leadership change can alter policy even if today's promise is sincere. Other uncertainty arises from inaccurate reporting, ambiguous language or strategic misrepresentation. A sound agreement cannot simply assume that all these problems disappear when leaders shake hands.

Verification seeks evidence about compliance. In a shared environmental agreement, for instance, the parties may agree on common measurement definitions, independent monitoring and a process for reviewing contradictory reports. A single suspicious reading need not prove a deliberate violation; an absence of public evidence need not prove compliance. The distinction between verification, adjudication and enforcement is important. Verification investigates conduct; adjudication determines the meaning or application of rules; enforcement concerns responses to violations.

Credibility is not the same as moral trustworthiness. A government may be capable of performing a particular obligation but unwilling to do so, or sincerely willing but institutionally unable to deliver. Sustainable commitments depend on legal authority, administrative resources and incentives as well as words. International institutions can reduce uncertainty, though their value depends on access, independence and cooperation.
