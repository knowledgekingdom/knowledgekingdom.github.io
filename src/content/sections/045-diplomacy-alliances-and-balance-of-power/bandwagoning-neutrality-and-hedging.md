Bandwagoning commonly refers to alignment with a stronger or threatening power rather than against it. A government may believe resistance is too costly, seek protection or expect benefits from association. Neutrality, by contrast, concerns nonparticipation in conflict under relevant legal conditions; permanent neutrality adds a longer-term policy and legal dimension. Nonalignment is a broader political stance of avoiding exclusive bloc alignment and is not identical to legal neutrality. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]

Hedging describes efforts to preserve options under uncertainty, for example through varied economic and diplomatic relationships without committing fully to one side. It is a contested analytical term. Diversifying relationships does not automatically make a government a hedger, and a country may combine cooperation in one policy area with explicit alignment in another. The best evidence is a pattern of decisions rather than one declaration.

Small states are not simply passive objects of great-power competition. Their governments can use legal institutions, regional cooperation, specialised expertise and diplomatic reputations to widen options. Nonetheless, the risks of pressure and dependency are real. A choice described as pragmatic by one government may be understood as abandonment by another. These descriptions illuminate differing interests; they do not settle the legal or ethical merits of the decision.
