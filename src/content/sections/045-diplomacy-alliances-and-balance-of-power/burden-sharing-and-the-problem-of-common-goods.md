An alliance can provide a common benefit that is difficult to divide neatly among members. Each government may hope to benefit from the others' contributions while preserving money for its own domestic needs. This is a form of collective-action problem. However, the contributions are not always comparable: money, institutional expertise, geography, civilian capacity and participation in shared programmes have different kinds of value.

The simplest accounting measure may not be the best measure of fairness or effectiveness. Suppose two partners contribute equal currency amounts while one has an economy ten times larger. The burden relative to national income differs. If the smaller state contributes a specialised service that is essential to cooperation, the value of its contribution may exceed its budget cost. Conversely, expenditure can be inefficient or poorly matched to agreed priorities. Chapter 44's distinction between resources and capability applies here.

Burden-sharing debates are therefore simultaneously empirical and normative. What is being measured? Who agreed to the target? Does it measure an input, an output or a distribution of sacrifice? How should differences in resources and vulnerability be weighed? Citizens can reasonably disagree about fair obligations without disagreeing about the underlying arithmetic.
