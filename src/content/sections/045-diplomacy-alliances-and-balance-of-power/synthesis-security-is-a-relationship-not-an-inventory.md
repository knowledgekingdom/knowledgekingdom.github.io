The chapters preceding this one established that geography shapes opportunities and that military capacity depends on institutions. Diplomacy adds another layer: the meaning of a resource or commitment depends on how other actors interpret it and respond. An alliance may provide reassurance to its members while causing concern elsewhere; a treaty may constrain behaviour even where the parties remain rivals. Neither consequence can be deduced from a list of national capabilities alone.

The balance of power offers a powerful way of thinking about relationships among political communities, but it is incomplete without attention to perceived threats, domestic authority, law, economic dependency and the agency of smaller actors. Collective security and diplomacy introduce possibilities beyond unilateral competition, though they cannot eliminate disagreement or guarantee enforcement. Historical cases reveal patterns without furnishing universal formulas.

The mature question is not whether diplomacy is a sign of weakness or strength. It is what a particular diplomatic relationship enables, what obligations it creates, what rights it protects, what risks it shifts to others and what evidence would demonstrate that it works. The next chapter examines the international legal framework of armed conflict, where these questions acquire especially consequential meaning. The purpose of understanding diplomacy is not merely to predict the behaviour of powerful states but to evaluate the institutions through which people try to live together under conditions of disagreement.
