In October 1962, a confrontation between the United States and the Soviet Union over Soviet missiles in Cuba brought the powers into a dangerous crisis. It ended through exchanges that included Soviet agreement to remove the missiles, US assurances concerning an invasion of Cuba, and a private understanding concerning US missiles in Turkey. Contemporary public knowledge did not reveal the full bargaining relationship. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]

The case is often narrated as a test of resolve between two leaders. Such a narrative overlooks Cuba's own security concerns, the role of alliance partners and the limits of a superpower-centred archive. It also risks interpreting survival as proof that all decisions were wise. A crisis may end peacefully despite serious uncertainty; the absence of catastrophe does not establish that the same approach would be safe elsewhere.

Diplomatic communication provided a way to identify an outcome acceptable to the principal decision-makers, but it did not resolve every conflict among the states involved. The case helps explain why records released long after an event can change historians' understanding of the settlement. It also illustrates the tension between secrecy, domestic political acceptability and the participation of affected parties. Nuclear deterrence receives dedicated treatment in Chapter 47.
