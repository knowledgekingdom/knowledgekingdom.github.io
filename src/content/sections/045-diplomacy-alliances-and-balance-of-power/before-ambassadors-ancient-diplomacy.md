Diplomacy is older than modern sovereignty. The Amarna letters, a collection of cuneiform correspondence from the fourteenth century BCE, reveal Egyptian rulers exchanging messages with other Near Eastern courts. The status of a sender, requests for gifts, marriages and the language used to address another ruler could all carry political significance. Courts did not simply communicate information; they negotiated recognition and hierarchy. Some participants spoke as peers while others addressed Egypt as dependants. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

The Egyptian-Hittite peace treaty associated with Ramesses II and Hattusili III, in the thirteenth century BCE, illustrates that powerful rulers could formalise a relationship after conflict. Its surviving versions embody reciprocal commitments, although the surrounding political world was very different from today's international system. The existence of a written treaty does not mean all communities were represented in it, or that its rules resembled modern human rights law. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

In South Asia, the Arthashastra, traditionally associated with Kautilya, treats relations among rulers as part of the wider practice of government. Its mandala or circle-of-states framework considers how a ruler's relationships change with position and relative strength. The date and composition of the surviving text are debated, and it should not be read as a timeless description of all Indian foreign policy. Its significance is that systematic thinking about political relationships was not confined to Europe. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]
