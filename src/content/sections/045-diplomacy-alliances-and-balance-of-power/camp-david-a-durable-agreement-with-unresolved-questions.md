In September 1978 Egyptian President Anwar Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin negotiated with US President Jimmy Carter at Camp David. The accords provided a framework for the Egypt-Israel peace treaty signed in March 1979. The peace treaty addressed bilateral issues including the end of the state of war, withdrawal from Sinai and normal relations. A separate framework addressed wider questions of Middle Eastern peace but did not produce a comprehensive resolution of the Palestinian question. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-27" class="cite">27</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

This distinction is essential. The durability of the bilateral peace is not proof that the wider regional conflict was solved, just as the persistence of regional conflict does not erase the significance of the bilateral settlement. Diplomatic outcomes should be evaluated by scope and over time. One agreement may accomplish an important limited objective while leaving other parties dissatisfied or excluded.

The case also highlights the role of domestic legitimacy and leadership choices. Government representatives had to deal with their own constituencies and political risks, not simply negotiate an abstract division of territory. A mediator with considerable influence can facilitate an agreement, but the parties and affected populations live with its consequences. Chapter 50 returns to the question of what makes a settlement durable and just.
