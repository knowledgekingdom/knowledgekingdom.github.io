The League of Nations, established after the First World War, attempted to create a wider framework of international cooperation and collective security. Its Covenant envisioned processes for settling disputes and responding to certain violations. The United States never became a member, despite President Woodrow Wilson's role in promoting the organisation. This illustrates the domestic ratification problem on an international scale.

The League was not simply ineffective. It assisted with the Åland Islands dispute between Finland and Sweden in 1921 and the Greek-Bulgarian border crisis in 1925. Its wider work included health, refugees and technical cooperation. These achievements are often lost when its history is told solely as a prelude to another world war. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]

However, the League could not secure effective responses to Japan's seizure of Manchuria and Italy's invasion of Ethiopia in the 1930s. Political disagreement, uneven commitments and the unwillingness of powerful governments to incur costs undermined collective action. Institutions cannot substitute for every form of material and political support. The League's experience is a warning against both institutional idealism and the opposite view that institutions never matter.
