An alliance is a commitment among political actors to cooperate on security, commonly in relation to possible threats. In international politics the term usually refers to states. Some alliances are formal treaties with defined obligations; others are looser alignments. A partnership can involve sustained cooperation without a mutual-defence guarantee. A coalition may be established for a particular purpose and dissolve when that purpose ends. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]

A formal alliance does not mean that members share all policies or that they are permanently united. They may disagree about priorities, domestic values or relations with third parties. Conversely, states that lack a formal alliance may cooperate closely on a specific issue. The important analytical question is what they have committed to do and under which conditions. A joint military exercise or common photograph does not by itself establish a treaty obligation.

The benefits of alliances can include reassurance, shared institutional costs, political influence and access to capabilities otherwise unavailable. The costs can include dependence, difficult negotiations over burdens and restrictions on policy autonomy. A smaller member may gain a stronger voice through common institutions while losing freedom to make certain unilateral choices. Whether that trade-off is worthwhile depends on its alternatives, its goals and the credibility of its partners.
