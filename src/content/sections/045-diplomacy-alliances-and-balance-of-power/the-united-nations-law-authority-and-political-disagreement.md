The UN Charter of 1945 established a framework in which sovereign equality, peaceful settlement and restraints on force coexist with special powers for the Security Council. The five permanent members—China, France, Russia as successor to the Soviet Union's seat, the United Kingdom and the United States—hold a distinctive position in substantive decisions. The Council has fifteen members; substantive decisions require at least nine affirmative votes and, in practice, no negative vote by a permanent member. An abstention by a permanent member is not treated as a veto. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-6" class="cite">6</a>\]\[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-38" class="cite">38</a>\]

The General Assembly is a different institution. Its resolutions are generally recommendations in relation to external conduct, although it has important institutional powers including budgetary decisions. The Secretary-General can facilitate diplomacy and bring certain matters to the Council's attention. The International Court of Justice addresses legal disputes between states under its jurisdiction and issues advisory opinions when properly requested.

The UN is neither a world government nor merely a discussion club. Its organs have different mandates, powers and limitations. A veto can prevent Council action even when many governments favour it; other diplomatic, humanitarian and legal processes may still continue. Distinguish the organisation's legal authority from members' willingness to act. Chapter 61 will examine its institutional structure in more depth.
