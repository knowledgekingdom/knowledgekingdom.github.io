The Helsinki Final Act of 1975 was adopted by thirty-five participating states in the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe. Its principles addressed sovereignty, borders, non-use of force, peaceful settlement, human rights and other aspects of cooperation. It was a political commitment rather than a conventional binding treaty. It did not end the Cold War or eliminate differences between Eastern and Western governments. \[<a href="/045-diplomacy-alliances-and-balance-of-power/sources/#source-29" class="cite">29</a>\]

The process nevertheless created an agreed vocabulary through which conduct could be discussed. Human-rights commitments became reference points for citizens and monitoring groups as well as for diplomats. The significance of a document can extend beyond what its authors initially expect; a principle recorded in a multilateral text can be invoked in later public and institutional debates.

Helsinki also illustrates that security is not limited to military capacity. Economic cooperation, communication and the treatment of people can become part of a common political framework. Later institutional developments produced the OSCE. Its experiences demonstrate both the utility and fragility of cooperative processes in periods of serious political disagreement. A politically binding document should be neither dismissed as meaningless nor mistaken for an automatically enforceable guarantee.
