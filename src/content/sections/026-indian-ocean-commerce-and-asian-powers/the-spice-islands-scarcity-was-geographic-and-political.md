Cloves historically grew in a restricted area associated with the northern Moluccas, especially Ternate and Tidore, while nutmeg and mace were closely associated with the Banda Islands. Cinnamon was linked particularly with Sri Lanka, and pepper was cultivated in several regions, including parts of South and Southeast Asia. Grouping them together as 'spices' hides very different agricultural conditions, ecological ranges and routes to market. Their value reflected not only European appetite but Asian consumption, medicinal traditions, elite gift exchange and demand elsewhere.

Because some products originated in narrow zones, political relationships near their sources could shape prices far away. A ruler with influence over a harbor serving a clove-producing island had leverage, but growers, rival ports, intermediaries and outside buyers could challenge that control. European powers would later attempt to intensify monopoly arrangements through military occupation and compulsory deliveries. Such coercion was not inevitable just because a commodity was geographically concentrated; it resulted from particular political choices.

A price observed in Venice or Lisbon should not be mistaken for a farmer's return. Between producer and consumer stood carriers, customs collectors, lenders, warehouses, risk of spoilage and numerous merchants taking margins. Historians also debate what European observers actually knew about origins and volumes at a given date.
