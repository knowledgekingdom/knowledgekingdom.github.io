The dates below are anchors, not a list of events that occurred everywhere at the same time. By the early fifteenth century, Asian and African maritime networks were centuries old. Around 1400 Melaka began its rise as an important Malay trading center. From 1405 to 1433 Zheng He's voyages placed Ming imperial fleets in an existing Indian Ocean world. In 1498 Vasco da Gama arrived on the Malabar coast via the Cape route, opening a new and consequential European connection.

The Portuguese naval victory at Diu followed in 1509; Goa came under Portuguese control in 1510; Melaka was captured in 1511; a durable Portuguese position at Hormuz followed in 1515. The Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517 altered the politics of western routes. Mughal rule began in northern India in 1526 and expanded over subsequent decades; Gujarat came under Akbar's authority in the 1570s. Ottoman-Portuguese clashes and the shifting power of Aceh and Johor show that no single victory decided the ocean's political future.

New chartered European companies arrived in the early seventeenth century: the English company in 1600 and the VOC in 1602. Batavia became a Dutch center in 1619, and brutal Dutch coercion in Banda followed in 1621. This chronology is best understood as overlapping processes—growth of merchant networks, changing regional states, attempts at naval control and sustained local production—rather than one story of replacement by outsiders. \[<a href="/026-indian-ocean-commerce-and-asian-powers/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/026-indian-ocean-commerce-and-asian-powers/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/026-indian-ocean-commerce-and-asian-powers/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]
