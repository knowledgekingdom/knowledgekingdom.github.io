Indian Ocean vessels were technically diverse. Large Chinese junks, ships of the western Indian Ocean often grouped under the imprecise modern term dhow, and numerous Southeast Asian craft had different hull construction, rigging, cargo capacities and operating environments. There was no single 'Eastern ship' that confronted one superior 'European ship.' Boatbuilding traditions answered different questions about available timber, wind, coastal waters, maintenance and the kind of cargo being carried.

Navigation relied on combinations of observing stars, reading winds and currents, measuring depth near coasts, remembering seasonal routes and learning from pilots. Nautical knowledge was often practical and collaborative. A captain might rely on local specialists to enter a dangerous harbor or pass through a strait. Written sailing instructions existed, but not every sailor needed to read a book to possess sophisticated geographic knowledge. Later Portuguese documents sometimes preserved routes learned from regional navigators rather than invented by Europeans.

Naval technology also changed through contact and competition. Cannon could matter greatly in a harbor or a siege, but weapons were only one part of successful maritime power. Fleets required timber, metal, sailors' wages, food, fortification maintenance and access to friendly shores. Powerful ships could bombard a coastal position and still struggle to control hundreds of alternative routes.
