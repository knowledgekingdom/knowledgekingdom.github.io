The eastern side of the Indian subcontinent supplied another network of ports, crafts and political relationships. Along the Coromandel coast, textile producers created cloth suited to markets in Southeast Asia. Ports around the Bay of Bengal linked southern India, Sri Lanka, Bengal, Myanmar and the Malay world. Commerce could move around the edges of the bay in shorter legs rather than forcing every vessel to complete a great transoceanic crossing. This coastal architecture rewarded pilots, merchants and rulers who understood local waters.

Sri Lanka occupied an important position near routes around southern India. It was also associated with valuable commodities, including cinnamon, precious stones and elephants, whose exchange depended on the island's varied political authorities and inland communities. We should not picture the entire island as one commercial warehouse under a single ruler. Local kingdoms, religious institutions and coastal settlements competed over access, and European campaigns later altered these relationships in uneven ways.

Bengal's river systems connected productive inland zones to maritime exchange. Rivers provided transport but also shifted courses, flooded and complicated military control. Bengal's importance became especially pronounced in later textile circuits, yet its earlier connections show how sea trade rested on densely populated land economies. The Bay of Bengal was also a corridor for the movement of languages, artistic styles, Buddhist and Islamic traditions, and political ideas.
