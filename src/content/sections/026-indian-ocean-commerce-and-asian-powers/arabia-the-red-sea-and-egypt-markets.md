Ports near Aden and the Red Sea connected maritime cargo to Arabia, Egypt and ultimately Mediterranean consumers. The Mamluk sultanate, ruling Egypt and Syria until the Ottoman conquest in 1516–17, benefited from commercial revenues and relations with merchants engaged in the spice trade. Goods passing through the Red Sea might travel onward by land or river, with different rulers, tax collectors and carriers taking a share. There was no single, effortless passage from an Asian spice island to a European dinner table.

Pilgrimage added another dimension. The sea routes serving the Arabian Peninsula connected Muslim communities across a wide geography to the holy cities of Mecca and Medina. Pilgrims and merchants sometimes used overlapping routes; ships might carry people as well as cargo. Scholarly travel, religious exchange and commercial movement therefore reinforced one another without being identical. The significance of a Red Sea port cannot be reduced to the price of pepper alone.

Control of these gateways attracted competition. Portuguese attempts to redirect or tax commerce threatened commercial interests connected to Egypt, Arabia and the Indian west coast. The political authorities responding to those challenges were not uniformly allied; local interests and rivalries mattered.
