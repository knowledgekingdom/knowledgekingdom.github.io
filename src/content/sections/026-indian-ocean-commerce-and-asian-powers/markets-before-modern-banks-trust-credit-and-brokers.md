Long-distance merchants rarely carried enough coins to pay for every exchange in cash. They used credit, advance payments, partnerships, agents and written accounts suited to different legal and social environments. Some commercial networks extended across generations. A merchant might dispatch goods with a trusted representative, settle debts at a later port, or arrange for another household to distribute the proceeds. The details varied considerably and should not be collapsed into one invented universal banking system.

Trust was partly personal, but it also involved incentives and enforcement. Merchants who repeatedly deceived partners could lose access to profitable relationships. Local rulers offered courts and protection, often in return for customs duties. Religious institutions, family alliances and community leaders could help broker disputes. Yet outsiders were not always equal before the law; coercion and discrimination could make commercial protection conditional on wealth, status or political connections.

A hypothetical calculation makes the logic clearer. Suppose a trader sends twenty bales of cloth, each expected to sell for 12 units. The possible gross sale is 240 units, but the owner still must pay for buying the cloth, freight, storage, customs, credit costs and losses. A headline about a 'profitable cargo worth 240' cannot establish net profit. Historical records often preserve transactions selectively, so historians resist treating one merchant's successful voyage as evidence that everybody became wealthy.
