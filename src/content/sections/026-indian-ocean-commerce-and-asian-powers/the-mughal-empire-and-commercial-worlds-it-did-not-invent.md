The Mughal Empire emerged in northern India after Babur's victory in 1526 and expanded under later rulers. Its major story of conquest, land revenue, court culture and religious politics belongs more fully in Chapter 30. Here we focus on a narrower distinction. A powerful empire could tax or influence ports and hinterland routes without originating the manufacturing skills and merchant connections already operating there. Gujarat's incorporation under Akbar in the 1570s is a useful example of a new imperial framework enveloping a long-established trading region.

Large inland markets were as important to coastal towns as overseas buyers. Merchants had reasons to travel among agrarian districts, political capitals and ports. Textile production depended on specialized labor and access to dyeing materials and food supplies. Money, credit, revenue demands and the security of roads influenced the movement of goods. A European merchant reaching the shore therefore encountered an economy whose logic could not be understood by inspecting ships alone.

It is misleading to label the Mughal state either wholly anti-maritime or a centralized navy dominating the coast. Its priorities and capacities differed by region and time, and merchants could pursue commerce through legal, semi-official or private relationships.
