Much of the written history of ocean trade features wealthy captains, rulers and traveling merchants because they left letters, treaties and accounts. But cargo began in farms, workshops, forests, mines and households. Women spun and processed fibers, managed household businesses and participated in exchange in ways that were not always formally recorded. Laborers loaded ships; fishermen and carpenters maintained ports; servants supplied elite households; enslaved people performed domestic and other work. Different societies allocated work and authority differently, making sweeping generalizations unsafe.

A silk or cotton cloth carries the work of people who might never have met the eventual buyer. Artisans could specialize in patterns ordered by distant customers, yet not necessarily receive a large part of the final retail value. Coastal trade could create opportunities for craftspeople while exposing others to changing demand or heavy taxes. A city rising on an export boom might still have poor residents who struggled with food prices or rents.

The sources themselves create an ethical problem of visibility. Customs registers may list taxable cargo without naming its makers. A traveler's account may describe a ruler's magnificent palace but overlook those who supplied its materials. Archaeology, textile analysis, legal cases and household documents can partially recover these histories.
