A thriving port needed more than deep water. It needed warehouses, markets, skilled labor, political agreements, roads to nearby production zones and credible systems for settling disputes. Some locations possessed useful harbors but failed to become leading entrepôts because security, taxation or rival ports made trade costly. Other cities overcame geographical disadvantages through institutions and merchant networks. Urban prosperity was a process, not a permanent reward bestowed by the map.

A port also consumed resources. It needed food, drinking water, building materials and labor to keep docks and ships operating. Sudden arrivals could raise food prices or generate conflict over storage and duties. Ships could bring epidemic disease, while port populations were exposed to storms and changing coastlines. Successful rulers therefore had incentives to maintain infrastructure and avoid driving traders away, although immediate military needs or dynastic rivalry could encourage destructive decisions.

Consider a hypothetical comparison between two neighboring ports. Port A charges high duties but protects goods and resolves disputes quickly. Port B charges less but frequently seizes cargo and cannot provide safe anchorage. Which attracts merchants? The answer depends on the full expected cost, not the posted tax rate alone.
