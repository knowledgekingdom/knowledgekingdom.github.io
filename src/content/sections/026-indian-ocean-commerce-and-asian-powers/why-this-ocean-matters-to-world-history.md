Indian Ocean history challenges three habits of thought. The first is the habit of assigning world commerce a European beginning. European voyages around Africa were significant, but they entered a prosperous and intricate maritime system. The second is reducing trade to maps of arrows and lists of luxury goods. The system depended on farmers, textile makers, shipbuilders, pilots, credit relationships, urban institutions and religious communities. The third is confusing a navy's capacity for violence with complete economic or political control.

We should also resist the opposite romantic myth of a peaceful, open Asian ocean destroyed at once by Europeans. Competition, slave trading, taxation, raids, war and unequal access already existed. European powers introduced new military and institutional pressures and eventually developed forms of imperial domination with extraordinary consequences, but the changes were gradual, uneven and shaped by Asian and African responses. Historical actors were neither flawless heroes nor faceless victims of a destiny decided elsewhere.

The deepest lesson is that networks connect societies without erasing their differences. A port can be locally governed yet globally influential; a cloth made by one community can matter to a buyer on another continent; and a powerful ruler may depend on intermediaries who do not obey every order. In Chapter 27 we turn to European maritime expansion more directly.
