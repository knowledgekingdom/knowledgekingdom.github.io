The region of Gujarat in northwestern India was one of the great manufacturing and commercial centers of the Indian Ocean world. Cotton textiles, dyes, skilled artisans and inland trading relationships supported merchants who operated between the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, western Indian ports and Southeast Asia. The Sultanate of Gujarat became an important regional power in the fifteenth century, while its rulers and commercial groups did not always have identical interests. Political rivalries could coexist with lively exchange.

Gujarat's importance explains why Indian Ocean trade cannot be understood only by following spice-producing islands. A ship bound for the Malay world might carry Indian cloth because that cloth could purchase pepper and other desired goods. Cloth was not merely an object wrapped around the 'real' commodity; it was a medium of exchange embedded in the tastes and production systems of different communities. Merchants also had to think about credit, the timing of the monsoon and protection against predatory rulers or raiders.

From the mid-sixteenth century, changing Mughal influence brought parts of the region into new imperial relationships; Akbar's conquest of Gujarat in 1572–73 is a major milestone. But the merchants, port facilities and production networks that gave the region economic importance were older than Mughal rule.
