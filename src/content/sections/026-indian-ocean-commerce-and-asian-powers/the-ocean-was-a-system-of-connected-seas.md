The phrase Indian Ocean describes an immense marine region, not a single commercial highway. On its western side lay the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, Arabian Sea and Persian Gulf. To the east lay the Bay of Bengal, the Straits of Malacca and waterways extending into the South China Sea. East African river valleys, the Indian subcontinent, the islands of Southeast Asia and inland Chinese markets supplied or consumed goods shipped through these waters. Geography created multiple routes rather than one chain of ports in a straight line.

A map can make these connections seem simpler than they were. Ships stopped for water, repairs, information and favorable winds. A bale of cloth from western India might be purchased by a merchant at Melaka and eventually reach another island without its original owner ever seeing that destination. Goods could pass from camel caravans to river boats to larger sailing vessels. A commercially important place was often not a producer of the most valuable commodity: its power came from organizing exchange among different places.

Ports also faced inland. The Swahili coast was tied to African hinterlands; Gujarat's coast drew on farming, textile manufacture and credit networks; Chinese coastal cities were connected to large internal markets. Calling the system maritime should not hide the farmers, weavers, porters, boatbuilders and people compelled to work who made sea trade possible.
