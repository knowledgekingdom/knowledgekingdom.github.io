Vasco da Gama's voyage around the Cape of Good Hope brought a Portuguese royal fleet to the Malabar coast in 1498. The event was transformative for European access to Asian goods and for the political ambitions of Portugal's monarchy. It was not the 'discovery' of India, an established center of population, political authority, learning and maritime exchange. The sailing route likewise connected Portugal to a commercially experienced world that did not lack knowledge of the sea.

Portuguese ambitions mixed profit, royal prestige, religious conflict and strategic hopes of redirecting spice commerce away from established routes toward the Red Sea and Mediterranean. The initial contacts at Calicut exposed large gaps in knowledge and expectations on all sides. Portuguese goods and diplomatic offerings did not necessarily command the prestige expected at court; established merchants had commercial interests that clashed with demands for exclusive privileges. The Portuguese crown wanted an advantage greater than another foreign trading community would normally receive.

Later encounters rapidly became violent. Portuguese forces used maritime raids, bombardment and seizure of ships while also negotiating commercial alliances. Local rulers made choices rooted in their own rivalries rather than accepting a single Asian or European bloc. The best explanation of 1498 is therefore neither a tale of universal Western genius nor a minor incident with no long-term consequences.
