Chinese merchants, migrants and state officials had long participated in maritime networks reaching Southeast Asia and beyond. The Song and Yuan periods supported extensive commercial activity through ports such as Quanzhou. Under the Ming dynasty, policy toward overseas exchange shifted across reigns, combining restrictions on some private ventures with forms of licensed trade, diplomacy and practical accommodation. There was no timeless policy of complete 'Chinese isolation.' The state could regulate particular channels while ships and traders continued to move through others.

Maritime commerce linked Chinese consumers and producers to pepper, aromatics, forest products, ceramics, silk and regional markets. A piece of Chinese porcelain found in an East African excavation demonstrates movement but not necessarily a Chinese trader's direct visit to the specific archaeological site. It may have passed through numerous hands. This distinction is crucial for reconstructing a network whose goods traveled farther than most participants.

Ming rulers also pursued international standing through embassies, ritual exchange and symbolic demonstrations of authority. Those aims overlapped with commercial interests without reducing foreign policy to a simple search for profit.
