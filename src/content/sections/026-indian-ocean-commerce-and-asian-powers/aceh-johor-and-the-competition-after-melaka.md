After the Portuguese conquest of Melaka, commercial and political activity did not simply stop at the strait. Aceh in northern Sumatra emerged as an influential sultanate with strong links to Islamic scholarly and merchant networks. Johor, connected to the political legacy of the Melaka ruling house, also became important in competition for trade and authority. Patterns differed across reigns, and each ruler operated amid competing regional alliances. A shift in a ship's itinerary could have far-reaching consequences for port revenue and political prestige.

Aceh's ties to Indian and Red Sea networks show that early modern Southeast Asian Muslim rulers were active diplomatic participants. Exchanges involving Ottoman authorities have sometimes been depicted as a permanent worldwide Islamic military alliance. The surviving evidence is better understood in the context of specific requests for aid, recognition, expertise and protection against Portuguese attacks. The ambitions could be extensive while the material support remained intermittent. The two are not the same.

The diversity of alternative ports limited efforts to create a complete Portuguese monopoly. Merchants wanted safe anchorage, predictable dues, buyers and access to imported goods. A commercially damaged city could lose traders to competitors, but those competitors faced their own political risks.
