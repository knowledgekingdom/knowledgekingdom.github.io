Near the end of our period, new European ventures joined the contest. The English East India Company was chartered in 1600 and the Dutch East India Company, or VOC, was founded in 1602. These corporations possessed distinctive arrangements for mobilizing capital and obtaining political authority from their states. Their later histories of war, monopoly and colonial rule deserve separate treatment; at the start, they confronted pre-existing Asian merchants and rulers rather than an empty economic field.

The VOC gradually assembled fortified trading positions and used military force, particularly in parts of Southeast Asia, while English merchants developed their own commercial relationships. Batavia, founded by the Dutch in 1619 at the site of Jayakarta, became central to the VOC's Asian system. The Dutch campaign for control of nutmeg production in Banda included massacres, dispossession and coerced labor in 1621. Such violence cannot be left out of a history focused on prices and commercial innovation.

Competition among European powers did not replace Asian competition; the two became intertwined. Local rulers sometimes sought advantage by dealing with different companies, while companies were constrained by Indian textile producers, Asian consumers and complex shipping calendars. Much of their profitable exchange took place within Asia rather than simply carrying Asian goods home to Europe.
