The Portuguese searched for local partners who could help them establish a durable position on the Malabar coast. Their relationships with Cochin, their confrontations with the Zamorin of Calicut and their eventual center at Goa were shaped by pre-existing regional rivalries. An Indian ruler might welcome military assistance to counter a stronger neighbor while resisting the terms that foreign captains later demanded. Local participation was not the same as passive acceptance of colonial power.

This pattern can be understood through a simple political choice. If a new fleet offers an ally cannon and maritime protection, a smaller port may gain short-term bargaining power. But the fleet may later demand customs revenue, religious privileges or exclusive supply. The ally then faces a new dependency. Portuguese officers, too, might find themselves reliant on a ruler for supplies, intelligence and permissions. The balance could change from one season to the next.

Historians should distinguish a documented treaty from effective compliance. A ruler's promise to channel pepper through one port does not show that all growers obeyed or that other buyers disappeared. Official Portuguese correspondence may exaggerate achievements, while hostile regional chronicles may emphasize atrocities or betrayal.
