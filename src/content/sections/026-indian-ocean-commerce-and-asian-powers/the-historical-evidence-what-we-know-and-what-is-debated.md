Our knowledge comes from different archives with very different biases. Archaeological finds reveal imported goods and local production. Coins, inscriptions, ceramics and surviving textiles can sometimes be dated or linked to places of origin. Merchant letters and contracts reveal commercial problems that official chronicles overlooked. Chinese court records, Arabic and Persian writings, South Asian chronicles, Malay traditions and Portuguese accounts provide overlapping but unequal windows onto maritime politics.

A European ship's log may record a fleet's arrival precisely while misrepresenting the intentions of a local ruler. A royal inscription may announce a victory or claim universal authority while providing limited evidence for day-to-day control. Much of what wealthy merchants recorded is lost, and the voices of agricultural workers, women and enslaved people are especially difficult to recover. The period around 1500 appears unusually vivid partly because European archives add substantial documentation. More surviving paper does not mean that economic activity began only when Europeans wrote about it.

Several famous claims deserve caution: the precise size of Zheng He's ships, the exact volume of trade through one port, the total number of victims of particular raids and the reach of any claimed monopoly. A careful historian distinguishes an established event from disputed quantitative detail.
