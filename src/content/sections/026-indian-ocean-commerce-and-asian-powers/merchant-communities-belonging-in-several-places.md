Some trading communities maintained relatives, language networks or religious institutions in multiple ports. Gujarati merchants, Hadrami and other Arabian merchants, Persian-speaking intermediaries, Tamil and other South Asian trading groups, and Chinese settlers helped build commercial relationships. Such categories were never perfectly sealed. Individuals could learn languages, marry locally, change religious affiliations or move from one occupation to another. We should avoid imagining permanent, homogeneous 'foreign quarters' with no interaction with residents.

Temporary settlement became especially useful where monsoon schedules demanded waiting. A religious endowment might support a school or mosque, a household might provide shelter to a visiting trader, and a multilingual broker could connect officials with people who spoke another language. These arrangements sometimes made large-scale trade possible with fewer permanent imperial officers than would be needed to compel every transaction. Merchant diasporas were not simply passive visitors: they shaped local tastes, politics and urban life.

Nor did shared religion guarantee harmony. Merchants belonging to the same broad faith could compete bitterly, while trading partnerships could cross religious boundaries. Communities also included dependents, women, servants and enslaved people, whose lives are less visible in records dominated by wealthy men.
