Chapter 32 described the American, French, and Haitian revolutions. Their effects crossed borders through ships, newspapers, soldiers, refugees, and rumor. The successful independence of the United States showed one possible outcome of colonial war. France's revolution destabilized monarchy and inspired arguments about rights, but its wars also threatened the European balance of power. Haiti's independence in 1804 demonstrated that enslaved people could overthrow plantation slavery and sustain a sovereign state against imperial reconquest.

These examples were interpreted very differently. Some American elites admired constitutional arguments while fearing mass upheaval. Slaveholders could invoke colonial autonomy yet recoil from the possibility of emancipation. Political leaders sometimes looked to France or Britain for aid while insisting that their own societies were unlike those of Europe. Ideas did not arrive in the Americas as finished instructions; people adapted or rejected them according to immediate interests and risks.

Transatlantic connections also worked through economic disruption. The wars of the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic era interrupted trade routes, changed naval control, and forced merchants and governments to rethink alliances. The trigger of 1808 can be understood only within that wider context. A European military crisis created an American argument about who had the right to govern.
