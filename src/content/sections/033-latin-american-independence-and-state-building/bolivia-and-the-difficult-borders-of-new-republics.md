Upper Peru, linked historically to mining centers such as Potosí, was claimed or contested through different political projects during the wars. After the military collapse of Spanish control, an assembly declared a new state in 1825 that took the name Bolivia in honor of Bolívar. The choice underscores the importance of symbolic founders while also revealing the limits of personal reputation as an explanation for actual state formation.

The new state inherited sharply unequal landholding patterns, Indigenous communities with distinct obligations and legal traditions, a damaged economy, and disputes over which provinces belonged together. Bolivian constitutional debates reflected efforts to produce political stability after war, sometimes including proposals for powerful executive offices. None of these dilemmas could be solved by choosing an inspiring national name.

This region illustrates the complexity of the word <em>nation</em>. A state can proclaim sovereignty over people who speak different languages, identify with different communities, and disagree about the legitimacy of a distant capital. Creating national symbols and constitutions is one process; building roads, courts, credible taxes, and trust in institutions is another.
