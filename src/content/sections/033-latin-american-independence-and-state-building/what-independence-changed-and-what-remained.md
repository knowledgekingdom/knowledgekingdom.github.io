The early nineteenth-century independence movements ended most Spanish rule over the mainland Americas and created new political units with their own constitutions, armies, diplomatic relationships, and public symbols. They challenged doctrines of dynastic sovereignty and opened arguments about citizenship and equality. Political careers, civic associations, print cultures, and legal debates developed in ways that would have been difficult to imagine under the old imperial system.

Yet the transitions were neither complete nor uniform. Indigenous territorial conflicts persisted; some states preserved slavery for decades; women's political exclusion remained widespread; and wealth and public office often stayed concentrated. Military conflict and uncertain revenue limited state capacity. New republics could be legally sovereign yet economically constrained. Even so, their peoples were not prisoners of a single colonial fate: political institutions evolved through repeated struggles and negotiations throughout the nineteenth century.

The most useful conclusion is that <strong>independence is an event and state building is a continuing process</strong>. A declaration can break an imperial claim. Building a functioning country demands agreements about who governs, who pays, who belongs, whose rights are protected, and what happens when citizens disagree. Chapter 34 will follow another transformation with global implications: industrialization, the changing organization of work, and the growth of urban society.
