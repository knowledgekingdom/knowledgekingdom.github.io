The Spanish American world before independence had institutions rather than a simple division between rulers and ruled. Royal officers collected taxes and administered justice; city councils supervised local matters; clerical institutions owned property, provided education, and shaped everyday life; corporate bodies and guilds asserted privileges. Indigenous communities retained varying forms of local government and communal land, often under restrictive legal arrangements. Litigation and petitions could sometimes defend customary claims, although access to justice was unequal and outcomes depended on power and resources.

Spanish-born officials and merchants, often described as <em>peninsulares</em>, competed with American-born people of Spanish descent, commonly called <em>criollos</em>, over office and status. Yet this distinction did not determine every political allegiance. Some American-born elites defended the monarchy, some Spanish-born people favored reform, and the interests of poorer groups were not reducible to either camp. Legal categories concerning ancestry and status existed, but actual social position was influenced by property, reputation, gender, occupation, and local relationships.

The Crown's reforms in the eighteenth century attempted to raise revenue, strengthen defenses, and improve administrative control. They created opportunities for some merchants and conflicts with some regional interests. To explain independence, it is necessary to examine these earlier bargains: people were accustomed to arguing about legitimate taxation, offices, exemptions, local privileges, and the rights of communities long before the dramatic events of 1808.
