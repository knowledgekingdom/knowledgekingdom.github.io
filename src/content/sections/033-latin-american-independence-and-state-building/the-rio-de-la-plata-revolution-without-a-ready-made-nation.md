In Buenos Aires, the May Revolution of 1810 replaced the local viceroyal authority with a junta. Its leaders faced disagreements about legitimacy, political representation, and the relationship between the port city and interior provinces. The Río de la Plata was a large administrative jurisdiction that included areas corresponding to several modern countries. Its regions did not automatically accept that Buenos Aires had inherited the viceroy's right to command them.

In Paraguay, local political developments led toward a separate independent trajectory. In the Banda Oriental, associated with present-day Uruguay, José Gervasio Artigas advocated a confederal vision rooted partly in provincial autonomy and local social demands. These competing projects were not small distractions from one Argentine nation. They were disputes about whether former imperial units should become one state, a federation of states, or several states.

The Congress of Tucumán declared the independence of the United Provinces of South America in 1816. Yet a declaration did not resolve the authority of a capital, provincial fiscal rights, or military control. Buenos Aires's customs revenues were particularly contentious: a port with international shipping could finance administration more easily than inland provinces whose commerce depended on routes through it. Geography, revenue, and sovereignty were tightly connected.
