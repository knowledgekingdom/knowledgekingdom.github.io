The armies of independence were not neatly separated by ancestry or social class. Royalist forces recruited American-born officers, Indigenous soldiers, free people of color, and other local supporters. Republican armies likewise relied on populations whose reasons for fighting might have little resemblance to constitutional pamphlets. Men enlisted for pay, protection, opportunity, local loyalties, coercion, and hopes of emancipation. Recruitment was sometimes voluntary, sometimes pressured, and often shaped by the circumstances of villages and estates.

One example was the participation of <em>llaneros</em>, mounted fighters from the plains of Venezuela and adjacent regions. Their mobility and familiarity with livestock-rich environments made them militarily significant. Different commanders and groups of llaneros fought on different sides at different times. José Antonio Páez became especially important to the patriot cause. This variation refutes the image of a single popular class naturally aligned with one revolutionary banner.

Military promises could produce political change. A commander who needed soldiers might offer personal freedom to enslaved recruits or pledge reforms that threatened established property relations. Yet wartime promises did not always become stable peacetime rights. Compare three stages in every mobilization story: what leaders offered, what recruits understood, and what governments actually delivered after the fighting.
