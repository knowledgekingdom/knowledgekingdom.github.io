Several territories experienced early republics or constitutional experiments that could not withstand military pressure and internal division. Venezuela's first republic, declared in 1811, collapsed in 1812 amid royalist successes, political fractures, an earthquake interpreted by some as a sign of divine disfavor, and logistical problems. In New Granada, rival provincial governments disagreed about whether sovereignty belonged in a central capital or in local communities. A shared opposition to distant rule did not ensure unity about the substitute government.

Failure did not prove that ordinary people were unfit for self-government. Republics confronting war needed revenue, food, soldiers, reliable communications, and allegiance from provinces with competing traditions. Royalist armies could exploit these divisions, while independent commanders had to keep coalitions together under extraordinary pressures. The term <em>royalist</em> itself included local American actors, not simply soldiers arriving from Spain.

The early experiments warn against hindsight. Because some states eventually became independent, later patriotic histories may portray the first uprisings as an inevitable opening stage. Participants did not know the result. In several regions they faced years of reversals, restoration, and renewed warfare before a durable break with the monarchy became possible.
