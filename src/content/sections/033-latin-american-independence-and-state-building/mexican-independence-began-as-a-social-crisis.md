New Spain's crisis did not follow the northern South American route. In 1810 the priest Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla launched an uprising commonly remembered through the <em>Grito de Dolores</em>. The mobilization drew large crowds and carried urgent grievances about hierarchy and colonial government, but its objectives changed as the movement expanded. The violence of war, attacks on property, and fears of social revolution alienated many elites who might otherwise have considered institutional reform.

Hidalgo's insurgent forces gained territory and suffered major defeats; Hidalgo was captured and executed in 1811. Leadership passed in part to José María Morelos, who developed a more explicit program for independence and social change. His <em>Sentimientos de la Nación</em> of 1813 advocated political sovereignty and significant reforms, though the war's military pressures limited their implementation. Morelos was executed in 1815.

A history centered only on 1821 misses the decade of village warfare, guerrilla campaigns, repression, and economic devastation that came before. Yet it is equally misleading to assume Hidalgo's movement and the eventual independence settlement shared all their aims. Mexican independence emerged from changing coalitions, not a continuous political organization with one fixed ideology.
