Not every Spanish possession in the Americas became independent in the 1820s. Cuba and Puerto Rico remained under Spanish rule long afterward. Cuba's economy was expanding through sugar plantations and slave labor, and powerful interests feared the social upheaval that a war for independence might bring. Geopolitical pressures and the strength of Spanish military positions also helped shape the different trajectory of the islands.

The continuing sovereignty of Spain in the Caribbean complicates a common textbook statement that Spain simply lost all its American territories after Ayacucho. Mainland independence was transformative, but it did not end the Spanish Atlantic empire. Cuba experienced further struggles for independence later in the nineteenth century, and its final separation from Spain came amid the Spanish-American War of 1898. Puerto Rico passed to United States rule in the same year rather than becoming an independent republic.

A map shaded by dates can conceal the distinct experiences of island populations, including enslaved people and free communities of African descent, whose choices were conditioned by plantation economies. The Caribbean belonged to the Latin American political story, but it did not follow one continental timetable.
