Women contributed to independence struggles by transporting information, organizing supplies, sheltering fugitives, managing estates during war, fundraising, and participating in political networks. Figures such as Manuela Sáenz, associated with Bolívar and the independence cause, and Leona Vicario, involved in Mexican insurgent networks, are remembered for significant political action. Yet historical attention to a few celebrated individuals can obscure the broader contributions of women whose names are less frequently preserved.

Women's activities also make visible the limits of the new republics. Most nineteenth-century constitutions did not recognize women as equal participants in national elections, and family law often continued to place substantial authority in men's hands. Women could be central to producing the conditions of independence while being excluded from defining political membership afterward. This was not simply an oversight of a document; it reflected entrenched assumptions about household, property, and public life.

Historical sources create an additional problem. Military correspondence and official decrees record prominent leaders more easily than domestic provisioning, clandestine networks, or coerced labor. To reconstruct women's experiences, historians combine petitions, court cases, letters, biographies, newspapers, and records of institutions. Absence from a military dispatch is not proof of absence from the revolution.
