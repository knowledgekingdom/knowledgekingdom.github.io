The Brazilian Empire preserved slavery on a vast scale. Its plantation and mining economies were deeply entangled with Atlantic commercial networks and the forced migration of Africans. Independence in 1822 did not emancipate enslaved people. In fact, Brazil continued to import large numbers of enslaved Africans in subsequent decades despite growing pressure against the Atlantic slave trade. The overseas trade was effectively suppressed in the 1850s; slavery itself remained legal until 1888.

A constitution in 1824 organized imperial government and provided for representative bodies within a system of restricted political rights and a powerful monarch. Enslaved people were excluded from citizenship, while other forms of economic, gender, and property hierarchy persisted. Provincial power and the difficulties of holding a vast territory together produced political conflict, especially during the regency after Pedro I's abdication in 1831.

Comparing Brazil with Spanish American republics reveals a caution. Stability under a monarchy was not identical to justice, and republican form was not proof of inclusive liberty. Brazil's more continuous central institutions came with slavery and unequal citizenship; some republican societies made different promises without consistently enforcing them. Every comparison needs several criteria rather than a single ranking.
