Peru's political landscape was complex. Lima housed major imperial institutions, and substantial elites feared that insurgent warfare or radical social change might threaten property and status. Royalist authority had deep administrative and military roots. But the region also contained long histories of Indigenous resistance, localized republican sympathies, and social groups whose interests did not match official Lima. Calling Peru uniformly royalist hides profound regional differences.

San Martín proclaimed Peruvian independence in Lima in July 1821, but that event did not mean the country was fully under republican control. Royalist forces retained important positions, while the patriot side struggled to finance armies and secure unified authority. The meeting between San Martín and Bolívar at Guayaquil in 1822 has become famous partly because its precise political discussions are imperfectly documented. Historians can describe the meeting and subsequent developments without pretending every motive is recoverable.

The final major continental campaigns involved Bolívar, Sucre, Peruvian leaders, and fighters from several regions. The victory at Junín in August 1824 and Sucre's victory at Ayacucho in December greatly weakened the remaining royalist armies in South America. But formal military victory, negotiations, and withdrawal of royalist positions occurred on different schedules. Independence arrived through overlapping political and military processes rather than one ceremonial instant.
