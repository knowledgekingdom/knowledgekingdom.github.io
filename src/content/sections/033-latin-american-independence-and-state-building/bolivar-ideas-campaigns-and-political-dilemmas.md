Simón Bolívar, born in Caracas in 1783, became the best-known leader of northern South American independence. His career included political exile, military defeat, renewed campaigning, and an increasingly ambitious vision of a united northern republic. He did not simply march from one victory to another. His <em>Cartagena Manifesto</em> of 1812 diagnosed failures of the first Venezuelan republic; his <em>Jamaica Letter</em> of 1815 explored the crisis of imperial rule and possible futures for Spanish America.

Bolívar's political thought combined republican aspirations with fears of fragmentation. He valued independence and representative institutions while repeatedly worrying that weak executives and rival provincial interests would make new states vulnerable. At different moments he supported unusually strong presidential authority and emergency measures. This tension does not reduce him to either a pure liberator or an aspiring dictator. It reflects one of the period's central arguments: how could a republican government remain effective during prolonged war?

His reputation also depends on what a historian asks. A military narrative emphasizes daring campaigns and battles. A constitutional history examines competing models of citizenship and executive power. A social history asks whose labor, military service, and resources sustained armies. Bolívar's significance is considerable, but independence was not the achievement of one man acting alone.
