The independence period did not invent resistance to colonial power. In the Andes, the massive rebellion associated with Túpac Amaru II began in 1780. José Gabriel Condorcanqui, who adopted the name Túpac Amaru II, challenged colonial abuses and drew support from diverse groups while confronting conflicting aims among followers and opponents. Around the same period, Túpac Katari led a major uprising in the region of present-day Bolivia, including the siege of La Paz. Colonial forces suppressed these rebellions with intense violence.

Their histories cannot be converted into straightforward rehearsals for republican independence. Some participants pursued remedies within monarchical language; others advanced more sweeping forms of social and political transformation. The struggles included conflicts over tribute, forced labor, commerce, race, and local authority. Nor was there a single Indigenous political perspective. Communities could be divided, and alliances changed with local circumstances.

These earlier movements mattered because they exposed how precarious colonial order could be and because elite revolutionaries later confronted the fear that popular mobilization might escape their control. Historians should avoid a double erasure: portraying independence as created only by prominent criollo men, or pretending that eighteenth-century Indigenous rebellions pursued exactly the same constitutional goals as later republican leaders.
