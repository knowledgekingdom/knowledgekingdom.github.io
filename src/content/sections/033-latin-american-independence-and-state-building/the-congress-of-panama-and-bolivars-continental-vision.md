In 1826 representatives from several newly independent American states met at the Congress of Panama, inspired by Bolívar's hopes for cooperation among republics. The project addressed diplomacy, common security, and the possibility of collective action in a world still dominated by European empires. It also exposed difficult disagreements about who should attend, which obligations states would accept, and whether shared institutions might threaten national sovereignty.

The congress did not create a durable continental federation. Weak communication, competing national priorities, and the difficulty of ratifying common obligations limited its results. Yet the fact of the meeting remains important: independence leaders did not all imagine that new states must pursue isolation. Regional cooperation was considered from the beginning, even while many of the same governments struggled to remain united internally.

Rather than judging Panama only by its lack of immediate institutional success, ask what problem participants sought to solve. A small or indebted state may gain security from an alliance, but only if partners can be trusted to honor commitments. The question connects directly to later chapters on diplomacy, balance of power, and international organizations.
