A comparative table clarifies the range of outcomes. Mexico experienced prolonged insurgency, brief empire, republican constitutional change, and repeated struggles over federal authority. Gran Colombia attempted to unite much of northern South America but fragmented. The Central American federation also dissolved amid provincial conflicts. Brazil preserved a monarchical center for much of the nineteenth century while retaining slavery and major regional inequalities. These examples cannot be ranked simply by how long a constitution lasted.

Why the divergence? The presence of an established capital, the geography of trade routes, the organization of armies, the distribution of revenues, the social status of labor, and the bargains that local elites would accept differed across cases. Countries also faced different external threats and commercial opportunities. The strongest explanation identifies mechanisms that can be checked against sources instead of attributing all difficulties to a vague regional mentality.

A counterfactual exercise is useful: what might have helped a large federation endure? More dependable shared revenues, credible provincial guarantees, stronger courts, and a common army answerable to constitutional institutions are plausible candidates. But a counterfactual is not a recovered historical fact. Each suggestion should be tested against the interests and capabilities of the actual actors, not treated as a simple solution they foolishly ignored.
