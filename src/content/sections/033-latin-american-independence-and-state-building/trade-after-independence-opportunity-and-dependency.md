The break with Iberian rule opened commercial possibilities and disrupted others. Merchants could seek new partners, and British trade, finance, and shipping gained influence in many regions. Governments sometimes reduced old monopolies and revised tariffs, but wars damaged infrastructure and destabilized currencies. Overseas investors did not simply arrive to create prosperity; commercial relationships involved bargaining, unequal information, credit risk, and the priorities of local political elites.

Some regions exported agricultural goods, hides, metals, or other commodities while importing manufactures. Such trade could provide tax revenue and consumer goods. It could also expose producers and governments to changes in foreign demand and borrowing conditions. Differences among countries mattered: the economic legacies of silver mining in Mexico were unlike those of a coastal export district or Brazil's plantation economy.

A useful distinction is between <em>political independence</em> and <em>economic autonomy</em>. An independent state chooses its formal laws, but its choices can be constrained by creditors, military weakness, technology, markets, and internal inequalities. That does not mean commerce was merely a disguised continuation of colonialism. It means each commercial relationship should be evaluated for its opportunities, costs, bargaining power, and distribution of gains.
