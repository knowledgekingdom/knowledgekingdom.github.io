Independence history was often written first by participants who wanted to justify decisions, praise comrades, condemn enemies, or claim political legitimacy. Later national textbooks sometimes presented each republic's origin as a clear march toward an inevitable nation. These accounts preserve valuable evidence, but they can hide losers, disputed boundaries, inconsistent motives, and changes in allegiance. A national holiday commemorates a chosen event; it cannot by itself settle the whole historical process.

Researchers compare decrees, constitutions, military letters, tax receipts, court records, church archives, shipping records, community petitions, and accounts from different political sides. A constitutional article demonstrates what lawmakers prescribed, not necessarily what happened in a rural district. A patriotic memoir shows how an officer remembered events, not every fact from the battlefield. An Indigenous petition records a community's claim to land but may omit disagreements within that community.

When facing a famous claim, ask three questions: <strong>What can the source actually establish? Whose perspective does it preserve? What independent evidence could challenge it?</strong> A good historical explanation does not deny the importance of exceptional individuals. It places individual decisions inside the social and institutional conditions that made them effective or limited them.
