The new republics approached slavery along different timelines. Warfare created opportunities for manumission and emancipation through military service. Some governments adopted gradual abolition rules, often using <em>free-womb</em> principles under which children born to enslaved mothers after a specified date would eventually attain freedom, sometimes after obligatory service. Other authorities maintained slavery or granted exceptions. Legal status could be shaped by region, age, military service, and the interests of owners.

It is therefore misleading to write that Spanish American independence immediately abolished slavery. In Colombia slavery was abolished in 1851 with implementation in 1852; other former Spanish colonies had different timelines. Brazil's slavery survived until 1888, and Cuba's until 1886. These dates illustrate the variety of abolition processes but do not describe all the social changes involved. Formerly enslaved people often confronted unequal labor contracts, discrimination, and limited access to land.

Freedom also had political meanings beyond the abolition of ownership. Free people of African descent participated as soldiers, artisans, traders, householders, and organizers. They contested racial hierarchies, sought legal protections, and influenced local politics. A chapter centered only on elite speeches would miss how emancipation and citizenship were negotiated in workplaces, courts, churches, and military camps.
