The Sicilian disaster did not immediately end Athens, but it weakened the empire and encouraged rebellion among its allies. Sparta received Persian financial support for naval operations, illustrating how former enemies could cooperate when interests changed. Internal political conflict sharpened. In <strong>411 BCE</strong>, an oligarchic group briefly took control of Athens; later, after military defeat in <strong>404 BCE</strong>, Sparta's victory was followed by the installation of the oligarchic regime commonly called the <strong>Thirty</strong>. Its leaders used violence and repression, provoking resistance.

Democracy was restored in <strong>403 BCE</strong>, accompanied by an amnesty that helped many citizens rebuild political life, although it did not erase losses or guarantee reconciliation for every individual. Athens remained important culturally and politically but no longer enjoyed its former imperial dominance. Sparta, meanwhile, faced severe challenges to its own power. A military victory did not create permanent regional stability.

The restoration shows that political institutions can recover after crisis, yet recovery involves choices: punishment or compromise, rule of law or revenge, inclusion of former opponents or exclusion. Ancient Athens offers no painless formula. It offers cases in which institutions survived, failed, and were rebuilt under pressure. It also reminds us that political memories are contested: supporters of one regime may describe restoration as justice while its victims remember fear and dispossession.
