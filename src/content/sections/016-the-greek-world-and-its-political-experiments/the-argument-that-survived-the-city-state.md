The decision on Sicily ended badly, but the questions it raised did not end with the destruction of Athenian armies. Was the expedition in the interest of the city or the ambitions of speakers? Can large assemblies recognize strategic limits? Do leaders make better decisions when they listen to dissent? How should citizens judge decisions whose costs fall on people excluded from the vote? How can a society praise its own freedom while using force abroad?

The Greek world matters not because it was uniquely virtuous, but because its communities left unusually rich evidence of people debating public life while trying very different arrangements of power. Sparta, Athens, Corinth, Thebes and the overseas Greek poleis created no single Greek answer. Their wars revealed limits of civic solidarity, and their writers described those failures with striking frankness. They received influences from older civilizations, reshaped them, and passed questions forward through later Mediterranean, Islamic, European and global traditions.

When you hear that 'the Greeks invented democracy,' make the claim more precise. Ask when, where, for whom, by which institutions and at whose expense. Then ask which of those questions still trouble us. The answer is not a list of marble monuments. It is a habit of political reasoning: distinguish the community from the state, the vote from the outcome, the citizen from the resident, power from legitimacy, and a confident speech from a good decision.
