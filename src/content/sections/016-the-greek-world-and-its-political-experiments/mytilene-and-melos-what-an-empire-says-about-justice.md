One of the most disturbing aspects of Athenian political life appears in debates over conquered or rebellious communities. In 427 BCE, after Mytilene revolted from Athenian authority, the Assembly initially supported severe collective punishment. It reconsidered the decision the next day and chose a less sweeping course, although leading rebels were executed and substantial consequences followed. Thucydides presents contrasting arguments that force the reader to ask whether policies should follow anger, deterrence, expediency or justice.

In 416 BCE, Athens demanded that the neutral island of <strong>Melos</strong> submit to its power. Thucydides' famous dialogue between Athenian representatives and Melian leaders presents a stark contrast between the security calculations of the stronger side and the claims of justice and independence made by the weaker. The Athenians eventually captured the island, killed adult male prisoners and enslaved women and children according to his account. The dialogue should not be treated as a stenographic transcript, but the episode demonstrates how imperial coercion could contradict Athens' self-image as a city of freedom.

An intellectually honest analysis must distinguish explanation from excuse. We can understand why a government fearful of defections might impose harsh punishment without approving its choices. Likewise, claims about justice can be sincere even when a weaker state makes a strategically disastrous decision. The Greeks did not invent the modern problem of power and morality. Their historians made it unusually vivid, because the same political society that fostered citizen debate also deliberated over the fate of people with no vote in its Assembly.
