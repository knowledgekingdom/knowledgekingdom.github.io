Between approximately the eighth and sixth centuries BCE, Greek communities founded settlements across the Mediterranean and Black Sea, including in Sicily, southern Italy, the Aegean, the northern Black Sea and the western Mediterranean. Modern historians often call this 'colonization,' but it did not reproduce the institutions of modern European overseas empires. Founders frequently established new independent poleis rather than obedient outposts of a unified mother country. Existing local inhabitants already occupied these regions; relations ranged from exchange and intermarriage to displacement and violence.

Why did people migrate? Pressure on land, political conflict, trade opportunities, family networks and strategic locations all mattered, but we should not assume one explanation for every foundation. Agricultural households might seek better land; merchants might value safe harbors; defeated political factions might rebuild their status elsewhere. Greek speakers encountered Phoenician traders, Etruscans, Egyptians, Anatolians and many others. Metalworking, shipbuilding, art and religious imagery changed through those contacts. The Met's collections reveal how Archaic Greek artists actively adopted and transformed Near Eastern and Egyptian motifs rather than inventing their visual culture in isolation.

Syracuse in Sicily became a major power with its own political history, not simply a distant suburb of Athens. Massalia, the settlement that developed into Marseille, linked Mediterranean commerce to inland Europe. Greek language and customs spread unevenly, while local societies retained agency. Whenever we study a 'Greek' artifact found abroad, we must ask whether it was produced there, imported, imitated, traded, seized or dedicated. Material culture does not automatically identify the ethnicity of its user.
