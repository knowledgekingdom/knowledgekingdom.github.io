In many Archaic poleis, aristocratic families held political office, controlled substantial land and claimed inherited prestige. Rising commercial fortunes, military obligations and disputes over debt could strain such arrangements. Putting laws in writing sometimes reduced the secrecy of elite decisions, but a written rule is not necessarily a just rule. A law protecting property might favor creditors over desperate farmers. And a community could write down laws while denying women, immigrants or enslaved people any share in governing.

The Greek word <em>tyrannos</em> originally referred broadly to an individual who gained exceptional or irregular sole power; it did not always mean a uniquely cruel ruler. Some tyrants built temples, sponsored festivals, strengthened economies and displaced entrenched aristocrats. Others ruled harshly, used violence and sought to secure power for their families. The crucial distinction is between the path to and concentration of authority, on one hand, and the particular results of a ruler's decisions, on the other. The latter varied.

A political reform can be beneficial while leaving the underlying problem of accountability unresolved. If one ruler cancels debts or pays for public works, how can the community replace him when he becomes abusive? If powerful families alone control courts, who protects the poor? Greek thinkers and political actors did not settle these questions once; different poleis arrived at contrasting institutional answers.
