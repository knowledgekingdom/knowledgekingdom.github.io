During the middle decades of the fifth century BCE, Athens invested heavily in public spaces and monumental architecture. The <strong>Parthenon</strong>, constructed on the Acropolis in the 440s BCE, was dedicated to Athena and formed part of a broader religious and civic landscape. Its sculpture celebrated divine stories and civic ideals. It was never simply a timeless monument to abstract democracy. The building demanded quarrying, transport, skilled labor, money and administrative coordination, and its financing must be understood in the context of Athenian imperial resources.

Pericles, a leading Athenian politician and elected general, became closely associated with the city during much of this period. He encouraged policies and projects that strengthened Athenian identity, naval power and the capacity of the poorer citizen classes to participate. Public payment for some civic duties made participation more possible for people who could not afford to abandon their work. Yet a famous leader in a democracy could still wield disproportionate influence by persuasion, patronage and reputation. The existence of a citizen assembly did not eliminate elite leadership.

Imagine two equally eligible citizens asked to spend a day serving the city. One owns land and has someone to supervise his fields. The other must earn wages to feed his household. If no payment is offered, the formal right to serve may be much more useful to the first. Public compensation can therefore change <em>effective</em> participation without changing formal eligibility. But someone must pay for that compensation. A government can enlarge political inclusion through money received from a wider, unequal system. Whether a policy is democratic in one sense does not settle whether its economic foundations are just.
