Sparta became one of the most distinctive Greek communities. Its full citizen men, often called <em>Spartiates</em>, cultivated an identity emphasizing collective discipline, military preparation and communal obligation. However, this citizen order depended upon the labor of a much larger noncitizen population, especially the <strong>helots</strong> of Laconia and Messenia. Helots were unfree, subject to exploitation and coercion, and not simply wage workers. The subordination of Messenia enabled Spartan citizen households to pursue military and civic duties while others produced much of the agricultural surplus.

That dependence shaped political fear. Spartan authorities worried about helot resistance and the possibility that war away from home would expose the social system to revolt. They maintained an intense emphasis on cohesion, surveillance and military readiness. Ancient accounts describe brutal treatment and state violence, although details of specific practices and their chronology require careful evaluation. Spartan unity had a cost borne unequally across the population.

Women in Sparta held a position that differed in some respects from the experience of citizen women in Athens, including prominent roles in propertyholding and household management in certain periods. Yet they did not participate as equal voters in the formal citizen assembly. Modern popular descriptions of Sparta frequently swing between romantic admiration for toughness and unqualified caricature. Neither approach explains the institutions and the population that sustained them.
