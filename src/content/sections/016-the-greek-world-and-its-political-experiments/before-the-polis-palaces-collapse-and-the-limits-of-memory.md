Greek history did not begin with democratic debates. On Crete, the Bronze Age Minoan palatial cultures developed impressive complexes and maritime connections. On the mainland, Mycenaean rulers occupied fortified centers such as Mycenae, Tiryns and Pylos. Their administrations used an early form of Greek written in <strong>Linear B</strong> script, principally to record goods, workers, and obligations. Linear B gives us evidence for palace management rather than the poetry and philosophy of later Greek city-states.

Around the end of the second millennium BCE, Mycenaean palatial institutions collapsed amid a wider eastern Mediterranean crisis. The causes remain debated and probably varied from place to place: warfare, political disruption, changing exchange networks, environmental pressures and internal difficulties are all investigated. The disappearance of palaces did not mean the disappearance of Greek-speaking people. Settlements changed; political centers shifted; some forms of craft and communication survived while others contracted. We should avoid imagining a uniform, empty 'Dark Age.' Historians increasingly recognize regional variety and gradual transformation between roughly 1100 and 800 BCE.

Centuries later, the poems associated with Homer described heroic aristocrats, great battles and long journeys. Their stories preserve layers of cultural memory, but the <em>Iliad</em> is not a reliable battle report of a specific Trojan War and the <em>Odyssey</em> is not a travel diary. The poems were composed and transmitted in oral traditions that developed across generations before their familiar written forms. Archaeology can test features of the world they evoke; it cannot convert every speech of Achilles into historical testimony. Historical time matters: the legendary heroes, Bronze Age palaces and Archaic poets belong to overlapping but distinct evidentiary worlds.
