In <strong>399 BCE</strong>, an Athenian jury convicted Socrates on charges commonly summarized as impiety and corrupting the young. He was sentenced to death and executed by drinking hemlock. Plato and Xenophon preserve influential accounts of his defense, but their narratives were written by admirers with different aims. We know the broad outcome, yet the full motives of jurors and the exact words of the courtroom remain inaccessible.

The trial occurred not long after Athens' military defeat, oligarchic terror and democratic restoration. Some individuals associated with Socrates had been involved in the preceding political crisis, though the trial was not simply a transparent prosecution for political conspiracy. His public style of questioning could make powerful people uncomfortable; charges about religion and education had force within the legal culture of Athens. Scholars still debate how the immediate accusations and broader political atmosphere interacted.

It is tempting to reduce the event to one tidy slogan: 'democracy killed its greatest philosopher.' That makes a memorable moral but weak history. A jury of citizens did condemn a critic, and the case raises serious questions about tolerance and freedom of inquiry. But the political and religious context matters, as do the literary character of the surviving accounts and the distinction between a society's ideals and its practices. Good historical judgment can hold the contradiction without hiding it.
