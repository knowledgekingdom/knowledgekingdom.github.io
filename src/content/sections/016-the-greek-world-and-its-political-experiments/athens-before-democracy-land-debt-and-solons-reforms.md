Athens did not begin as a democracy. It was one polis among many, governed through powerful families and offices that shifted over time. In the early sixth century BCE, deep tensions over debt, land and status helped bring the lawgiver <strong>Solon</strong> to prominence. Later tradition credited him with measures that relieved debt burdens and forbade certain forms of debt bondage of Athenian citizens, while establishing or adjusting political participation according to property classifications. We possess poetry attributed to Solon and later accounts of his legislation, but not a complete contemporaneous constitutional archive.

Solon's reforms did not make all adult residents equal political citizens. Wealth still mattered, and households were structured by gender, status and slavery. Nonetheless, they offered an important response to a familiar historical danger: an elite political order can become unstable if debt allows powerful creditors to dominate and dispossess fellow community members. Solon sought a settlement between competing groups, not a blueprint for modern representative democracy.

Later Athens experienced the rule of Peisistratos and his sons, commonly called the <strong>Peisistratid tyranny</strong>. The regime sponsored public works and religious festivals; it also concentrated political authority. The fall of the dynasty, especially after the expulsion of Hippias in 510 BCE, reopened struggles over who should govern. Greek political experimentation was not a straight road from monarchy to liberty. It involved reversals, alliances, institutional inventions and conflicts over who counted as part of the political community.
