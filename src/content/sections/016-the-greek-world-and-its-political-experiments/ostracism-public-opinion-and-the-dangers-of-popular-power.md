Athenian institutions offered several ways to scrutinize politicians and prevent the concentration of personal power. One famous procedure was <strong>ostracism</strong>: in certain circumstances, a citizen might be required to leave Athens for a period, traditionally ten years, without the process being a criminal trial in the usual sense. Ostracism could be used against a powerful figure considered dangerous to the community, but it was also vulnerable to factional conflict. Its existence demonstrates that Greek citizens worried about tyranny even while distrusting powerful political rivals.

Citizen government did not ensure wise decisions. The trial of the generals after the naval battle of Arginusae in 406 BCE is a warning against reducing legality to the passion of a public meeting. The generals faced collective proceedings over their failure to rescue shipwrecked sailors and recover the dead. Ancient accounts emphasize the controversial and apparently unlawful collective trial and the pressures of anger and grief. The incident invites a distinction between <strong>majority decision</strong> and <strong>rule-governed fairness</strong>. A majority may support a punishment while violating rules intended to protect each accused person.

Nor should we treat political accountability as a purely Athenian achievement. Different Greek communities experimented with councils, customary constraints and collective decision-making. Athens is exceptionally well documented and particularly influential in modern political language; it was not the only place where Greeks asked who should govern.
