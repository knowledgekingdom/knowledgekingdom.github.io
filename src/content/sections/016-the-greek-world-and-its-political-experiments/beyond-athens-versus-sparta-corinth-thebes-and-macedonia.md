The fourth century BCE was not merely the aftermath of Athens' decline. Sparta's postwar supremacy generated resistance. The <strong>Corinthian War</strong> (395–387/386 BCE) drew several major Greek states into new combinations of conflict and Persian diplomacy. The king's peace associated with 387/386 BCE showed that the Persian Empire retained influence over Greek political affairs despite its earlier military setbacks. It is an error to imagine Greek city-states steadily displacing Persia after 479 BCE.

<strong>Thebes</strong> rose as an important land power and defeated Sparta at <strong>Leuctra in 371 BCE</strong>, undermining Spartan dominance and contributing to the erosion of its control over Messenia. Rival alliances and leadership struggles continued. Political experimentation did not occur in a peaceful laboratory. Each polis adapted under external threats, economic pressure, internal division and military competition.

To the north, <strong>Macedonia</strong> under Philip II developed formidable political and military power and defeated a Greek coalition at <strong>Chaeronea in 338 BCE</strong>. Philip's son Alexander later led the conquest of the Achaemenid Empire, which we encountered at the end of Chapter 15. Macedonia's rise did not erase Greek cultural life; instead it changed the scale and political setting of Greek-speaking communities. A later chapter will explore the Hellenistic world, where Greek institutions, art, language and scholarship spread and transformed within larger kingdoms.
