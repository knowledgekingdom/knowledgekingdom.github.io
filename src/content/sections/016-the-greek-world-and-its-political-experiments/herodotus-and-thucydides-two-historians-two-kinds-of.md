The Greeks also developed influential traditions of historical inquiry. <strong>Herodotus</strong>, writing about the conflicts between Greeks and Persians, investigated customs, origins and remarkable stories across a wide geographical area. His narrative includes competing versions and explicit uncertainty alongside episodes that modern historians doubt. To call him 'the father of history' is a conventional tribute, not permission to accept every tale as a documented fact. His interest in foreign societies is essential to the work, and his sources reflect the limitations of travel, translation and hearsay.

<strong>Thucydides</strong>, writing about the Peloponnesian War, analyzed motives, alliances, resources, fear and political decisions with particular intensity. His account is an indispensable source but not an unbiased photograph of the past. He was an Athenian participant, an exiled general and a literary craftsman. As he explained in his methodological remarks, his speeches were shaped to express what was called for in particular situations and what he understood of their broad content. Historians therefore distinguish Thucydides' analytical reconstruction from independently verified wording.

Comparing the two teaches a lesson already developed in Chapter 4: a valuable source can still be selective, political and literary. Herodotus may preserve a useful account in an entertaining story; Thucydides may clarify an alliance's logic through an artfully composed debate. Neither author should be treated merely as a modern news reporter. Their methods differ, and our use of them should differ accordingly.
