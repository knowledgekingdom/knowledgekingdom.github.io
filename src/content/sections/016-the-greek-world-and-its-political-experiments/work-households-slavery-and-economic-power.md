Ancient Greek life consisted of much more than public speeches. Farmers planted cereals, olives and vines; herders kept animals; craftspeople made pottery, metal goods and textiles; merchants exchanged goods over considerable distances; households managed property, inheritance, marriage and labor. The pattern differed among cities and periods. The Athenian port of Piraeus connected the city to grain supplies, luxury commodities and overseas traders. Maritime commerce was a source of prosperity but also exposed Athens to blockades, storms and war.

<strong>Enslaved labor</strong> was central to many Greek economies, including Athens. Enslaved people worked in households, workshops and mines, and some carried out specialized or managerial tasks. That variety must not soften the basic fact of unfreedom: the legal capacity to control another person's labor and movements remained deeply unequal. The dangerous labor of the Laurion silver mines contributed to the economy supporting Athenian naval strength. Sparta's helot system took a different form, but it too depended on coerced labor. Greek political thought often accepted hierarchies that modern readers rightly question.

Women's experiences varied with wealth, location and household arrangements. Athenian citizen women could exercise religious roles and manage aspects of domestic economy, but their legal and civic agency was restricted relative to that of adult male citizens. Spartan women could possess considerable property and had distinctive social roles, though political decision-making remained male dominated. Sources authored primarily by elite men often describe ideals of how women <em>ought</em> to behave, rather than comprehensive accounts of how all women lived. Every reconstruction requires attention to the difference between prescription and practice.
