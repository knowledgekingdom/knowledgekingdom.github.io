Greek religion was woven through government, households, warfare and festivals. Cities honored gods through temples, sacrifices, processions and competitions, and the divine world offered an imaginative language for justice, jealousy, conflict and obligation. Religious life was not uniform across the Greek world, nor was there a single authorized sacred book comparable to those in some later religious traditions. Local cults and festivals could be deeply important to civic identity.

At Athens, public dramatic festivals produced tragedies and comedies that asked difficult political and ethical questions. In a tragedy, a ruler may defend an order while another character appeals to unwritten obligations; a hero's pursuit of honor may destroy a family. A comedy can ridicule politicians and expose public foolishness, though it too reflects the biases of its author and audience. Plays by Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides and Aristophanes remain invaluable windows into concerns of their periods, but a playwright's dramatic voice is not a public opinion poll.

Art was likewise political without being a straightforward government poster. Sculptures of idealized bodies, funerary monuments and painted pottery can show social values, trade connections and skills, but not the complete attitudes of a society. Archaeologists compare objects with contexts, dates, inscriptions and patterns of use. The surviving marble buildings and literary masterpieces come disproportionately from affluent, successful or well-preserved communities. What survives can make Athens appear more central and prosperous than the many Greek lives less well documented.
