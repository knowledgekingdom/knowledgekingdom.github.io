Suppose a hypothetical region's population falls from 500,000 to 100,000 over several generations. That is an 80 percent fall. It does not mean historians have counted each death or know a single cause. Births, deaths, migration, boundary changes and the reliability of both counts must be examined. If one estimate comes from a tax register, households outside the state's control may have been excluded.

Now suppose a paper estimates population loss across the entire Americas by combining many such regional reconstructions. It may be a valuable synthesis, but uncertainties do not magically disappear when the results are combined. Researchers must ask whether the underlying records share biases, whether missing regions are representative and how sensitive the total is to changing assumptions.

This exercise echoes Chapter 6 on statistics: precision in the printed result does not guarantee precision in the evidence. A range stated with careful explanation is often more informative than an exact-sounding number without context.
