The question can be answered only by specifying the people, timescale and outcome. For many Indigenous communities, the first centuries of sustained contact brought devastation through disease, dispossession and coercion. For some people in Afro-Eurasia, newly available crops eventually improved nutrition and farming opportunities. Merchants and colonial elites could become wealthy through trade and plantations even when workers suffered. A single moral total cannot capture all the experiences.

We should also distinguish an outcome from its justification. Later global benefits of potatoes or maize do not justify conquest or enslaving human beings. Conversely, condemning colonial violence does not require denying that crop movement changed the possibilities of agriculture and diet. Historical understanding becomes stronger, not weaker, when it can hold these claims together.

The most useful ethical question is: <em>Who gained the power to decide, who bore the risk and who received the benefits?</em> Asking it transforms a narrative of objects crossing oceans into one about people and unequal institutions.
