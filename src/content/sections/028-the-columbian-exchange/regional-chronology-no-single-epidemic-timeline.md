Caribbean islands were among the earliest places to experience sustained Iberian settlement and devastating population decline. Central Mexico and the Andes encountered different combinations of epidemics, conquest, political division and forced labor in the sixteenth century. Northern North America, the Amazon, Patagonia and California cannot be pressed into the same exact chronology. Some places faced successive outbreaks after centuries of changing contact patterns.

Bioarchaeological research in California, for example, has investigated whether the earliest purported continent-wide epidemic models match local evidence. Such studies help distinguish dramatic demographic events that occurred after direct colonial settlement from earlier declines linked to different causes. In a field with uneven evidence, a regional correction is not a denial of the broader colonial catastrophe. \[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

Historians should also be careful with the names of diseases in early chronicles. Words for fever, rash or plague may not correspond to modern laboratory diagnoses. Ancient DNA, where recoverable and ethically studied, can sometimes clarify the story, but molecular evidence remains limited and must be interpreted alongside archaeology and written records.
