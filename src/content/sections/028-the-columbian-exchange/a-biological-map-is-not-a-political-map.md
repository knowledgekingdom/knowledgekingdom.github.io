The outline of a colonial possession shows a claim or administration, not the actual boundary of a pathogen or plant. Smallpox could spread through human contact beyond a fort; horses might be adopted by Indigenous communities far from direct European rule; maize could move from a coast into an interior landscape through intermediaries. Organisms followed trade, marriage, migration and conflict as well as official imperial routes.

Those pathways were uneven. A coastal port could experience new crops decades before an inland valley. A highland climate might welcome potatoes while a tropical lowland did not. A newly introduced pig might reproduce successfully on an island yet fail elsewhere. Historians reconstruct these movements from letters, botanical descriptions, archaeology, genetic evidence, farm records and linguistic changes. Each source is partial; absence in one record does not prove absence from a whole region.

One lesson underlies the entire chapter: imperial expansion created networks, but local environments and Indigenous or African decisions shaped what the networks actually carried and how the consequences unfolded. \[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]
