The claim that Europeans conquered the Americas 'because of germs' contains part of a real explanation but becomes misleading if used as the whole story. Weapons, Indigenous rivalries and alliances, politics, navigation, supply lines, local geography, diplomacy and labor demands all shaped conquest. Epidemics could destabilize societies, but that fact did not force conquerors to seize land or impose coercive institutions.

Nor did pathogens only travel from one hemisphere to another in one direction. Some diseases' origins and pre-contact histories are complicated. Genetic research has demonstrated that tuberculosis-related disease existed in parts of pre-contact South America, undermining simple claims that all such infections arrived only after Europeans. The origin and spread of venereal syphilis remain areas of active scientific investigation; recent ancient-genome research strengthens a possible American ancestry of some treponemal lineages while preserving uncertainty about exact histories and diagnostic categories. \[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]\[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]

A sound explanation separates evidence for the existence of an infection, its historical route and its social effects. Three questions may demand three different sources. This approach is consistent with Chapter 1's central principle: use confidence proportionate to evidence.
