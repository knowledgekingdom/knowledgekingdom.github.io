Cacao was cultivated and consumed in Mesoamerica before Spanish expansion. European consumers eventually reshaped cacao into forms of chocolate that differed in preparation, sweetness and social setting from many earlier drinks. Sugar, another plant with an older Afro-Eurasian history, became deeply entangled with chocolate's commercial success. An American product and an Old World crop together underlay a new luxury and, later, a mass market.

Tobacco offers another trajectory. Indigenous American societies used tobacco in diverse ritual, medicinal and social settings; European adopters frequently detached it from those original contexts. Tobacco cultivation and consumption expanded across the globe, eventually creating fiscal revenues, commercial fortunes and major health burdens. The word <em>exchange</em> here includes the spread of habits and demand, not only living plants.

Neither cacao nor tobacco can be understood solely through the excitement of novelty. Plantation labor, imperial monopolies, marketing and changing social norms shaped their growth. Historical accounts should distinguish the existence of a market from whether everyone involved benefited from it. \[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]
