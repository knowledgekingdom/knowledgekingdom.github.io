American crops eventually reshaped diets in places whose rulers had not participated in the earliest Atlantic voyages. Maize and sweet potatoes spread into China; peppers entered cuisines across Asia; cassava became important in parts of Africa; potatoes supported farming in several regions of Europe and Asia. Their adoption helped link American agricultural achievements to demographic and culinary histories far from the Atlantic coast.

The routes were often indirect. A crop could cross the Atlantic to Iberia, circulate through Mediterranean or African ports and move via Indian Ocean shipping. Traders and migrants could carry seeds along paths ignored by official chronicles. Maritime empires established some pathways, while local merchants and farmers provided crucial links. In the same way that Silk Roads history cannot be reduced to one empire, the spread of American crops cannot be attributed solely to a few explorers.

A necessary qualification: growing populations have multiple causes, including changes in peace and warfare, institutions, mortality, technology and climate.
