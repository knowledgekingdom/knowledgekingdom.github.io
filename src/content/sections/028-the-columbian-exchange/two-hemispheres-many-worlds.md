It is tempting to divide the past into an American world and a single Old World. That shorthand conceals complexity. The Americas contained tropical forests, Arctic coasts, dry plateaus, Andean highlands, vast grasslands and intensively cultivated valleys. Afro-Eurasia connected Mediterranean cities, savannas, the Indian Ocean, Central Asian steppes, Chinese agricultural regions and thousands of other environments. Every region possessed distinct diets, diseases, domesticated species and political institutions.

The pre-contact Americas were not empty wilderness. Indigenous peoples bred crops, built cities and waterways, managed forests and wetlands, burned landscapes in controlled ways and developed detailed ecological knowledge. In Africa and Asia, similarly, farming systems reflected long histories of adaptation. A seed did not travel into an ecological blank slate; it arrived among other species and among people with practical and social reasons to cultivate or reject it.

The language of origin should therefore distinguish a species' ancestral range, the region of domestication and the place where a particular variety was developed. Sweet potato, maize and the ordinary potato all have American histories, but different domestication centers and trajectories. Rice is also not one homogeneous story: African rice and Asian rice were separately domesticated. Crop identities are human histories as well as botanical labels. \[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]
