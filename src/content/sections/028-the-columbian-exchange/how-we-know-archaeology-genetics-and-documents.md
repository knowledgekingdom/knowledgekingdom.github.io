Written sources are uneven. European chroniclers often recorded royal voyages and elite concerns while neglecting Indigenous or enslaved people's perspectives. Some Indigenous-language accounts, pictorial records and oral traditions preserve different experiences, though surviving materials have their own dates, contexts and interpretive challenges. Colonial tax registers may document the number of people a government could tax, not the full population.

Archaeologists can study changes in settlements, diet, pottery, plants and animal remains. Pollen and botanical remains can reveal land-use change. Ancient DNA can identify particular pathogens in some preserved remains, but sampling and preservation are limited and ethical protocols are essential. Modern crop genetics can suggest ancestral origins and movements, although it rarely provides an exact itinerary for one seed carried by one trader.

These lines of evidence are most convincing when compared. A chronicle describing a devastating fever may be corroborated by demographic and archaeological change; it should not automatically be assigned a modern diagnosis. Historical reasoning, introduced in Chapters 1 and 4, is indispensable in an apparently biological topic.
