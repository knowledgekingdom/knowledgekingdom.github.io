A crop that offers high yields can become dangerous when social systems make it the only affordable food. Imagine a hypothetical community that replaces most diverse crops with one potato variety. In a normal year it harvests ample calories, rents land and exports other produce. A disease then destroys that single crop. If poor families lack savings, land rights or political protection, the famine risk is dramatically greater than yield figures alone suggest.

Compare a neighboring community that maintains several plant varieties, reserves grain, has fair access to food and can buy from other regions. It might experience the same blight but avoid a catastrophe. The difference is institutional as well as biological. Crop introductions affect the range of choices, while markets and governments influence who can afford those choices.

This is a bridge to the economics section of Knowledge Kingdom: productivity, inequality, insurance and food access must be understood together. High output is not the same as shared security, and a global crop's success can coexist with local hunger.
