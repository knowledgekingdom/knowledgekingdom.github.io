Cassava, domesticated in tropical South America, is now a staple in many parts of Africa. The plant can tolerate some difficult soils and variable rainfall, making it attractive in particular farming systems. But its usefulness depends on knowledge: varieties contain compounds that can release cyanide, and some require careful processing before eating. The technology of a food is not just the plant; it includes inherited skills for preparation, safety and storage.

Sweet potatoes moved through tropical and subtropical regions and often performed well in small plots. Their history also complicates simplistic accounts of total isolation before 1492. Botanical, linguistic and genetic research supports pre-Columbian movement of sweet potato from South America into parts of Polynesia, though scholars still study the precise routes and timing. The Atlantic exchange after 1492 dramatically increased global circulation without implying that all earlier oceanic contact was impossible. \[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]

Cassava and sweet potato mattered not only to households but to states, plantations and armies because they could feed people in altered settlements and landscapes. Some uses were voluntary innovations; others developed under forced migrations and the coercive demands of colonial economies. An account focused solely on crop yields would overlook whose labor made those harvests possible and who controlled the food.
