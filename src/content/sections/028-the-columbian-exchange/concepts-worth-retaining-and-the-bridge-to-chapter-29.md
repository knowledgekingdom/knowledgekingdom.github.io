Remember six connected ideas. First, the Columbian Exchange was a sustained transfer of organisms and people, not merely a list of crops. Second, Indigenous American farmers shaped much of the agricultural knowledge that later spread globally. Third, disease created one of history's greatest demographic catastrophes, interacting with coercion and social disruption. Fourth, the effects of introduced species varied with local ecology and political institutions. Fifth, crop diffusion transformed agriculture, diet and commerce across Africa, Asia and Europe as well as the Americas. Sixth, the evidence is rich but uneven, especially for precise death totals and some disease histories.

The exchange is a turning point in the making of a connected planet, but connection has never been automatically just. Ships could transport a lifesaving food or an epidemic; plantations could produce valuable commodities while destroying human freedom. To understand the darkest institutions that developed within these Atlantic networks, our next chapter focuses on \*<em>Atlantic Slavery and Plantation Economies</em>\*.
