Port cities became clearinghouses not only for silver, textiles and sugar but for seeds, animals and food habits. A ship arriving from Veracruz, Havana or Salvador might carry cargo, ballast, foodstuffs, plants or stowaway organisms. Travelers could be infected before showing symptoms. New diets emerged around port markets where ingredients from several continents crossed paths.

Urban change was uneven. Some colonial settlements imported European food preferences and attempted to reproduce familiar agricultural arrangements. Others became places where Indigenous, African and European practices mixed under conditions of unequal power. The resulting foods, markets and languages were new cultural formations, not simple copies of one homeland.

A botanical diagram showing an arrow from 'America' to 'Europe' compresses all this into one line. Behind it lie sailors, markets, household gardens, migrant workers, Indigenous traders, empire-wide provisioning and generations of experimentation. The arrow describes a pathway, not a complete explanation.
