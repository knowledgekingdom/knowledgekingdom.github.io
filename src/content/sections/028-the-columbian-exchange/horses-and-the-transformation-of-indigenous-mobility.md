Horses, domesticated in Eurasia, reached the Americas with European colonization. They later spread by trade, capture, exchange and breeding beyond direct colonial administration. Some Indigenous nations adopted them in ways that dramatically affected travel, hunting, diplomacy, warfare and social organization. Horses became embedded in Indigenous cultural life rather than remaining merely 'European tools.'

Chronology matters. Images of horses on the Great Plains may seem timeless to modern audiences, but many equestrian traditions expanded in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Their development differed from one nation to another. People trained horses, managed grazing, built gear and reworked social customs in relation to new opportunities and pressures. This history demonstrates agency: foreign biological introductions did not mechanically determine social outcomes.

The spread of horses also brought costs. Grazing changed landscapes; competition over animals and pasture could intensify; colonial powers used mounted forces and sought to control Indigenous mobility. An organism could expand choices for some communities while supporting dispossession by others.
