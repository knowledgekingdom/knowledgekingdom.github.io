Seeds do not travel only when someone intends to plant them. Grain sacks, clothing, soil, ships and animals can carry weeds, insects and microorganisms. Some newcomers became agricultural pests or changed ecosystems. Others failed to spread or became useful parts of managed environments. The effects depended on whether species found suitable conditions and how people responded.

Historians use the concept of <em>ecological imperialism</em> to examine how organisms and human settlement reinforced one another. Introduced cattle, crops, grasses and pathogens sometimes made landscapes more familiar or profitable to colonists and less compatible with earlier Indigenous land use. Yet newer scholarship urges caution against treating this as a uniform process. Local environments were diverse, and Indigenous and colonial communities made many competing decisions. \[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]\[<a href="/028-the-columbian-exchange/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]

The language of an 'invasive species' is often useful in present-day ecology, but historical uses need nuance. A plant's arrival and its later harmful effects should be demonstrated rather than assumed; legal land seizures must not be disguised as a purely biological phenomenon.
