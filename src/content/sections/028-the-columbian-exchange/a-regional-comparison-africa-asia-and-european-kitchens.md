Africa offers clear examples of crops adapting to established agricultural systems. Maize, cassava, peanuts and peppers entered some regions via maritime routes and later inland circulation. Local farmers tested their performance against traditional crops and adapted processing methods. No single date marks the moment 'Africa adopted maize' or 'Africa adopted cassava.' Historical patterns depended on trade routes, ecology, states and households.

In Asia, sweet potato and maize could provide food on land less suited to some existing cereals, while chili peppers entered kitchens with longstanding culinary traditions. European consumers took to potatoes and tomatoes at different speeds. This is why global crop movements should be told as a patchwork of regional histories, not a smooth diffusion from a point of origin.

What looks like a simple modern fact—tomatoes in pasta sauce, potatoes in stew, chiles in sauces across the world—is evidence of creative adaptation over centuries. Those dishes are records of contact, migration and social choice.
