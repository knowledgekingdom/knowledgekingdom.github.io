Ancient Egyptian women could own, inherit, buy and sell property and make legal arrangements in their own names in ways that varied by historical period and social context. The Metropolitan Museum's study of Hatnefer, a New Kingdom household figure, offers an accessible window into marriage, home life, textiles, cosmetics and property rights. That legal capacity matters, but we should not confuse legal possibility with equal social power for all women or with modern equality. \[<a href="/012-ancient-egypt-and-the-nile/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

A wealthy woman connected to court society might have access to land, servants, writing and kinship networks unavailable to a poor rural woman. Gender intersected with status, geography, age, family relations and time. Male dominance in many offices did not make women historically invisible, but archaeological survival does not distribute attention fairly. Some women held important religious offices; a small number became sovereigns. Neither fact describes the everyday agency of a typical household member.

Consider marriage and property as institutional rather than only romantic subjects. Who owned an asset when a couple formed a household? Could a person inherit it? How did obligations pass between generations? What happened in a dispute? These questions connect the microhistory of families to the macrohistory of states, because officials, witnesses, contracts and inheritance practices depend on wider social rules.

Egyptian written evidence can make some women unusually vivid, while leaving others nearly silent. The ethical obligation of a historian is not to invent speeches for the missing people. It is to explain the survival bias and compare legal, archaeological and artistic sources without pretending every person's experience is recoverable.
