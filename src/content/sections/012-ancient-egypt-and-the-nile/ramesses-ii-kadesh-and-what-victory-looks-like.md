Ramesses II ruled during Dynasty 19 and became one of ancient Egypt's most visible monarchs through extensive building and inscriptions. His reign included major conflict with the Hittite empire, particularly around Kadesh in Syria, and a later treaty and diplomatic relationship. Egyptian monumental accounts emphasize royal bravery and victory. The battle's full outcome and strategic meaning require considering both Egyptian sources and the wider diplomatic context. A military encounter can be portrayed as a triumphant personal achievement even when it fails to produce decisive territorial change. \[<a href="/012-ancient-egypt-and-the-nile/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]\[<a href="/012-ancient-egypt-and-the-nile/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]

This is an ideal opportunity to distinguish three propositions. First: Egypt and Hatti fought over influence in the Levant. This is strongly supported. Second: Ramesses II publicly represented his actions at Kadesh as glorious and divinely supported. That is visible in royal inscriptions. Third: every claim about enemies destroyed, decisions made or territory permanently secured is literally accurate. That proposition requires careful independent evaluation and cannot be established from the king's monuments alone.

The later peace treaty associated with Ramesses II and Hattusili III is historically significant because versions of its diplomatic provisions survive in different ancient traditions. Treaties can aim to stabilize relations even after years of rivalry. Peace is rarely the moment one side suddenly becomes morally enlightened; it can emerge when costs, threats and bargaining positions change. This is the same international-relations logic introduced in Chapter 7, now visible in a Bronze Age setting.

Ramesses II also constructed and enlarged temples that presented royal power through grand scale, sacred imagery and repeated inscriptions. Such buildings were more than propaganda billboards; they also supported cult, festivals, economic activities and institutional continuity. One site can be religious, administrative, artistic and political at once. We should not force it into only one category.
