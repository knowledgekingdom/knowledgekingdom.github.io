A familiar argument says that a government facing economic failure or protest may attack abroad to distract the public. This is called a diversionary-war hypothesis. It is plausible in some circumstances, but notoriously difficult to demonstrate. Leaders who face domestic trouble may lack the resources to fight; an unsuccessful war may accelerate their downfall. Sometimes politicians invoke an external enemy without actually launching combat operations. Evidence of falling popularity followed by war is not enough to show that diversion was the decisive motive. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]

To test the claim, historians need internal records, communications, documented preferences and an account of why other available policies were rejected. They must also consider selection effects: perhaps leaders choose confrontation because they think victory is likely, not because domestic dissent is uniquely severe. This chapter's broader rule applies: a mechanism may be logically possible without being established in a particular case. Political analysis becomes weaker when every war is explained by the same clever-sounding motive.
