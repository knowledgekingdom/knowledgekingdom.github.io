Trade can raise the opportunity cost of war. Businesses lose customers, borrowers face default, investment collapses and governments risk tax revenue. Economic ties also create relationships among citizens who favor continuity. This is one reason scholars explore whether commercially connected states are less likely to fight. Yet trade dependence can also generate fear: a state worried about embargoes, blockades or access to strategic supplies might use force to secure routes it considers vital. Whether economic links restrain or inflame conflict depends on expectations about future trade and the political importance of those goods. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]

A common mistake is to say that two states 'could not possibly fight' because their merchants depend on each other. Europe was economically intertwined before 1914, yet war still occurred. The existence of trade does not identify the beliefs and political incentives of leaders during a crisis. Conversely, wars may emerge between economies with very little direct trade. A more precise question is whether the decision-makers expected future exchange to remain reliable and whether they valued the benefits of peace more than the disputed political objective.
