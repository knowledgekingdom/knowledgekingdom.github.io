These two ideas sound similar but refer to different fears. A <em>preventive</em> war is initiated to counter a projected future deterioration in the balance of power: the attacker believes it may be weaker if it waits. A <em>preemptive</em> attack is launched because a threat is believed to be imminent and striking first seems advantageous. Governments may describe a campaign as preemptive to suggest immediate necessity even when evidence for an impending assault is thin. The distinction concerns the reasoning and timeline, not whether a particular use of force is lawful or justified.

Consider two hypothetical cases. A government strikes a neighbor's industries because it expects that neighbor to be much stronger in a decade: preventive logic. A government attacks an opposing air force because it believes that air force is hours away from launching raids: preemptive logic. Both motives can be exaggerated, manipulated or mistaken. International law applies its own rules about the prohibition on force and self-defense, which should not be confused with analytical categories describing why decision-makers believed war advantageous. Chapter 46 will address those legal rules in detail.
