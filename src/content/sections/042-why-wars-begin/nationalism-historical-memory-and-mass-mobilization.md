Nationalism can inspire a population to defend itself from conquest and can also support expansion against neighbors. The effect depends on whether national identity is presented as a claim to equal self-government, a story of permanent victimhood, a mandate to annex territories, or a demand for exclusion. Schools, maps, monuments, religious institutions and media shape what people believe belongs to their community. Where populations are mixed, incompatible visions of national territory can make compromise deeply contentious.

History does not act on people without mediation. A battle fought centuries earlier cannot mechanically cause a modern war; contemporary leaders and organizations choose which episodes to highlight and how to interpret them. National historical narratives can sustain reconciliation or build resentment. In the Balkans, for example, overlapping claims about Macedonia and Thrace mattered because governments, armies and movements linked memory to borders, education and security. The historian must distinguish a past grievance from the political process that made it operational in the present.
