A diplomatic threat communicates what a government says it will do if its demands are ignored. The receiving state must decide whether the threat is credible. Cheap messages are easy to imitate: a leader can sound determined without being willing to incur the costs of battle. Some actions, such as mobilization, visible deployments or accepting domestic political risk, may be more informative because they are costly. But they are also dangerous. An action intended as a warning can look like preparation for attack, creating exactly the crisis it was supposed to prevent.

The signals most useful for bargaining often combine clarity with restraint. Arms-control inspections, negotiated withdrawals, mutually observed exercises, channels between commanders and carefully designed guarantees can provide information without demanding immediate surrender. Yet even seemingly strong evidence may be disputed. A leader might mobilize partly to impress voters, or retreat temporarily while preparing a new offensive. This creates a problem of interpretation: signalling is not a universal language, and the same action can carry very different meanings to different audiences. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]
