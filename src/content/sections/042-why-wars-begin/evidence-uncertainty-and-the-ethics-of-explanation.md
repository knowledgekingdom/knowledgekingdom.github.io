Causes of war are especially vulnerable to retrospective storytelling. Governments issue public justifications that may conceal private motives; archives survive unevenly; commanders shape memoirs to protect their reputations; victims' accounts can be marginalized; later nationalist narratives compress complex decisions into simple tales of betrayal or heroism. Quantitative conflict datasets reveal broad patterns, but their categories and thresholds require careful reading. Case studies illuminate decisions in context, yet one episode cannot automatically establish a universal law. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-22" class="cite">22</a>\]

Explaining why a government launched a war is not excusing its actions. A historian can investigate a leader's security fears and still find their invasion aggressive, illegal or unjustified. Similarly, understanding how propaganda mobilized a population does not mean the victims were responsible for the violence inflicted upon them. A balanced account is not one that distributes blame equally irrespective of evidence. It is one that compares claims fairly, applies consistent standards, identifies uncertainty, and reaches conclusions proportional to what the records support.
