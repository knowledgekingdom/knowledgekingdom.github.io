Political violence is often described using broad ethnic or religious labels, as if entire communities acted with a single mind. In reality, civilians may be divided over participation, and armed factions frequently use intimidation to claim authority over those they say they represent. Rumors of imminent massacres, local histories of discrimination, discriminatory state policies and attacks by militias can generate self-reinforcing fear. A family may flee not because it seeks conflict but because it no longer trusts that police or neighbors can keep it safe.

Ethnic identity can become central to a conflict without being its original or sufficient cause. Political entrepreneurs can turn ordinary differences into claims that coexistence is impossible, while peace activists, religious leaders and local institutions may work to prevent escalation. The danger of essentialist explanations is moral as well as analytical: describing a war as an outbreak of 'ancient hatreds' overlooks documented policy choices and the individuals who organize killing. Cases such as the Yugoslav wars require attention to state collapse, propaganda, organized force and civilian resistance, not myths of timeless hostility.
