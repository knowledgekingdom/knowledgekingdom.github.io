Territorial disputes are common in the history of interstate conflict because territory can combine economic value, military position, population and national meaning. A small strip of coastline may control a port; an island may affect access to fishing grounds or shipping routes; a mountain frontier may be difficult to defend. These material concerns interact with public stories about historical ownership. A government may claim that a disputed region is indispensable, although other locations or cooperative arrangements could serve some of the same practical purposes.

Natural resources can raise the value of control over land, but the simple claim that wars are 'really about oil' or 'really about water' often fails to account for the full evidence. Resource dependence sometimes encourages trade, treaties and joint development instead of conquest. To demonstrate a resource cause, historians need evidence that officials identified the resource as a motive and that it affected decisions relative to alternatives. Distinguish the origin of a war from the sources of money that allow it to continue. Mineral wealth may finance combatants after fighting starts without explaining why the dispute first turned violent.
