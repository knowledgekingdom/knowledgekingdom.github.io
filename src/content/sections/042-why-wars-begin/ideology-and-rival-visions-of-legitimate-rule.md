Governments and movements sometimes fight over the political order itself rather than a divisible territory. Revolutionary groups may seek to replace monarchies or class systems. Regimes may regard neighboring ideological models as contagious threats. Religious organizations can frame struggles in sacred terms, and states may mobilize people with competing claims about liberty, equality or imperial civilization. Yet ideology rarely provides a complete causal explanation without attention to military opportunity, resources, institutions and political coalitions.

A leader's stated ideology can serve several roles: genuine commitment, public justification, recruitment tool and means of disciplining supporters. The Soviet Union and the United States competed over political and economic systems during the Cold War, but their interventions also reflected security interests, local allies and regional conditions. Avoid reducing every struggle to ideology or denying that ideological beliefs can matter profoundly. Read speeches against private correspondence, behavior and the costs leaders were willing to accept for their principles.
