Some disputes concern objects that seem impossible to divide: a sacred site, a capital, the status of a community, or sovereignty over a small territory. Dividing a shrine into pieces would not preserve its meaning; swapping symbolic recognition for money may insult the parties. But an issue's symbolic importance does not mean no institutional compromise can exist. Shared administration, limited autonomy, access guarantees, layered jurisdictions, neutral oversight and phased arrangements sometimes make a previously binary issue more negotiable. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

Indivisibility is partly material and partly created by political framing. A border may become a symbol of past suffering, and leaders who make public pledges of 'not one inch' can reduce their freedom to bargain. Groups may also disagree internally on what compromise means, making an agreement between national leaders insufficient. Analysts should ask which specific feature is truly indivisible and whether the claim is widely shared or promoted by a smaller political constituency. Calling an issue sacred should prompt careful investigation, not the automatic conclusion that war could not have been avoided.
