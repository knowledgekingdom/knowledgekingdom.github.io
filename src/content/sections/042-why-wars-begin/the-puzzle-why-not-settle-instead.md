War destroys resources that the winners and losers might otherwise divide. Soldiers are killed, bridges ruined, trade interrupted, and civilian life unsettled. If both sides knew what a war would achieve and could agree on a distribution giving each at least as much as it expected from fighting, a negotiated settlement would often be cheaper. This is the bargaining puzzle developed in influential international-relations scholarship: why do governments sometimes fail to reach an agreement that appears preferable to war? \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

The puzzle does not assume that leaders are kind or states equally powerful. A government may seek annexation or regime change, and some political objectives may be profoundly unjust. It asks a narrower explanatory question: what blocks a bargain when physical destruction creates a potential surplus for peace? Three frequently discussed obstacles are uncertainty coupled with incentives to misrepresent, inability to make credible commitments, and disputes believed difficult to divide. Domestic incentives, ideology, emotions and institutional failures can intensify these obstacles. The presence of an imaginable compromise does not mean either side considers it safe, legal, legitimate or politically survivable.
