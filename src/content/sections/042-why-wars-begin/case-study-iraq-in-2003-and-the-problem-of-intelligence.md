The U.S.-led invasion of Iraq in March 2003 was justified publicly through arguments about weapons of mass destruction, security, enforcement of Security Council resolutions and the danger posed by Saddam Hussein's regime. Subsequent investigations found major defects in key intelligence assessments. The United Kingdom's Iraq Inquiry concluded that judgments about the severity of the alleged weapons threat were presented with unjustified certainty, and that peaceful options had not been exhausted before military action. The fact that a regime was repressive did not establish the accuracy of the particular intelligence claims used to advocate invasion. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

The war also revealed the gap between defeating an army and constructing stable political order afterward. The fall of Saddam Hussein was rapid; the ensuing occupation, armed resistance, sectarian violence and state-building crises were prolonged and destructive. The lesson is not that intelligence errors explain all motives—leaders and officials held multiple political objectives—but that public claims should be tested against documents, alternative assessments and outcomes. When evaluating a war, separate the decision to invade, the conduct of combat, and the governance choices that followed.
