Political leaders are people, not calculators. Fear can make compromise feel like helplessness. Humiliation after defeat may fuel demands for reversal. Pride and honor can make public retreat costly, especially when national status appears at stake. Anger after a massacre can mobilize resistance that no economic model alone captures. These emotions operate within institutions and narratives; they are not simply irrational interruptions to otherwise perfect strategic reasoning.

A group can regard symbolic recognition, mourning or the return of displaced people as essential conditions of peace. Negotiators who focus exclusively on money or troop numbers may overlook those needs. Yet emotions can also be manipulated: propaganda may exaggerate threats or conceal the experiences of another community. Understanding emotions is not equivalent to accepting every claim made in their name. A defensible analysis identifies who felt what, how that feeling was expressed politically, and whether leaders had plausible opportunities to de-escalate.
