Some wars begin when actions intended to prevent conflict create a momentum that decision-makers struggle to control. A reserve call-up looks defensive at home but threatening across the border. A rigid timetable makes delay expensive. An ally's promise changes another government's willingness to gamble. Intelligence agencies provide incompatible reports just as officials must decide whether to respond to a border incident. Each action can make the next one seem less optional.

The language of a 'runaway machine' must be used carefully. Mobilization systems do not sign declarations of war; people do. Technical plans influence choices, but leaders can revise them, even at substantial political and military cost. The important analytical question is where decision-makers perceived options narrowing, whether those perceptions were accurate, and who refused opportunities to pause. Claims that escalation was accidental should not erase deliberate threats, offensive plans or political responsibility.
