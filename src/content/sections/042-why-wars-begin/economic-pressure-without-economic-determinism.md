Poverty, recession, inflation and inequality can weaken political legitimacy and increase anger toward governments. They may reduce the resources available for peaceful accommodation, strengthen armed movements or make foreign enemies useful in political rhetoric. But economic hardship does not produce war according to a universal formula. Some poor countries remain peaceful; prosperous states have initiated major wars. The effects of economic conditions operate through political institutions, organizations, military capacity and the choices available to competing leaders.

A recession can weaken a regime and encourage compromise, or lead rulers to intensify repression, or strengthen groups urging confrontation. The outcome depends on who benefits, who loses, whether violence seems feasible, and whether alternative political channels exist. When examining a war during an economic crisis, compare it with other countries experiencing similar hardship without fighting. This counterfactual test helps us avoid confusing a circumstance that was present with a circumstance that was decisive.
