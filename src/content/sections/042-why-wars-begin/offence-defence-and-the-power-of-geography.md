A military balance is not merely a tally of soldiers, planes and budgets. Terrain, fortifications, logistics, weather, intelligence and the technology of the period influence whether attack is easier than defence. A river crossing may create a bottleneck; a wide plain may permit rapid maneuver; mountains may help defenders but isolate settlements. When leaders believe the first mover can capture decisive positions, pressure to attack rapidly may grow. When defence is clearly stronger, negotiation and deterrence may be more feasible. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-6" class="cite">6</a>\]

These calculations vary across war aims. Seizing a border town is different from occupying a hostile country, controlling an island, removing a regime, or suppressing an insurgency. A campaign may win quick battles but fail to achieve its political objective. The distinction between battlefield victory and sustainable governance is essential. Commanders tend to count capabilities that are easy to measure; political resilience, outside support and the costs of occupation can be underestimated. Geography does not dictate a single destiny; it changes which choices are plausible and how much they cost.
