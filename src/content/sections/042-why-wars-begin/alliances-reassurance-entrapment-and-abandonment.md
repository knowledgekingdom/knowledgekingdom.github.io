Alliances are designed to deter attacks by joining the capabilities and commitments of several states. They can stabilize a region when an aggressor believes resistance would be overwhelming. But alliance politics also generate two fears. <em>Abandonment</em> is the worry that an ally will fail to help in a crisis. <em>Entrapment</em> is the worry that the ally will take reckless risks because it expects support. Governments may issue especially firm promises to prevent abandonment, unintentionally giving an ally more confidence to confront rivals.

The opposite response is restraint: a powerful ally may signal limits to its support to discourage unnecessary escalation. Yet ambiguity can invite testing by adversaries. Historical alliance systems range from formal military treaties to loose diplomatic understandings, and their actual obligations depend on text, procedures and political will. Before attributing a war to 'alliances,' ask which commitment was legally binding, which was expected politically, and which decision-maker chose to activate it. A network of alliances can facilitate the spread of a crisis, but does not mechanically compel every declaration of war.
