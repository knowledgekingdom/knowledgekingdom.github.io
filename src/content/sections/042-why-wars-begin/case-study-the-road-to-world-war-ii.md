The German invasion of Poland in September 1939 arose in a very different setting. Nazi ideology, demands for territorial expansion, the dismantling of European treaties and a succession of deliberate acts of aggression formed part of the record. German leaders made choices to overturn borders and impose a new racial and political order. Other governments debated deterrence, concessions, rearmament and the risks of another European war. The August 1939 Nazi–Soviet Pact altered calculations and helped clear the way for the invasion. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]

The distinction from 1914 is instructive. Uncertainty and fear still affected every government, but attributing the outbreak solely to mutual misunderstanding would misrepresent the available evidence of Nazi expansionism. Not every war can be explained as a symmetrical bargaining failure between equally defensive sides. Historical context identifies the political program, documented war aims and coercive actions. Analytical models help organize inquiry only when they remain subordinate to evidence about what leaders actually sought to do.
