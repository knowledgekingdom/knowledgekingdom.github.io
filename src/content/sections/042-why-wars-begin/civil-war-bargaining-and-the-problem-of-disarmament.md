Ending an internal war can be harder than ending a war between states because the parties may have to live under one political authority after combat. A rebel group told to surrender its weapons may fear that a government will imprison or eliminate its leaders. A government asked to integrate rebel commanders may fear that they will use new positions to seize power. Peace agreements may promise elections, amnesty, police reform or autonomy, but enforcement can be difficult when institutions are devastated. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

Designers of peace settlements therefore consider verification, security guarantees, phased demobilization, power-sharing and mechanisms to resolve violations. None is a universal cure. Power-sharing can reduce immediate fear but entrench armed elites; blanket amnesties may enable negotiations but deny victims justice; foreign troops may deter renewed fighting but cannot remain indefinitely. A credible agreement requires more than a photograph of leaders shaking hands. It must change incentives for armed groups and provide some protection for communities whose security is at stake.
