Governments rarely know an opponent's complete military strength, war plans, willingness to sacrifice, internal divisions or political red lines. Leaders may know important facts that they have reasons to conceal. A country that announces weakness may invite coercion; one that boasts of strength may be bluffing. Consequently, two parties can negotiate with sincerely incompatible expectations. A ruler expecting an inexpensive victory may reject a compromise that another ruler believes is already generous. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

The difficulty goes beyond inaccurate troop counts. Military effectiveness depends on training, coordination, morale, logistical endurance, local allies and political resilience—factors uncertain even to the armed forces themselves. Decision-makers can also overestimate the likelihood that outsiders will intervene. Historians studying a diplomatic crisis should reconstruct each government's information environment instead of treating later knowledge of the outcome as if it had been available beforehand. War sometimes reveals information through devastating tests, but it can also produce propaganda, new commitments and escalating aims rather than clarity.
