Bulgaria's experience between 1912 and 1918 illustrates how compatible short-term objectives can conceal incompatible long-term ones. Bulgaria, Serbia, Greece and Montenegro formed the Balkan League in 1912 against the Ottoman Empire, but they disagreed over the disposition of territory—especially Macedonia—after victory. Military success against a common opponent did not produce a stable agreement on what should follow. The Second Balkan War of 1913 intensified grievances, and different calculations about restoring territory influenced Bulgaria's entry into World War I in 1915. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

The mechanism cannot be reduced to a timeless hostility among Balkan peoples. Alliances, treaties, maps, demographic claims, battlefield results, great-power diplomacy and leaders' choices all mattered. A Bulgarian-centered narrative may emphasize lost territory and the aspiration to unite populations described as Bulgarian, while Serbian, Greek and other accounts emphasize competing national claims and local communities' diverse identities. For rigorous analysis, distinguish each government's goals from the experiences and preferences of civilians in disputed districts. The lesson applies far beyond the peninsula: defeating a shared adversary does not eliminate competition among former allies.
