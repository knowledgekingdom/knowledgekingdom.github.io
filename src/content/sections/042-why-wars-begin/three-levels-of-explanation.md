Political scientists often separate causes at three levels. The international level concerns how states interact in a system without a single authority reliably capable of protecting each one. The domestic level concerns regimes, political institutions, interest groups, economies, and conflicts within states. The individual level concerns leaders' beliefs, character, health, advisers, risk perceptions and choices. These categories are aids to investigation, not separate boxes in which real events neatly fit. A leader may exaggerate an external danger because of domestic pressure within a threatening international environment. \[<a href="/042-why-wars-begin/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

Consider a state that mobilizes troops after a neighboring revolution. The international explanation might emphasize a shifting balance of power. A domestic explanation might point to generals fearing loss of political privilege. A personal explanation might point to a ruler who misreads the revolutionary government's intentions. None automatically cancels the others. We need evidence about the sequence: what changed first, what information decision-makers possessed, whose preferences mattered, and which alternative choices were actually available. Strong explanations identify causal connections rather than collecting every background condition that sounds relevant.
