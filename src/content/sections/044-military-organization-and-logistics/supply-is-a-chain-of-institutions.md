A manufactured product is the result of many different activities. Raw materials, energy, components, trained workers, factories, quality testing, contracts, transport and records are all involved. A government that purchases a finished item may have little immediate control over the suppliers that produce its smaller components. A change in one sector may therefore have consequences in apparently unrelated sectors.

Modern industrial goods can be especially difficult to replace because a substitute that looks similar may not meet the same specifications. Specialised production requires expertise and certification. A company that supplies ordinary consumer goods cannot instantly become a producer of sophisticated military goods merely because a government offers it a large contract. Industrial knowledge is embodied in workers, equipment and organisations, not only in money.

The same issue appears in civilian health care. A laboratory may own expensive machinery but depend on a particular reagent or maintenance service. The machine is useless for certain tests when the required inputs are absent. An understanding of supply chains should therefore follow dependencies across an economy, not assume that domestic assembly means complete self-sufficiency.
