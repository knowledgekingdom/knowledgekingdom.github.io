The Mongol Empire, introduced in Chapter 22, demonstrated the political importance of military organisation adapted to particular environments. Its forces drew on traditions of pastoral life, horsemanship and collective organisation in the Eurasian steppe. Their institutions also evolved as the empire incorporated people with different skills and economic backgrounds. A medieval imperial army was not simply a fixed group of warriors repeated unchanged from one campaign to the next. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]\[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

The ecological foundation of steppe society shaped its capabilities. Animals needed suitable conditions, and the geography of Eurasia varied from grasslands to agricultural regions, deserts and mountains. As Mongol rule expanded, officials encountered different languages, administrative traditions and technical expertise. The empire's history is therefore also a history of learning, appropriation, coercion and incorporation.

Do not turn this explanation into geographical determinism. Many pastoral societies inhabited the steppe, but they did not all create the same empire. Political unification, institutions, leadership, the circumstances of neighbouring states and contingent events also mattered. Likewise, the eventual fragmentation of the empire reveals that military expansion and durable political unity are different achievements. An ecological advantage is not a permanent guarantee of political success.
