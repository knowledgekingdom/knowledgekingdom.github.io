A limited war is one in which participants restrict important aspects of their objectives, means or geographic scope. The restrictions may reflect political preferences, fear of escalation, alliance commitments, legal obligations or practical constraints. The term does not imply that the suffering experienced by people within the conflict is limited or morally insignificant.

A powerful state may possess the ability to inflict extraordinary damage while being unable to establish the political order it desires. Occupation, legitimacy, public administration and the consent or resistance of populations cannot be inferred from the number of engagements won. The Korean War and the wars in Vietnam illustrate different historical settings in which political objectives, international involvement and military means became difficult to reconcile. Their causes and outcomes should be studied separately rather than turned into a single universal formula. In the case of Vietnam, declassified American diplomatic documents reveal how officials tried to reconcile their military activity with political aims, including sustaining a non-communist South Vietnam and limiting escalation. The fact that these objectives were stated in internal documents does not prove that the policies used to pursue them were effective or that Vietnamese actors shared American priorities. The documents are evidence of one government’s intentions, not neutral accounts of all participants. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-41" class="cite">41</a>\]

The Battle of Adwa on 1 March 1896 illustrates another kind of relationship between military and political power. Ethiopia defeated Italy’s attempt at conquest and preserved its independence, while Italy retained its colony of Eritrea. Ethiopia’s success cannot be explained by treating European industrial resources as an automatic guarantee of victory, or by attributing Ethiopian agency to terrain alone. The country’s political leadership, capacity for mobilisation and international diplomacy deserve independent attention. A recent geographic study by Daniel Waktola specifically challenges overly simple accounts that make terrain the determining factor. Adwa became a powerful symbol of resistance to colonial rule, although Ethiopia’s own imperial expansion and internal inequalities also belong in the wider historical account. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-39" class="cite">39</a>\]\[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-40" class="cite">40</a>\]

Time can alter the political meaning of costs. A government that initially expects a short commitment may confront years of expenditure, public disagreement and changed international circumstances. Local actors may assign a different value to the same political outcome. The question is not simply which country is stronger, but what each seeks, how much it values that objective, and what other actors are willing or able to accept.
