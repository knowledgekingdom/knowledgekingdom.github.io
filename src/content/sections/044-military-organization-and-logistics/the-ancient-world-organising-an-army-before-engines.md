Agrarian societies faced the problem of sustaining people and animals without modern transport or preservation technology. Their rulers relied on agricultural surpluses, taxation, labour obligations, trade and local arrangements. The ability to extract and organise resources varied enormously across time and place. A description of an ancient army that ignores the rural economy risks treating it as an institution suspended in the air.

Rome offers an instructive example. Its capacity to sustain warfare developed over centuries through institutions, allies, recruitment, taxation, roads, ports and administration. Republican and imperial arrangements differed, and neither period was uniform. The Roman military's effectiveness cannot be attributed to roads alone or to a timeless national character. The relationship between government, military service and the wider Mediterranean economy changed as Roman power expanded.

The evidence is uneven. Literary sources often emphasise the deeds of famous leaders, while inscriptions, archaeological remains, documentary records and surviving objects illuminate ordinary institutional life. Historians use them together to reconstruct provisioning and military organisation. The precise scale of some ancient forces remains disputed. Apparent numerical certainty in a modern popular account should be checked against the nature of surviving evidence. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]
