Napoleon's invasion of Russia in 1812 is often explained with a single image: soldiers struggling through winter snow. Cold was unquestionably a grave factor, but an explanation that begins and ends with weather is incomplete. Long before the final retreat, the campaign encountered serious problems of provisioning, exhaustion, disease, distance and the political unwillingness of Russia's leadership to accept Napoleon's desired settlement. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]

Napoleon's decision rested on political assumptions as well as military expectations. The campaign did not produce the peace he sought. The destruction and suffering experienced by soldiers and civilians reveal the limitations of translating impressive military capacity into coercive political leverage. The resources of France and its allies were substantial, but the circumstances in which those resources had to be organised were exceptionally demanding.

The historian should distinguish several questions. Why did the campaign begin? Why did its human and material difficulties become severe? Why did the Russian government continue the war? Why did the political settlement Napoleon expected not occur? A theory that answers only one of these questions cannot explain the entire outcome. In this example, geography, institutional limits and political resolve interacted. Winter weather intensified an already disastrous situation rather than acting as a complete independent explanation.
