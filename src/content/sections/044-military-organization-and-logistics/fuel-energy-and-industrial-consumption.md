Premodern societies depended heavily on human and animal energy. Industrial societies introduced coal, oil, electricity and mechanised transport on a far larger scale. The development of internal combustion engines expanded the capabilities of many institutions while tying them to complex energy systems. An economy's ability to refine, distribute and use energy is not the same thing as its ownership of energy resources underground.

Military institutions consume energy in many ways, including transportation, manufacturing, buildings, communications and maintenance. They also depend on civilian energy infrastructure, such as electricity supplied to hospitals and factories. Fuel prices and availability affect government finances and economic activity. These connections are why an analysis of industrial warfare belongs alongside the study of energy in Chapter 51 and later scientific chapters.

Ammunition is another material input whose production and replacement involve industrial labour, quality assurance and specialised inputs. Its significance varies enormously by historical period and type of conflict. The historian should not infer available stocks or consumption simply from televised images, budget announcements or the production claims of interested parties. Publicly available totals may be incomplete, refer to different definitions or conceal major differences in what is actually delivered.
