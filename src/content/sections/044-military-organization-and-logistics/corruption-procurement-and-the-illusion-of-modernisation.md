Procurement involves public institutions obtaining goods and services. Its quality depends on clear requirements, competent contracting, competition where appropriate, oversight and transparent accounting consistent with legitimate security needs. Defence procurement can be particularly difficult because the goods are specialised, the suppliers may be few and some information is legitimately sensitive.

These characteristics can also create opportunities for waste or corruption. A contract might be awarded on the basis of political connections rather than public value. An institution may underestimate long-term costs to obtain approval for a project. A supplier may deliver a product that meets the wording of a contract but does not fulfil the underlying institutional need. These are general governance problems, not evidence that any specific contract or country is corrupt.

Effective oversight requires a distinction between secrecy necessary to protect legitimate interests and secrecy used to prevent scrutiny. Public auditors and authorised parliamentary institutions may need access to information that cannot responsibly be published in full. An announcement of a purchase is not itself proof of a functioning capability.
