Chapter 34 examined industrialisation as a social and economic transformation. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, railways, telegraphy, factories, mechanised transport and scientific developments also changed warfare. States could mobilise and supply far larger institutions than many earlier governments, while becoming dependent on industrial and financial systems of unprecedented complexity.

During the First World War, military requirements placed extraordinary demands on factories, transportation, labour and public finance. Governments expanded administrative involvement in economic life, and the social division of labour changed as men entered military service and women took on many essential jobs. Rail transport mattered enormously, but the system also relied on numerous forms of local transportation and manual work. Industrialisation did not erase the physical demands placed on individual human beings.

The war also exposed the difference between military and economic endurance. A government could continue to field an armed force while food shortages, inflation, strikes or political opposition weakened its position at home. In some states these pressures contributed to revolutionary change.
