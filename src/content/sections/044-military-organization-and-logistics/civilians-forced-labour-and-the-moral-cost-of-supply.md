Military supply has always involved society. Historically, governments obtained food, transport, labour and other goods through purchase, taxation, requisition, coercion and sometimes outright plunder. These arrangements could transfer the burden of warfare to people who had no meaningful choice about participation. The language of efficiency must not obscure the human experience of those whose resources were taken. Contemporary scholarship on the long history of logistics also places the relationship between military requirements and the burdens imposed on noncombatants at the centre of analysis. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-42" class="cite">42</a>\]

Contemporary international humanitarian law imposes limits on conduct during armed conflict. The starvation of civilians as a method of warfare is prohibited, and objects indispensable to civilian survival receive special protections under applicable law. Medical and humanitarian protections are not optional expressions of kindness. They form part of legal obligations whose details and scope must be assessed in the relevant context. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]\[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-32" class="cite">32</a>\]

It is important to distinguish a historical description from moral endorsement. Explaining that coercive extraction helped sustain a historical empire does not justify the abuse of its subjects. Equally, an analysis of economic consequences should include the distribution of those consequences: which households lost food, which workers were forced into service, which communities were displaced and which institutions benefited. Chapter 46 will examine the legal framework systematically; Chapter 49 will explore colonial violence and resistance.
