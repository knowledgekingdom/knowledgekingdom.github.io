Information is a report or observation. Intelligence, in the institutional sense, involves collecting, assessing and interpreting information relevant to decisions. The distinction matters because a report does not necessarily reveal the situation it claims to describe. It may be outdated, misunderstood, deliberately misleading or accurate but incomplete.

In military and political institutions, intelligence assessment can suffer from the same cognitive biases examined in Chapters 1 and 2. Leaders may prefer evidence consistent with prior commitments. Analysts may have difficulty communicating disagreement. Information may acquire a reputation for reliability merely because it is repeated by several offices that actually depend on the same initial report.

Responsible interpretation distinguishes what was observed, how it was inferred and how much confidence is warranted. A decision-maker must also separate evidence about capability from evidence about intentions. The fact that a government possesses a particular capability does not establish that it has decided to use it. Equally, a declaration of peaceful intentions cannot substitute for evidence when commitments are contested. Uncertainty is a feature of international politics, not simply an error that more technology will always remove.
