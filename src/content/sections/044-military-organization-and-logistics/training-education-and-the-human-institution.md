Training builds competence in particular duties. Education develops broader understanding and judgment. Experience can reveal mistakes that a classroom cannot reproduce, but experience alone does not ensure that an individual understands why an event unfolded as it did. An institution needs ways to learn from both success and failure.

Collective competence differs from individual competence. A hospital may employ excellent surgeons and still struggle if its departments cannot exchange records or agree on procedures. Similarly, armed forces depend on the coordination of people who have different expertise, backgrounds and responsibilities. Language, institutional culture, trust and continuity all influence whether specialists can work together.

Professional military education should not be confused with unquestioning acceptance of tradition. History is useful partly because it shows how previous assumptions failed when technologies, political conditions and societies changed. Institutional learning requires open discussion, accurate evidence and the ability to distinguish a genuine lesson from an attractive anecdote. If a training exercise is organised primarily to reassure an audience of senior officials, its apparent success may reveal little about the institution's capacity to respond to uncertainty.
