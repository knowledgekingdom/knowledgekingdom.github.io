Not every historically important military-supported transport undertaking is a battle. In 1948 the Soviet Union blocked land access to the western sectors of Berlin. The Western Allies responded by transporting essential supplies to the city by air. The airlift lasted into 1949, continuing for a period after the blockade was lifted in May. Chapter 41 introduced this crisis as a pivotal moment in the early Cold War. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]

The event illustrates the connection between material provision and political commitment. West Berlin's residents needed food, fuel and other necessities, and the Western governments sought to maintain their position without initiating a direct military confrontation with the Soviet Union. The political message derived from the sustained provision of essential services, not simply from the possession of aircraft.

It is also an example of the danger of reducing history to the actions of great powers. Berliners experienced uncertainty, deprivation and dependence on decisions made far beyond their city. Their endurance was part of the political context of the crisis. Transport institutions were important because they connected international policy to the daily lives of civilians. Logistics can therefore be studied as an instrument of statecraft, social resilience and humanitarian provision as well as military capacity.
