The history of military technology is often narrated as a progression from simpler to more sophisticated machines. A more useful approach asks which dependencies changed. Mechanisation reduced some forms of animal labour but increased demand for industrial production and fuels. Electronics and digital systems expanded the possibilities of information processing but added reliance on software, semiconductors, electricity and specialised expertise.

Technology may create impressive capacity in one area while introducing significant financial and organisational costs. An advanced system may depend on a supplier whose products cannot easily be substituted. A software upgrade may require institutional testing and new training. The value of innovation depends partly on how it fits the organisation and its legitimate purpose, rather than on novelty alone.

Avoid two opposite errors: assuming that new technology automatically determines political outcomes, and assuming that technology never changes the nature of military institutions. Its consequences depend on the interaction of economics, infrastructure, skills, law, geography and government decisions. This is the same systems approach applied to agriculture in Chapter 10, industrialisation in Chapter 34 and geopolitical geography in Chapter 43.
