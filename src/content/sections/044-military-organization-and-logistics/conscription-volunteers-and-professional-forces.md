Conscription is compulsory military service under law; voluntary recruitment relies on individuals choosing to join under specified conditions. States have used mixtures of both, including professional career personnel, short-service volunteers and compulsory service. A professional force describes institutional expertise and occupational commitment, not simply the absence of conscription. A conscript force can include highly trained career officers and specialists.

The growth of mass citizenship in the nineteenth century linked service to political belonging, as Chapter 36 explained. Some governments defended compulsory service as a shared civic obligation; critics emphasised individual freedom, unequal burdens, conscientious objection and the economic cost of taking young people out of civilian life. The choice cannot be evaluated only by counting how many names can be entered into a register.

An all-volunteer force faces different challenges: competition with civilian employers, retention of experienced specialists and the social distribution of service. Conscription creates its own costs, including training successive cohorts and maintaining institutions that can support them. Recruitment policy involves economics, demography, legitimacy and the political goals of defence. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-34" class="cite">34</a>\] A country with a declining working-age population cannot assume its past personnel model will remain affordable indefinitely.
