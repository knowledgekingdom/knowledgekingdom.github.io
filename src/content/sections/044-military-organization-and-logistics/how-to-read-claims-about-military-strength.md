A headline announces that a country is becoming much stronger because its defence budget has doubled. Before accepting the conclusion, ask whether the figure is nominal or adjusted for inflation; whether the accounting definition changed; whether expenditure was budgeted or actually incurred; and what the country intends to accomplish. Then separate inputs, institutional outputs and political outcomes.

Other claims require similar care. A photograph of a military parade documents an event, not the readiness of an entire institution. A published inventory may include assets of uncertain condition. An official statement may express political intent without independently verifying capability. An independent estimate may also have limitations because military data are often incomplete. Compare several kinds of evidence rather than treating scepticism as a reason to dismiss all evidence.

The central intellectual habit is to replace the question 'Who is strongest?' with 'Strong in relation to what, according to which evidence, under what conditions, and with what political purpose?' The first question encourages a single dramatic ranking. The second helps explain why governments with substantial resources sometimes fail, why small countries can matter disproportionately, and why a military achievement does not necessarily become a durable political settlement.
