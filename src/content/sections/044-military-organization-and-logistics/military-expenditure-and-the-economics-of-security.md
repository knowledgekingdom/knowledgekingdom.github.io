Military expenditure measures resources devoted to military purposes according to a defined accounting method. SIPRI's military expenditure database includes categories beyond the purchase of arms, such as personnel and relevant military operations. The exact availability of data and treatment of expenditures vary among countries. SIPRI cautions that expenditure is an input, not a direct measure of military effectiveness. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-35" class="cite">35</a>\]

Consider two fictional states. One devotes five percent of a relatively small economy to its armed forces; the other devotes two percent of a much larger economy. The former may bear a greater relative economic burden while the latter spends more in absolute terms. Neither percentage establishes which country has the greater capability for a particular task. Prices, wages, accounting definitions, exchange rates and the efficiency of institutions affect the interpretation.

Public expenditure also has an opportunity cost. Resources committed to one purpose cannot simultaneously be used for every alternative purpose. This does not establish whether defence expenditure is too high or too low; that is a political judgment involving threats, values and feasible alternatives. It does mean that a democratic discussion should compare the expected benefits of spending with its costs and uncertainty. Chapter 63 will introduce the economic reasoning behind this trade-off in more detail.
