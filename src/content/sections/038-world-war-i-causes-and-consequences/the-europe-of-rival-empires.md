In 1914 much of Europe was organized into empires rather than ethnically uniform nation-states. Austria-Hungary encompassed German speakers, Hungarians, Czechs, Slovaks, Poles, Ruthenians, Croats, Serbs, Romanians, Italians and others. The Russian Empire ruled vast populations with different languages, religions and legal positions. The Ottoman Empire still controlled Anatolia, Arab provinces and a small European territory. Germany possessed overseas colonies, while Britain and France governed enormous imperial possessions across Africa and Asia. Serbian ambitions, Bulgarian grievances from 1913 and fears about the future of Macedonia were part of a crowded political landscape.

Competition took forms other than territorial conquest: commercial disputes, battleship construction, diplomatic prestige and domestic arguments about national security. Leaders interpreted other powers' behavior through memories of earlier crises. Yet the great powers also cooperated, invested in one another's economies and attended international conferences. War was not a mechanical result of imperialism. The problem was that rivalry coexisted with increasingly dangerous expectations: some decision-makers feared that delaying confrontation would leave their country in a weaker position. \[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]
