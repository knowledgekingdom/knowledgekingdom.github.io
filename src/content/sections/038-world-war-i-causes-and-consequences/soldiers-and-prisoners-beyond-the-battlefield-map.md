Military service could mean extended stretches of waiting interrupted by moments of terror. Soldiers endured mud, lice, shellfire, frostbite, heat, poor sanitation, missing comrades and the obligation to follow orders even when success seemed doubtful. Illness and exposure remained serious dangers. Letters and diaries reveal longing for families and frustration with command, but surviving texts come disproportionately from men able to write, preserve correspondence or leave accessible archives. Prisoners of war faced varying treatment depending on captor, location, supplies and international agreements.

Psychological injury was described through terms such as shell shock, though contemporary explanations were inconsistent and often morally judgmental. Some military doctors recognized traumatic effects; others suspected weakness or misconduct. Modern diagnoses should not be projected uncritically backward onto every individual. Desertion, mutiny, surrender and obedience also require context. A soldier could believe in national defense while losing confidence in a particular offensive. Military discipline and solidarity coexisted with dissent, and much of the war was sustained by ordinary people rather than the dramatic choices of famous generals. \[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]\[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]
