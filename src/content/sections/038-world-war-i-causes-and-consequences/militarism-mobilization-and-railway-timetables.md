European armies expanded before 1914, and many societies celebrated uniforms, military parades and national service. New industrial technology increased the destructive power of artillery, rapid-fire weapons and large armies. Military leaders developed detailed mobilization schedules because a state that could assemble soldiers and move them to a frontier sooner might achieve an early advantage. Railway networks made this possible, but they could also produce a dangerous belief that mobilization had to proceed rapidly or the opportunity would be lost.

It is misleading to claim that generals automatically overruled every civilian leader. Cabinets could approve, modify or reject military proposals, and in some states their authority was contested. Nevertheless, political decision-makers sometimes accepted planning assumptions they did not fully grasp. German strategy sought to defeat France quickly in the west while Russia mobilized more slowly in the east. The Russian government feared that partial mobilization might leave it vulnerable. Choices intended as deterrence could be interpreted by opponents as preparations for attack.
