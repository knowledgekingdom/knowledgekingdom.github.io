Heavy guns accounted for a large share of battlefield casualties. A bombardment required not only guns but ammunition plants, railways, horses, trucks, engineers, trained observers and mathematical calculations. An army could fire enormous quantities of shells yet fail if its targeting information was weak or the enemy had constructed deep shelters. Shell craters destroyed roads; unexploded munitions remained dangerous long after the fighting. Modern warfare depended on production and distribution systems as much as the courage of individual soldiers.

Industrial output did not translate directly into victory. Quality of shells, fuses, guns, logistics and trained personnel mattered. Factory workers were exposed to hazardous chemicals and exhausting labor. States borrowed heavily, raised taxes and managed currency to finance supplies, often producing inflation and shortages. The trench soldier at the front and the worker producing explosive charges in an industrial city were parts of the same military-economic system.
