The Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919, imposed territorial, military and financial obligations on Germany. Germany lost colonies and territory, accepted restrictions on armed forces, and faced reparations whose schedule and practical operation developed through later decisions. Article 231, often called the 'war guilt clause,' established legal responsibility for losses caused by Germany and its allies in the context of reparations. It should not be read as a complete historical account of every cause of war.

Critics described the treaty as humiliating, while advocates argued it provided security after an invasion and immense destruction. The treaty had consequences, but historians disagree about how directly it caused later political extremism; the Great Depression, domestic political structures and choices made after 1919 mattered greatly. Some punitive provisions were severe, yet it is inaccurate to say reparations alone made a second world war unavoidable. The League of Nations was also established in this settlement, although the United States did not join. \[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]\[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-33" class="cite">33</a>\]
