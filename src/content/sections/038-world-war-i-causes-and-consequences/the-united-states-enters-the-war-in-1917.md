When the war began, the United States remained neutral while American firms and banks conducted substantial trade and finance with the Allied side. Citizens disagreed sharply about intervention. German unrestricted submarine warfare put American ships and lives at risk, and the intercepted Zimmermann Telegram—in which Germany proposed cooperation with Mexico if war broke out—intensified suspicion. President Woodrow Wilson asked Congress for war, and the United States declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917. Economic ties mattered, but they do not replace the immediate diplomatic and security triggers.

American manpower, finance, supplies and industrial capacity gradually strengthened the Allied position. Its army required training and transport; it did not arrive as a fully prepared mass force overnight. Wilson's language of making the world safer for democracy expressed a political vision that sat uneasily beside racial inequality in the United States and colonial rule elsewhere. Americans entered a war in which their national objectives overlapped with, but were not identical to, those of Britain and France.
