In 1915 Ottoman authorities initiated mass deportations and killings of Armenian Christians. Large numbers were murdered directly or died through forced marches, starvation, disease and exposure. The destruction, widely recognized by scholars as the Armenian genocide, was connected to wartime fears, the policies of the Committee of Union and Progress and the Ottoman state's effort to transform its population. Some Ottoman accounts invoked rebellion or military danger, but such claims do not justify the systematic targeting of an entire civilian community. Armenian experiences and the documentation assembled by contemporaries are indispensable evidence.

The wartime Middle East also witnessed tremendous suffering among Assyrian and other Christian communities, as well as Muslim civilians in several theaters. War does not dissolve responsibility for deliberate violence. It complicates evidence gathering, because mass death may result from direct killing, forced displacement, hunger and epidemics in different combinations. Historians compare official orders, diplomatic reports, eyewitness testimony, relief records and later demographic work.
