The League of Nations was intended to provide mechanisms for collective security, dispute resolution and cooperation after the catastrophe. It promoted some important work in health, refugees, minority protection and international administration. But the League lacked its own standing armed forces, depended on member governments' willingness to cooperate and was weakened by the absence of the United States. Important defeated and newly created states entered or participated at different times, limiting the organization's reach in its early years.

The institution should not be dismissed merely because it failed to prevent another world war. Its activities help explain the longer development of international organizations, and its weaknesses offer lessons about the difficulty of enforcing commitments when great powers disagree. Collective security is a political bargain, not a machine that runs independently of its members. When studying the League, distinguish its constitutional design, its actual achievements in particular disputes and the circumstances under which it could not act. \[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]\[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-34" class="cite">34</a>\]
