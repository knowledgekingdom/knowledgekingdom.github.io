In the decades after the armistice, governments, families and veterans constructed memorials and accounts of the war. Some celebrated sacrifice and victory; others emphasized futility, betrayal or lost opportunities. National interpretations diverged sharply. German arguments about an alleged 'stab in the back' blamed internal enemies despite the military deterioration of 1918; this myth became politically destructive. In Britain and France, particular Western Front battles came to stand for the war as a whole. In Bulgaria, memories of sacrifice, the Macedonian Front and the Neuilly settlement shaped political grievances.

Historical memory matters, but it is not a substitute for historical evidence. A memorial records who a community chose to honor; it may leave out colonial soldiers, civilian victims, dissenters or opponents. An officer's memoir can reveal motives while also defending his reputation. Across Europe, revolutionary violence, border conflicts, demobilized soldiers and economic crisis continued after 1918. The 'interwar' period was not simply twenty peaceful years between two wars.
