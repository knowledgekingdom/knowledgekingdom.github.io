World War I grew out of a dangerous international environment, but it began through a sequence of decisions. Nationalist violence and the imperial tensions discussed in Chapter 37 mattered. So did Germany's strategic calculations, Habsburg ambitions, Russia's commitments, French security expectations, Britain's decisions and military mobilization plans. The war endured because states mobilized industrial production, drew on worldwide empires and struggled to translate battlefield suffering into a settlement their leaders considered acceptable. It ended through political and military collapse, not because all sides simultaneously decided they had fought enough.

Retain three distinctions. First, background conditions differ from immediate decisions and from explanations of wartime duration. Second, victory or defeat in an individual battle does not determine the fate of an entire coalition; Bulgaria's effective defense at Doiran in parts of the war coexisted with a catastrophic breakthrough at Dobro Pole in 1918. Third, armistice, treaty and lasting peace are different achievements. The war destroyed empires and opened possibilities for new states while leaving violence, grievances and exclusion behind.
