Bulgaria signed the Treaty of Neuilly-sur-Seine on 27 November 1919. The settlement imposed territorial losses, military restrictions and reparations. It transferred areas of the western border to the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes; Southern Dobruja remained with Romania; and Bulgaria lost direct control of Western Thrace, which later passed to Greece. The treaty's treatment of the Aegean question and access arrangements is legally more complicated than saying the border was decided in one simple transaction. Bulgaria's army was sharply restricted, and wartime losses compounded domestic instability.

Many Bulgarians experienced the treaty as a second national catastrophe after 1913. Yet the consequences also need to be viewed from the perspectives of neighboring states and minority communities whose security claims were not always compatible. Aleksandar Stamboliyski's postwar government pursued agrarian reforms and difficult diplomacy amid political conflict. The treaty did not settle disputes over Macedonia, Dobruja or minority rights for all time.
