Victorious and associated powers met in Paris in 1919 to negotiate peace. Leaders including Wilson, Clemenceau, Lloyd George and Orlando had different priorities: national security, reparations, territorial adjustment, domestic public opinion and new international institutions. Defeated powers were not equal partners in drafting the principal treaties. The rhetoric of national self-determination was influential, but it was applied unevenly; some European peoples secured recognized states while many colonized populations remained under foreign rule.

Wilson's Fourteen Points offered a language of transparency, self-determination and collective security, but the resulting settlements were political compromises among governments with unequal bargaining power. New or enlarged states included Poland, Czechoslovakia, Romania and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes. Each contained minority populations, and territorial disputes remained. The peace-makers had to contend with food shortages, disease, domestic revolutions and the fear of future war.
