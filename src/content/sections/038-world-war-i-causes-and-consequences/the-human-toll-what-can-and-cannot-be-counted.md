The First World War killed millions of military personnel and civilians. Any single worldwide total depends on choices about which years, countries, causes and populations to include. Combat deaths, epidemic deaths, wartime starvation, genocide, forced displacement and excess mortality are not identical categories. The 1918 influenza pandemic spread during the final war year and affected military and civilian populations across the world; attributing every pandemic death directly to military combat would be a serious historical error. Likewise, exact casualty figures vary by archive, reporting system and whether missing prisoners are subsequently reclassified.

Counting remains necessary. Records of mobilization, wounds, missing soldiers, graves and pensions reveal the scale of suffering, while demographic comparisons can indicate the effects of food shortages and disease. But a human being is not only a point in a national total. Disabled veterans needed work and care; widows and orphans navigated difficult legal and economic systems; displaced people searched for homes.
