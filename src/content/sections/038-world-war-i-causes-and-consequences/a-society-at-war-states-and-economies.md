Governments organized production, controlled prices or supplies, issued war bonds, censored information and directed transport. The term total war is useful only with qualifications: mobilization was unprecedented in many places, but not every economic activity was centrally directed, and states had different capacities. Farming households, industrial workers and businesses continued to negotiate with authorities and sometimes resisted. Food queues and rationing were political experiences that could affect confidence in government just as strongly as a defeat at the front.

The war changed finance. States accumulated public debt and relied on taxes, borrowing and money creation in differing proportions. Inflation, shortages and disrupted international trade reshaped purchasing power. Colonial populations contributed men and materials without always enjoying equal political rights. Mobilization was socially uneven: some firms profited while wage earners faced rising prices; some women obtained new employment while also retaining unpaid care responsibilities.
