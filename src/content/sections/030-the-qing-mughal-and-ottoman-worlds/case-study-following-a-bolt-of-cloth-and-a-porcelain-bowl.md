Imagine a merchant carrying Indian cotton textiles to an Indian Ocean port around 1700. The cloth may have been spun and woven by separate households, bought on credit, transported through a regional market and sold to traders using different languages. Taxes might be paid to a Mughal official, a regional governor or another authority depending on the route. The merchant need not care which historian would later call this 'the Mughal economy'; the practical questions were prices, protection, finance, quality and delivery. This is a constructed example grounded in the period's trading institutions, not a single documented shipment.

Now follow a porcelain bowl made in China. Craft specialists, clay and kiln fuel, river transport, wholesalers and maritime traders might all be involved before a buyer in another region ever saw the object. European interest did not remove the roles of Asian merchants or domestic demand. If the bowl entered an Ottoman household, its presence would show exchange and taste; it would not prove that the Ottoman sultan controlled the Chinese kiln.

Objects connect societies precisely because trade crosses political borders. One vessel can become evidence for production technology, consumption, cultural borrowing, credit, exchange rates and the reach of armies. Yet an object rarely reveals the full story by itself. To infer the working of global commerce, historians connect material finds with port registers, contracts, texts and comparative prices.
