Cities such as Agra, Lahore, Delhi, Ahmedabad, Surat and later other regional centers drew together officials, soldiers, money changers, craftspeople, pilgrims and merchants. The location of an imperial capital could shift, and court movement itself stimulated demand for transport, provisions and luxury goods. Political power and commercial prosperity were linked without being identical: a wealthy textile region did not become rich only because it hosted an emperor.

Indian cotton textiles held a major place in world commerce. Cotton cloth of different qualities traveled through Indian Ocean networks, while silk, indigo, spices and other goods circulated through regional and overseas markets. Merchants, brokers, financiers and credit instruments supported long-distance exchange. Europeans arriving in western Indian ports entered markets already served by Gujarati, Armenian, Arab, Persian and other traders. Portuguese, Dutch and English companies could gain advantages, but they did not create Indian commercial sophistication from nothing.

The Mughal state took an interest in customs, trade security and port revenues. Its authority at coastal outlets varied, and some influential maritime regions lay beyond direct control. The politics of overseas trade thus depended on both imperial officials and regional rulers. The broader lesson from Chapter 26 remains: commerce crossed boundaries that imperial armies could not easily eliminate.
