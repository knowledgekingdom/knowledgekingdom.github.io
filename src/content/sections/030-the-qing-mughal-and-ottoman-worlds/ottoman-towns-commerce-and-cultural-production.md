Istanbul was a vast imperial city supplied by networks of grain, animals, timber, textiles and shipping. Other centers such as Cairo, Aleppo, Damascus, Salonica and Bursa connected inland and maritime trade. Goods moved by caravan, river and sea; merchants used credit and partnerships, while urban guilds shaped production and local regulation. The empire's economic life cannot be reduced to the tax receipts that reached the sultan's treasury.

Ottoman architecture, book arts, textiles, calligraphy, ceramics and music combined traditions from different regions. The architect Sinan and the construction of imperial mosque complexes show how technical knowledge and court patronage shaped urban landscapes. Religious foundations supplied food, education and water in some places while symbolizing the legitimacy and generosity of patrons. Such building projects involved skilled labor and extensive supply chains; their materials tell economic as well as aesthetic stories.

The empire also participated in global commercial changes. European maritime routes altered some long-distance circuits, but they did not simply make Mediterranean and Red Sea commerce disappear. Ports adapted, provincial traders remained active, and customs and transit routes continued to matter. The old claim that Vasco da Gama immediately ruined all Ottoman or Middle Eastern trade is an example of a dramatic but misleading historical shortcut.
