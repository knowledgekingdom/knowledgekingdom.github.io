Courtly architecture and ceremony helped rulers transform power into legitimacy. The Forbidden City projected an ordered imperial hierarchy; Mughal audience halls, gardens and palaces staged universal kingship; Ottoman ceremonial processions and mosque complexes displayed a Muslim dynasty's authority and beneficence. Foreign envoys could recognize grandeur without accepting an emperor's claims. Subjects might participate in ceremonies while resenting taxes or military levies.

Artisans, calligraphers, painters, architects, metalworkers and textile producers often moved across courtly borders. Styles borrowed from Iran, India, China, Central Asia and Europe were adapted rather than merely copied. Chinese motifs appeared in other media; Persianate manuscript traditions reshaped Mughal painting; Ottoman workshops transformed regional techniques into distinctive design languages. Cultural exchange should be explained through patrons, labor, materials and knowledge, not described as an automatic melting pot.

The historian must resist a selection bias. Museums preserve courtly objects partly because rulers and elites possessed resources to commission durable luxury goods. Common households used perishable items and left less visible remains. The brilliance of surviving objects is evidence of skill and patronage, not a census of living standards. Pair the palace with the farm, the workshop and the tax record.
