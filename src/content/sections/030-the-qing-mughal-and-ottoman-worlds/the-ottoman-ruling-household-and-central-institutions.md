The Ottoman sultan's court combined dynastic, military, administrative and ceremonial functions. The imperial council and senior ministers, notably the grand vizier, helped manage decisions. Palace-trained officials, scribes, legal scholars and provincial appointees sustained communication across regions. Succession struggles were often dangerous because dynastic rule did not always provide a peaceful, universally accepted method of passing power to the next sovereign.

The household system, including the palace's women's quarters, produced its own networks of patronage and influence. Valide sultans and other powerful women could affect appointments, diplomacy and charitable foundations, although the scope of their involvement varied. The familiar phrase 'Sultanate of Women' describes a historical interpretation of certain periods and should not substitute for close analysis of individual actors and institutions.

Public religious and charitable foundations, called waqfs, helped finance mosques, schools, fountains, kitchens, hospitals and other services in many towns. Such endowments could serve pious, social, family and political objectives simultaneously. They also reveal that an empire's institutions were not only military commands and tax offices: public services and patronage connected state elites with urban communities.
