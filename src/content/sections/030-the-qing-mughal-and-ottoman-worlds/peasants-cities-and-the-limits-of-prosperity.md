All three imperial economies depended on the labor of food producers. Regional markets and craft specialization flourished in many places, but prosperity could be interrupted by drought, war, epidemics, high taxation or the capture of land and resources by powerful groups. A good harvest one year did not guarantee security the next. Village households bore risks not always visible in court histories.

Cities concentrated wealth and knowledge. Qing commercial centers, Mughal textile towns and Ottoman ports linked manufacturing, credit and consumption. At the same time, large cities required food and fuel imported from broad hinterlands, making them vulnerable to interrupted transport and price increases. Administrative decisions to stabilize food supplies could protect some residents while shifting costs to producers elsewhere.

Modern growth comparisons are especially difficult. Historians can use wages, price series, tax records, household inventories, estimates of urbanization and output, but each measure has blind spots. Arguments that 'Asia was rich' or 'Europe was rich' flatten stark differences between regions, decades and classes. Economic history asks what grew, who benefited, which prices changed, and how much confidence the evidence warrants.
