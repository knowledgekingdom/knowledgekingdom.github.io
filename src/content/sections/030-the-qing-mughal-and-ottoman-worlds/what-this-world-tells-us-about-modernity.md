These empires were not frozen remnants of a 'traditional' world waiting for European modernity. They developed institutions for long-distance rule, supported large markets and distinctive intellectual cultures, and confronted problems of taxation, army finance, religious diversity and unequal development. Their responses helped shape later political possibilities. The Qing left profound territorial and institutional legacies in China and Inner Asia; Mughal courts and successor states influenced South Asian languages, culture and law; Ottoman administration and provincial society shaped the Balkans and Middle East long after particular institutions changed.

Their achievements should not be romanticized. Imperial conquest, forced labor, legal hierarchy, frontier violence and fiscal coercion were real. Nor should their later losses be used to dismiss earlier economic capacity and administrative sophistication. The point of a global history is not to award a trophy to one civilization. It is to ask how societies solved problems, whose interests defined the solutions, and why change followed multiple paths.

Chapter 31 will examine the Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment, including the institutions and debates that reshaped European intellectual life. It must not be written as a claim that Europe alone possessed reason, experiment or scientific knowledge. The Qing, Mughal and Ottoman worlds remind us that knowledge traveled across languages and political boundaries. Europe’s transformations were important; they occurred within an already connected and intellectually active world.
