The English East India Company began as a trading company with royal privileges and investors, established in 1600. For much of its history, it bargained for access in Asian markets, paid customs and operated through local rulers and merchants. The Dutch VOC and other European companies were also major participants. These organizations combined commercial aims with armed shipping, fortified settlements and diplomatic ambitions; their powers changed over time.

The Battle of Plassey in Bengal in 1757 illustrates the importance of local politics. Company forces under Robert Clive confronted the forces of the nawab Siraj ud-Daulah amid disputes over privileges and fortifications, and the defection of key actors helped produce the outcome. The later grant of diwani rights in 1765 gave the company far greater access to revenues in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Even then, political institutions and practical power evolved over years rather than transforming in a single instant.

It would be anachronistic to describe Akbar's sixteenth-century empire as already subordinate to the British because of what happened two centuries later. It is equally misleading to depict Indians as having no agency in the company's rise. Creditors, bankers, soldiers, local politicians, rivals and those affected by exploitative rule all shaped the transition. A core historical challenge is to explain changed power relations without assuming the final result had been inevitable.
