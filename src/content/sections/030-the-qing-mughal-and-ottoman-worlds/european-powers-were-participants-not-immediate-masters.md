From the sixteenth through much of the eighteenth century, European merchants usually negotiated access to existing Asian markets rather than commanding them outright. The Portuguese used naval violence and fortified posts in some waters; the Dutch and English companies assembled commercial and military networks; missionary and diplomatic contacts accompanied trade. But Qing officials controlled the framework of much Western commerce at Guangzhou, the Mughals and successor states shaped conditions within India, and Ottomans regulated trade across many vital routes.

This does not mean European powers were insignificant before the nineteenth century. They had already established empires elsewhere, participated in coercive trade, developed powerful navies and increasingly intervened in Asian politics. Yet outcomes varied greatly. In Bengal, a European company acquired major fiscal rights in the later eighteenth century. In the Qing world, European pressure produced a very different chronology of confrontations. Ottoman-European relations included both warfare and practical alliances, commerce and diplomacy.

Historical 'inevitability' is usually a story told after the fact. A court in 1700 did not know the global political map of 1900. Understanding possibilities available to contemporaries helps restore the uncertainty, contingency and human choice that hindsight too easily removes.
