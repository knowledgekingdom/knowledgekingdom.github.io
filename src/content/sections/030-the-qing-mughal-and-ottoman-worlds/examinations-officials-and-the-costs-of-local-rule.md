The Qing continued the scholar-official recruitment system, with highly competitive examinations emphasizing classical learning and literary performance. The system cultivated a shared elite culture and offered some avenues of mobility, but success required training, resources and social networks. It did not create universal equality of opportunity. Scholars, clerks, magistrates, merchants and lineage leaders all played roles in local administration.

One official might oversee a jurisdiction containing far more people than he could personally supervise. He relied on clerks, assistants, village notables and written petitions. Formal taxation and court judgments were only part of governance; grain relief, infrastructure, mediation and local charitable institutions also mattered. The central government could issue standards but had limited information about daily compliance.

A simple comparison helps: a state might appear highly centralized because appointments flowed from the throne, while actual enforcement depended on village intermediaries. This is not hypocrisy—it is a structural feature of large premodern states. But reliance on intermediaries created opportunities for unequal treatment, informal payments and conflicts over who really possessed authority.
