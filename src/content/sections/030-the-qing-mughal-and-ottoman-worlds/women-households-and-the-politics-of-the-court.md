Dynastic households were political institutions. Royal women exercised influence through property, marriage diplomacy, patronage, advice, religious endowments and networks of servants and relatives. The degree of influence depended on rank, family alliances and individual circumstances. Figures such as Nur Jahan under Jahangir illustrate that power at court cannot be measured simply by the official rank of male ministers.

Women also appear in records as artisans, merchants, cultivators, dependents, household managers and patrons, although evidence is uneven and frequently shaped by male authors. The word 'harem' often evokes fantasy more than analysis. Seclusion and household rules were real in certain elite settings, but the court household could be a center of strategic relationships and decisions. Its institutions varied between the Mughal, Qing and Ottoman worlds and across centuries.

A comparison with ordinary households matters. Elite women could command resources inaccessible to most people, while rural women labored in agriculture, family production and local markets. To reconstruct women's experiences, historians combine court chronicles with property records, petitions, travelers' accounts and material culture. The fact that sources speak unevenly does not mean that the unrecorded people lacked agency.
