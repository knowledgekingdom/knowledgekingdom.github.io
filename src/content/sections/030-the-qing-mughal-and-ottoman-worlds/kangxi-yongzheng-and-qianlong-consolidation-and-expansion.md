Three long or consequential reigns shaped the Qing's high imperial period: Kangxi (reigned 1661–1722), Yongzheng (1722–1735), and Qianlong (1735–1796). Kangxi's government overcame the Revolt of the Three Feudatories, consolidated Taiwan after the defeat of the Zheng regime in 1683, and contested power across Inner Asia. War with Russia ended in the Treaty of Nerchinsk in 1689, one of several diplomatic arrangements through which frontiers and trade were negotiated.

Yongzheng pursued administrative and fiscal measures, including reforms to the collection and allocation of taxes and attention to the operation of imperial communications. Qianlong presided over territorial expansion, notably the mid-eighteenth-century campaigns against the Dzungar polity and in Xinjiang. Those wars involved mass violence, forced displacement and reorganization of frontier societies; the word 'expansion' should not hide civilian consequences.

Eighteenth-century rule also produced impressive literary compilations, artistic patronage and monumental building. Yet the court's ideal of prosperity was unevenly shared. Population growth, pressure on land, local corruption, environmental stress and frontier commitments posed challenges. The later Qing crises of the nineteenth century belong primarily to subsequent chapters, but their roots must not be erased by an uncritical tale of a golden age.
