Jahangir (reigned 1605–1627) inherited a vast empire and cultivated distinctive courtly interests, including portraiture and naturalistic painting. Mughal artists studied plants, animals, manuscripts and human faces with extraordinary skill. Political conflicts continued, including issues of succession and the standing of powerful court figures; the exquisite image of a peaceful imperial audience was itself part of the work of legitimizing authority.

Shah Jahan (reigned 1628–1658) patronized architecture that combined imperial display with religious, aesthetic and spatial symbolism. The Taj Mahal at Agra, built as the mausoleum of Mumtaz Mahal, is a major example, but its global fame can distort the wider picture. Forts, mosques, palaces, roads, gardens and urban redevelopment were parts of extensive patronage networks and required large flows of labor, materials and revenue. Craftsmen and designers working on these projects contributed to traditions that reached far beyond the court.

Art should be used as evidence with care. An imperial portrait may tell us what virtues a ruler wished to project rather than what every subject thought of him. A splendid building demonstrates technical coordination and concentrated resources, but does not by itself prove that most people were prosperous. Palace aesthetics and agrarian conditions must be studied side by side.
