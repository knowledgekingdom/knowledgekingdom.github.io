The Ming dynasty ruled China from 1368 until the seventeenth-century crisis. Its bureaucracy, examination culture, land taxation and enormous urban economy did not disappear when a new house gained the capital. Late Ming communities participated in commercial expansion, production of porcelain and textiles, regional publishing and silver exchange. Yet climate pressures, military expenditure, fiscal tensions and rebellion created vulnerabilities. The problems differed by province and decade; a single story of effortless decadence is not adequate.

Silver was important in taxation and markets, although the amount and origins of imported bullion varied over time. American silver reached Asian markets through global routes, including transpacific Manila commerce, while Japanese silver and domestic commercial flows also mattered. A dependence on monetized revenues could make collection vulnerable to shifts in availability or purchasing power, though historians debate the exact contribution of silver to the Ming collapse.

Armed revolts and a weakening frontier situation culminated in crisis in 1644. Rebel forces under Li Zicheng entered Beijing; the Chongzhen emperor died; Manchu forces from the northeast, aided by alliances involving Ming commanders, entered the capital. Yet 1644 was not the date when all China instantly became peaceful Qing territory. Armed resistance, competing claimants and violent campaigns continued for decades. Distinguish the capture of a capital from the stabilization of a whole empire.
