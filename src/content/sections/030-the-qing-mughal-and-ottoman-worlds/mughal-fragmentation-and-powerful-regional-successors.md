Following Aurangzeb's death in 1707, Mughal emperors retained prestige while fiscal and military authority became increasingly decentralized. This is often called decline, but the term can conceal the vitality of regional powers. Hyderabad, Awadh and Bengal developed substantial autonomy while often acknowledging the emperor formally. Maratha forces and confederated interests, Sikh communities, Jat polities and other regional powers also reshaped political life. The result was not one empty space waiting for Europeans.

Nadir Shah's invasion and sack of Delhi in 1739 dealt a severe blow to imperial authority and prestige. Yet India remained a region of abundant agriculture, textiles, commercial networks and competing governments. A weakening center could coexist with prosperous districts, cultural innovation and new military states. Distinguish the fate of a ruling dynasty from the fate of an entire civilization.

As regional authorities gained control over taxation and armies, European companies found more openings for alliances and intervention. But company victories depended heavily on Indian finance, supplies, soldiers and factional politics. The transformation from trade to territorial power was a complicated political process, not an automatic consequence of superior European technology.
