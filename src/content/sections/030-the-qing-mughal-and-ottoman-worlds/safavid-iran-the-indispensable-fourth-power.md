A three-empire chapter cannot ignore Iran. The Safavid dynasty, founded in the early sixteenth century under Shah Ismail I, established a powerful state in the Iranian plateau and made Twelver Shi'ism a central public religious tradition. Safavid power affected Ottoman military planning to the west and Mughal diplomacy in the east. Battles over Iraq, the Caucasus and other frontier zones were not solely territorial; religious affiliation, trade routes, dynasty and elite politics all mattered.

The Battle of Chaldiran in 1514 gave the Ottomans a major victory over the Safavids, but it did not permanently settle the frontier or eliminate Iranian power. Under Shah Abbas I, Safavid authorities pursued military and fiscal restructuring, urban development and silk commerce. Armenian merchants, including communities connected with New Julfa, played significant roles in regional and global trade. Safavid artistic production and court culture interacted with Ottoman and Mughal traditions without losing distinctive features.

A map that groups the Qing, Mughal and Ottoman empires as separate continents of power can make them look like three independent stories. In reality, they inhabited a world of Persianate scholarship, religious networks, diplomatic exchanges, caravan trade and strategically important rival states. Iran was neither merely a buffer nor simply a religious adversary: it was an influential civilization and polity in its own right.
