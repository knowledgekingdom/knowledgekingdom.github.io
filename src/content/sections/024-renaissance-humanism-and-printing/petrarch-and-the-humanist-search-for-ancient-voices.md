Francesco Petrarca, commonly called Petrarch (1304–1374), is often treated as a foundational figure in Renaissance humanism. His importance lay partly in his love of ancient Latin writing, his efforts to recover manuscripts and his insistence that classical literature could teach people to think about moral life, fame and history. His correspondence with ancient authors offers a striking example of how scholars could treat texts as voices from a distant time rather than simply as repositories of timeless propositions. Petrarch also wrote Christian devotional and self-examining works; the humanist turn did not require rejecting religion.

Manuscript recovery was more demanding than finding a lost book in a box. Copies could be incomplete, texts might have been altered during transmission, and authorship might be uncertain. A humanist compared handwriting, wording, historical clues and alternative copies. Some writers hoped to restore classical Latin style; others studied ancient history to find models for contemporary politics. The recovery of Cicero's letters to Atticus, for example, complicated idealized impressions of a flawless Roman statesman by revealing personal and political uncertainty.

Humanism was not simply the philosophy that people, rather than God, occupy the center of the universe. That familiar formula confuses a historical educational movement with later ideas about secular individualism. Renaissance humanists were Christians, critics of clerical behavior, courtiers, republicans and members of many other overlapping communities. Their common enterprise was often the disciplined study of language, antiquity, rhetoric, history and moral conduct: the studia humanitatis.
