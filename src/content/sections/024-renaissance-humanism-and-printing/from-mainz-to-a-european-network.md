The expansion of European printing after the 1450s depended on movable people as much as movable type. Printers and trained workers established businesses in many centers, including German towns, Italy, France, the Low Countries, the Iberian Peninsula and England. Venice became especially important because it combined commerce, skilled labor, connections with universities and access to Mediterranean markets. Printers did not follow one perfectly coordinated route, and the presence of a press did not mean every local community immediately became literate.

Books printed before 1501 are commonly called incunabula, a bibliographical term meaning works from the infancy of European printing. Surviving copies help historians reconstruct production and distribution, but survival is uneven. Libraries and wealthy institutions preserved some kinds of book more reliably than cheap pamphlets used to destruction. A surviving catalogue also cannot tell us by itself whether readers believed, understood or discussed the words they owned.

What accelerated circulation was the combination of relatively standardized copies, booksellers' networks and readers seeking the same works in distant places. A scholar could refer to a printed edition more readily than to one unique manuscript, while still arguing about variants and errors. Religious institutions and universities purchased collections; merchants sold books at fairs; family libraries expanded where wealth and learning permitted. Printing both depended on and strengthened urban exchange.
