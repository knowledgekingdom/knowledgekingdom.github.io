The European story of printing begins long before Gutenberg. Printing from carved blocks developed in East Asia centuries earlier, while movable-type technologies were used in Chinese and Korean contexts. The oldest surviving book known to have been printed with movable metal type is the Korean Buddhist work commonly called Jikji, dated 1377. Its existence prevents the common but false statement that Europeans invented printing itself. The first relevant question is not who invented 'the press' in the abstract, but which processes and materials appeared in which societies and what made them useful. \[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-22" class="cite">22</a>\]

Chinese, Korean and European printing operated under different writing systems, commercial structures and state or religious institutions. The number and form of characters, available materials, demand for particular genres and traditions of manuscript production affected each technology's economics. Europe also adopted and expanded paper use through earlier transregional exchanges. By the fifteenth century, paper and urban literary markets helped make large-scale European book production feasible. None of these connections proves that one technical method was directly copied from another without evidence of transmission.

Historians distinguish the existence of a technique from its economic adoption. A method may work in one workshop but spread slowly because materials are costly or institutions do not support it. Others may choose an alternative method for good practical reasons. A technological history that moves from 'East invented it' to 'West perfected it' merely replaces one simplistic ranking with another. Instead, compare local goals, materials, scale and the specific characteristics of each surviving artifact. The global history of printing is an excellent reminder that invention is not the same as diffusion, and diffusion is not the same as social transformation.
