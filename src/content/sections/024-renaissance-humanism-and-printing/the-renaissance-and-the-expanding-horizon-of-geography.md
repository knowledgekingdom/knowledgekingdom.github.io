Maps, navigation and geographical writing became fields of intense interest in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. European scholars recovered and revised versions of Ptolemy's Geography, while mariners and mapmakers assembled information from ports, merchants and expeditions. Cartographic methods did not advance along one straight path. A mathematically structured inherited world map could be impressive yet inaccurate about places beyond the ancient author's knowledge. New travel reports could be fresh evidence but also contain error, fantasy and self-serving claims.

The broad geographical expansion discussed in Chapter 27 arose from economic ambitions, shipping technologies, political competition and long-standing Afro-Eurasian networks. It should not be explained by saying Renaissance curiosity automatically caused ocean voyages. Mariners made practical judgments about winds, currents and provisions; states sought commerce and power; merchants assessed risk. Geographical learning and maritime action influenced each other, but knowledge was uneven and often acquired through contact with peoples Europeans encountered.

We must also recognize the ethical horizon of these developments. European maritime expansion helped produce conquest, enslavement, disease transmission and profound transformations in the Americas and elsewhere. The Renaissance cannot be isolated in a gallery of beautiful paintings while ignoring the power relations developing in the same centuries. Yet it would be anachronistic to make every artist responsible for the policies of every prince. The goal is to understand overlapping histories without collapsing them into a single moral label.
