Architects and their patrons drew inspiration from Roman ruins and surviving texts, particularly Vitruvius. Columns, arches, proportion and classical orders could communicate learned authority, but architects adapted them to new building needs. Churches, palaces, hospitals and civic structures were different kinds of commission. The resulting architecture mixed practices inherited from medieval craft with new rhetorical claims to classical order. The Renaissance did not bulldoze Gothic Europe; it added another influential vocabulary.

A palace facade is a political statement as well as masonry. Regular windows may project discipline and wealth; a great entrance invites or controls access; a courtyard organizes the movement of servants, visitors and household members. Churches provided opportunities to express devotion, commemoration and civic prestige. The meaning of a building changed depending on whether one stood inside as a patron, passed outside as a taxpayer or worked on the scaffolding as a mason.

Consider a city's decision to construct an expensive public square. It may improve circulation and ceremonial space, but the funds compete with roads, hospitals, fortifications or debt repayment. The opportunity-cost framework from Chapter 3 applies here too: a celebrated landmark was never financially free. Architecture reveals how communities and elites prioritized visibility, function and legitimacy. Its survival can exaggerate the wealth and values of the past because ordinary wooden buildings, crowded homes and temporary structures have disappeared more readily from the record. \[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]
