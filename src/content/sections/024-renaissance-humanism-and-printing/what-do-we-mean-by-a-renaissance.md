The period is often introduced through a sequence of familiar names: Petrarch, Brunelleschi, Donatello, Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael. Such a list is a doorway, not an explanation. Historians distinguish the emergence of humanist literary study in fourteenth-century Italy, the experimental art and civic culture of fifteenth-century cities, and the changing politics and spread of these styles during the sixteenth century. These chronologies overlap. A printer in 1470 did not announce that medieval history had ended, and a village household in 1520 might have noticed very little of the intellectual arguments raging among scholars.

Renaissance is therefore an analytical label, not a universally dated event. Scholars often distinguish an early Italian Renaissance, a High Renaissance conventionally associated with artistic production around 1490–1520, and forms of northern Renaissance culture whose chronology and priorities differed. The categories should not imply that every object or thinker improved on a medieval predecessor. Gothic cathedrals, illuminated manuscripts, scholastic logic and Arabic medical texts did not become worthless because some Italian humanists preferred classical Latin rhetoric.

The label also risks treating Europe's cultural development as the story of humanity as a whole. Ming China, the Ottoman world, Persianate societies, African trading cities and Indian Ocean networks had their own intellectual and artistic histories. An Italian revival of Roman antiquity was not a global rebirth of knowledge. We can admire specific accomplishments without imposing a Western periodization on everyone.
