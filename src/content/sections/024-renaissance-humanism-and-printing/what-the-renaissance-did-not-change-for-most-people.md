A rural family in 1500 might cultivate land using practices familiar to earlier generations, negotiate rents, attend religious observances and worry about weather, war or disease. Print and humanist schooling affected households indirectly and at different speeds. Urban workers supplied essential labor while often possessing little authority over the religious and political controversies that later dominated history books. Early modern European society remained unequal by wealth, gender, legal condition and access to power.

New ideas did not erase violence, prejudice or authoritarian practices. Humanists admired Roman civic virtue while sometimes endorsing oligarchy or writing for powerful princes. Scholars could practice refined textual criticism yet accept stereotypes about foreign peoples. Artistic workshops were creative environments but also hierarchical workplaces. A portrait commissioned for elite prestige reveals aspirations more directly than the material circumstances of an ordinary resident. Historical balance requires looking at sources other than celebrated paintings and treatises.

Nor did a new cultural vocabulary instantly alter mortality, food supply or wages. Improvements in one city's buildings might coincide with crisis in another region. Printing could widen a particular intellectual community while excluding people who lacked the language or resources to participate. None of these limitations makes the Renaissance insignificant. It makes the transformation historically real rather than magical: achievements were uneven, conditional and tied to structures that could generate both creativity and domination. \[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]
