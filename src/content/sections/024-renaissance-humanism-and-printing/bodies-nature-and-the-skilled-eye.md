Renaissance artists studied anatomy, plants, light and movement for reasons ranging from devotional realism to professional competition. Sculptors and painters observed muscles, joints, gesture and the effects of fabric on bodily form. Some investigated dissections, although their opportunities and documentation differed. Leonardo da Vinci produced extraordinary anatomical drawings and mechanical studies, but his notebooks should not be treated as a completed modern scientific system. Many observations were precise; others remained speculative or unpublished in his lifetime.

The modern phrase Renaissance man suggests mastery of many disciplines, and figures such as Leonardo and Alberti indeed worked across conventional boundaries. Yet broad interests were only possible through networks of patrons, craftsmen, books and technical collaborators. The solitary genius model hides the labor of instrument makers, builders, apprentices and suppliers. Moreover, artistic studies of nature did not always pursue the same goal as later experimental science. An anatomically persuasive painting might contain deliberate idealization, religious symbolism or a political message.

The body was also a site of social norms. Renaissance images regularly celebrated elite male power, idealized classical forms and Christian subjects, sometimes combining them in tension. The depiction of women could reveal the preferences of male patrons more clearly than the lives of women themselves. A historical interpretation should therefore combine formal analysis of artistic technique with questions about audiences, sponsorship and the people portrayed or excluded.
