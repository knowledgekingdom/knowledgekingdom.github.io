Chapter 23 followed medieval towns, churches, kingdoms and long-distance exchanges. All four remained essential. European universities had emerged before the Renaissance. Scholars had worked through Aristotelian philosophy, law and theology; urban notaries developed exacting documentary skills; cathedral workshops trained specialists in geometry and materials; merchants used increasingly sophisticated accounting and correspondence. In addition, Byzantine scribes preserved and copied Greek texts, while scholarship in Arabic and other languages engaged with, translated and extended ancient mathematics, medicine and philosophy. Latin readers encountered some of this material through Iberia, southern Italy and other connected regions.

The university tradition and the humanist movement did not relate as a simple replacement of ignorance by reason. Some humanists disliked the technical style of scholastic debate and preferred eloquent Latin prose, history and moral philosophy. Yet the same educated societies could sustain both university disputation and humanist textual study. A doctor could consult Arabic-derived medical materials and humanist editions of Galen. A church administrator might employ methods associated with different schools. Criticism of inherited authorities was possible within medieval institutions, not merely after their disappearance.

Nor did ancient works remain completely unknown until 1453. Latin writers had studied Cicero and Virgil for centuries; Greek scholarship continued in Byzantium; translation programs preceded the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople. The arrival of additional Byzantine scholars and manuscripts was important, but the popular claim that the fall of the city suddenly transported all classical wisdom into a dark Europe is untenable.
