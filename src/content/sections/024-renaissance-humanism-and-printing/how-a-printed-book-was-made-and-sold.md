Imagine a printer planning a new edition of a Latin grammar. A scholar or publisher supplies a text; an editor prepares a copy; compositors set lines of metal type; proofreaders catch errors; press workers ink and print sheets; other workers dry, sort and assemble them; booksellers arrange distribution. The purchaser may then pay a binder or select a format. Some divisions of labor differed among towns and shops, and book production changed over decades, but the sequence illustrates why the finished book concealed many decisions and possible failures.

Printing did not eliminate mistakes. One incorrectly set letter could be repeated across hundreds of copies, while a correction introduced midway through a run could leave variant states of the same edition. Advertisements could overstate accuracy; publishers could pirate successful titles; authors might complain of distortion. A printed title page or authoritative-looking imprint made a text easier to identify, but it did not establish truth. In this respect the earliest age of mass-produced European books raises familiar modern questions about the relationship between repeatability and reliability.

An early printer also made commercial judgments. Would a theological textbook, romance, devotional tract, classical edition or legal manual sell? Which language would reach buyers? Could a press afford elaborate illustrations? Merchants and traveling sellers moved books along networks already used for other goods. Reputation mattered, as did censorship and local legal privilege. Printing was a technology of copying, but its history cannot be separated from the business model that determined which voices became widely available. \[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]\[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]
