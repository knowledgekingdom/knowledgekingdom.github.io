Suppose three handwritten copies of an ancient treatise survive. One reads 'the city chose peace'; the second reads 'the city sought peace'; the third omits the sentence. Which version deserves confidence? A responsible editor does not merely choose the most elegant wording. The scholar studies the copies' dates, shared errors, lines of descent and the ways scribes typically changed texts. If two copies derive from one flawed ancestor, their agreement may represent only one evidential line. This connects Renaissance philology directly with our earlier discussion of independent sources.

Humanist scholars sought older manuscripts, noticed anachronisms and criticized attributions. Lorenzo Valla's analysis of the so-called Donation of Constantine is a famous case: linguistic and historical features undermined the document's claim to be a fourth-century grant of vast authority from Emperor Constantine to the papacy. Valla's argument was not a modern laboratory experiment; it used the history of words, institutions and literary form to test provenance. The broader lesson is that a document's claims about its origin can themselves become objects of investigation.

Philology did not instantly produce universally accepted correct texts. Scholars had limited evidence, personal rivalries and religious constraints; some editions introduced errors. Even the idea of classical purity could lead editors to reject valuable medieval linguistic forms simply because they disliked them. Nevertheless, comparing copies and contextualizing language expanded the toolkit of historical criticism. It is difficult to understand later biblical scholarship, legal history or modern textual editing without this tradition. The enduring achievement was not to discover a final, unchanging canon, but to build habits that could expose mistakes in prestigious documents. \[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]\[<a href="/024-renaissance-humanism-and-printing/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]
