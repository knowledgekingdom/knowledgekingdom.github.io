The highlands of New Guinea give us an especially useful antidote to a wheat-and-barley-centered account. At the Kuk site, archaeologists have documented changing wetland land-use and cultivation practices, including early mounded cultivation and later drainage systems. UNESCO describes evidence for an independent agricultural development involving vegetatively propagated crops such as taro, bananas and yams, with clear cultivation features around seven thousand to six-and-a-half thousand years ago. Earlier traces of exploitation or cultivation remain more uncertain. \[<a href="/010-the-agricultural-revolution/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]

A root crop does not necessarily spread in the same way as a cereal. People may propagate a tuber, cutting or sucker rather than relying on dry grain seeds. Archaeological evidence is also different. Carbonized cereal grains can survive in some contexts, while many tropical plants leave less direct evidence. Researchers therefore rely on combinations of sediments, drainage features, plant microremains, environmental reconstruction and tools.

Imagine maintaining fields in a wetland. Your central innovation may be not a new seed but a ditch, a mound, and a reliable understanding of water. That produces a different rhythm of labor, a different landscape, and a different archaeological signature. This example also reminds us that the absence of well-preserved seeds does not mean the absence of sophisticated food production.

Kuk's significance extends beyond its age. It demonstrates that agriculture emerged through distinct human-environment experiments, not simply through the expansion of a single Eurasian 'package.' \[<a href="/010-the-agricultural-revolution/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]\[<a href="/010-the-agricultural-revolution/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]
