Early agriculture in East Asia followed several pathways. Millet cultivation became important in parts of northern China; rice cultivation and domestication developed in the Yangtze region and related ecological zones. These were not interchangeable crops. Their growing requirements, processing methods, field systems and associated settlement patterns differed.

Rice provides a revealing example of how the word <em>invention</em> obscures gradual change. A study published in <em>Science</em> in 2024 examined microscopic silica bodies formed in rice tissues, called <em>phytoliths</em>, and argued for stages of wild rice exploitation, pre-domestication cultivation and domestication across long periods in the Lower Yangtze region. Other researchers use different traits, sites, and definitions to date stages of rice domestication; some parts of the timing remain debated. The underlying lesson is not that every proposed date must be memorized. It is that ancient agricultural transitions are reconstructed from multiple imperfect indicators and did not necessarily happen all at once. \[<a href="/010-the-agricultural-revolution/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]

Millet is well adapted to several drier and more temperate conditions than wet rice cultivation. Over time, the combination of different crops supported different agricultural systems across East Asia. Some communities built substantial settlements and managed storage, while others blended cultivated and wild resources. Later states would transform these local systems through taxation, irrigation, labor obligations and large-scale trade, but those institutions did not exist in their final form at the beginning of cultivation.

When we say East Asia was an independent center of agriculture, we do not mean it was isolated from all contact forever, nor that every crop grown there originated within the same territory. Domesticates traveled. People exchanged seeds, animals and techniques. A region's early local innovation can coexist with long later histories of borrowing and adaptation.
