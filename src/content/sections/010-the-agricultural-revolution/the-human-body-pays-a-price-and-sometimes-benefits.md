Agricultural societies transformed diet and health. Dependence on a limited set of staple crops can reduce dietary variety or create deficiencies under some conditions. More frequent consumption of certain starchy foods can contribute to dental changes, especially when combined with other factors. Crowded settlements, closer contact with domesticated animals, water contamination and waste accumulation can alter exposure to infectious diseases.

But 'farming made people unhealthy' is too broad. Health outcomes differed by community, sex, status, age, diet and environment. Food production could support more reliable access to calories, enable infant and child survival in some conditions, and provide buffers through storage or trade. Migration, local ecological disease burdens, and changing cultural practices matter as much as the word 'agriculture.'

Archaeologists investigate ancient health through skeletal remains, teeth, stable isotopes, parasites, and sometimes ancient pathogen DNA. Each type of evidence has limitations. Skeletal samples overrepresent people who were buried and whose remains survived. An apparent decline in average adult height can reflect diet, childhood infection, demography and selection effects. The absence of a disease marker on bones does not prove the person never experienced disease.

Population growth creates another ambiguity. If a settlement contains more children, more graves and higher food production, is life better? The answer depends on mortality, fertility, migration, and living conditions. A society can become larger while average individual wellbeing stagnates or declines. Separating <em>how many people a system supports</em> from <em>how well each person lives</em> is one of the central analytical lessons of the agricultural transition.
