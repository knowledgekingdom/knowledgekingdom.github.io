Fields are ecological engineering. Clearing vegetation changes habitat, runoff and erosion. Herds affect grazing pressure and nutrient cycling. Irrigation can support crops in dry places but also create problems of waterlogging or salt accumulation if poorly managed. Terraces can stabilize slopes when well maintained yet require collective labor and long-term upkeep. Agricultural fires, drainage and repeated cultivation alter the composition of species across a landscape.

These processes are neither uniformly destructive nor automatically sustainable. Some land-management systems supported resilient production for very long periods; others exhausted soil, depleted water or intensified erosion. Agricultural techniques depend on rainfall, slope, crop selection, fallow periods, tools, labor and institutions. A modern environmental judgment about a prehistoric practice requires reconstructing the local ecosystem and the alternatives actually available to its inhabitants.

Consider the New Guinea wetlands again. Ditches and mounds helped transform growing conditions, but they also required maintenance. Consider a river floodplain: alluvial soils can be productive, while floods impose risks. Consider the Sahel: crops and mobility strategies must respond to rainfall uncertainty. Geography is not a backdrop behind human history. It is part of the evolving system in which human decisions create new opportunities and vulnerabilities.

The environmental reach of agriculture expanded over millennia. Later societies would bring larger fields, grazing systems, irrigation canals and trade-connected crops to new areas. The environmental consequences were cumulative and varied. From a long-term perspective, agriculture was one of humanity's most consequential ways of reshaping other species and the land itself.
