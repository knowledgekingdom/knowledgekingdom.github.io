Counterfactual history asks what might have happened under different conditions. It cannot provide a definitive alternative timeline, but it can expose hidden assumptions. Imagine a world in which no community had developed sustained food cultivation. Human populations would probably have been distributed differently; many later cities and states would not have arisen in the forms we know. But we cannot conclude that people would have stopped inventing, trading, creating art or developing sophisticated institutions. Their ancestors had already done all those things.

A second counterfactual is more interesting: what if agriculture had developed in the same regions but remained one strategy among several rather than becoming dominant? Some communities did retain mixed economies, and others turned away from certain crops or settlements when conditions changed. The transition to farming was not irreversible everywhere. Drought, conflict, migration, exhausted soil or better opportunities could prompt new strategies.

These alternatives help us avoid <em>teleology</em>: the mistaken idea that the past exists to produce the present. The people who began managing wild grain were not attempting to invent the modern supermarket, global capitalism, or government ministries. They solved immediate problems, maintained relationships and pursued goals of their own. The long-range results exceeded anything they could have foreseen.

The correct lesson is not 'agriculture was good' or 'agriculture was bad.' It is that agriculture changed the trade-offs. It enabled more densely populated societies and extraordinary new forms of collective life while exposing people to new burdens, diseases, inequalities and environmental risks. Its evaluation depends on whose experience we study and which consequences we consider.
