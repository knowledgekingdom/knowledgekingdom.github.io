Across the Americas, crops such as squash, maize, beans, potatoes, quinoa, cassava and other useful plants were cultivated or domesticated in different places and periods. We must not compress this diversity into one Mesoamerican event or imagine that every crop was domesticated in the same manner. In Mesoamerica, maize descended from a wild grass relative called <em>teosinte</em>. Its eventual dramatic transformation is one of the most familiar examples of sustained plant selection, but the precise geography and chronology of cultivation and diversification have been refined repeatedly as new archaeological and genetic evidence appears. \[<a href="/010-the-agricultural-revolution/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]

The familiar later combination of maize, beans and squash became a powerful agricultural system in many communities. The plants can complement one another nutritionally and agronomically, yet the 'three sisters' arrangement should not be projected backward unchanged onto the very first cultivators. Agricultural techniques, crop varieties and culinary traditions developed over long periods across societies with different environments.

In the Andes, potato cultivation and other highland crops supported agricultural systems in challenging altitudes and climates. Terracing, local knowledge of frost and soils, and storage strategies became important in later periods. Tropical lowland regions, meanwhile, followed different pathways with crops such as cassava. Some societies combined gardening, fishing, hunting and forest management without constructing the large grain-growing landscapes familiar from old European history books.

The Americas make a large conceptual point: <em>a continent is not a single laboratory</em>. Independent experimentation, diffusion, exchange and ecological adaptation all played roles. Even after domestication, crops continued evolving under selection and as people carried them into new climates.
