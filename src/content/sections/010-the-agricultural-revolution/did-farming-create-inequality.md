It would be tempting to say that once people owned land and stored grain, wealthy landowners appeared and political equality ended. That claim is too quick. Inequality exists in many forms: unequal food, unequal prestige, unequal household size, differences in ritual authority, control over labor, and the inheritance of productive assets. A settlement might exhibit strong social differences in one dimension and modest differences in another.

Recent comparative research across a large archaeological dataset finds that persistent household wealth inequality often increased <em>long after</em> cultivation began, rather than arising immediately at its onset. Differences were associated with factors such as land becoming a limiting productive resource, growing settlement hierarchies and political scale. The exact measures and their interpretation still require caution: house size is an imperfect proxy for all forms of wealth, and different regions preserved different kinds of evidence. \[<a href="/010-the-agricultural-revolution/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]

This matters because agriculture created <em>possibilities</em>, not a fixed social program. Some households could gain advantages by controlling fertile plots, irrigation points, herds, labor or exchange. Others could maintain strong sharing norms and institutions that reduced wealth accumulation. Larger populations and inter-settlement authority might eventually weaken some balancing mechanisms, but communities responded in different ways.

Varna again provides an informative contrast. Richly furnished burials in the fifth millennium BCE reveal conspicuous differences in some people's treatment after death. Yet the earlier agricultural communities in southeastern Europe were not simply smaller versions of Varna. The interval between early farming and later status displays matters. To explain inequality, we must track institutions and economic change across time rather than imagining seeds mechanically producing social classes.

Property itself has many forms. A person may hold the right to use a field without being able to sell it, pass it to any heir, or exclude all neighbors. Collective ownership, family claims, seasonal use rights and obligations to local authorities can coexist. Calling these arrangements 'private property' without specifying the actual rights risks hiding their diversity.
