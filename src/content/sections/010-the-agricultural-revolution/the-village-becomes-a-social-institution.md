A village is more than houses standing near one another. As settlement becomes persistent, neighbors encounter one another repeatedly. Families share pathways, refuse areas, water points, workshops, fields and obligations. A dispute over an animal or a field boundary can persist across seasons. A damaged wall may affect adjacent houses. Common storage may require rules about who contributes and who receives food in a hard year.

Consider Çatalhöyük on the Anatolian Plateau. UNESCO describes occupation in the eastern mound from approximately 7400 to 6200 BCE, with densely packed houses, roof access and a rich material record of domestic and symbolic life. The architecture is striking: people often entered homes from above, and house clusters were arranged differently from a modern street grid. Archaeological interpretations of the community's equality and authority remain under discussion, so its unusual form should not be used as proof that every early settlement was ruled by priests, chiefs or a central government. \[<a href="/010-the-agricultural-revolution/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]

Repeatedly rebuilding homes in the same place creates archaeological layers called a <em>tell</em> or settlement mound. To a future archaeologist, a town's long life may appear as walls above walls, floors above floors and deposits of broken objects. These layers record patterns of stability and change, but also losses: much ordinary speech, conflict resolution, and emotional life has vanished.

Permanent settlement changes the meaning of distance. When a group is mobile, access to a resource can be negotiated by changing camp or route. When people invest in fixed fields, wells and buildings, moving can be costly. A neighboring household's behavior becomes more consequential. That may encourage cooperation and shared rules, but it can also intensify arguments about land, labor, water, and access to food.

Some large communities arose while still maintaining foraging activities. Other settled villages were strongly agricultural. Many communities remained seasonally mobile. Thus 'village' and 'farmer' describe overlapping but not identical categories. The important transformation is the growing investment in places, infrastructure and recurring relationships.
