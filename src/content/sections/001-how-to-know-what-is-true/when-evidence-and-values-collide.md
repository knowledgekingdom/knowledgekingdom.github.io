Many discussions become heated because participants are answering different questions. One person asks, “Did a particular surveillance policy reduce crime?” Another asks, “Even if it works, is the intrusion on privacy acceptable?” A third asks, “Who is most likely to be monitored?” These questions are related, but the first concerns empirical effectiveness, the second moral legitimacy, and the third the distribution of burdens and rights.

You cannot settle a moral disagreement simply by producing a spreadsheet. Yet values without facts can become empty. If a policy is justified as a way of saving lives, information about whether it saves lives matters. If two people both value freedom but disagree about a law, their disagreement might concern what freedom means, what harms are likely, or what trade-offs are acceptable. Clarify those differences before debating outcomes.

An educated person can describe competing ethical positions fairly without pretending that all conclusions are equally persuasive. For example, an argument for strict limits on speech might stress protection from severe harm; an argument against broad restrictions might stress the risk of abuse and the value of dissent. The strength of each argument depends partly on empirical facts and partly on priorities that require moral reasoning. Honest discussion identifies both.

This is also where good manners serve intellectual purposes. If disagreement becomes an exercise in humiliation, participants protect their dignity instead of reconsidering evidence. Useful questions sound like: “Which part of this is established?” “What other explanation could fit?” “What outcome are we optimizing for?” and “What evidence would be enough to reconsider?” These are not polite decorations. They make the underlying disagreement visible.
