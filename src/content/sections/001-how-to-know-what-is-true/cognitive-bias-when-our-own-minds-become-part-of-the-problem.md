You can master every source-checking technique in this chapter and still misjudge evidence if your identity or emotions are tied to the answer. Human beings are not detached computing machines. We often notice information that confirms our existing view, interpret ambiguous details in its favor, and remember striking examples more easily than ordinary ones. These tendencies are common; they do not make any one person foolish or malicious.

<strong>Confirmation bias</strong> is the selective search for, interpretation of, or recall of evidence that supports a belief. If you suspect all politicians are corrupt, you may immediately save every corruption scandal and barely notice quiet examples of competent administration. If you believe one economic system guarantees prosperity, you may focus on its successes and explain away its failures. <strong>Motivated reasoning</strong> describes the wider way our goals, loyalties, or desired conclusions can influence how we process arguments.

A subtler habit is <strong>reflexive disagreement</strong>: treating the act of challenging a claim as evidence of independent thought. Questioning can be intellectually valuable. But automatically looking for a flaw in every unfamiliar assertion can become another bias—an assumption that a claim is probably wrong because someone else confidently presented it. Genuine independence means being capable of agreement when warranted and disagreement when warranted. Neither response is a virtue by itself.

The <strong>availability heuristic</strong> makes memorable examples feel more typical than they are. A widely reported plane crash may loom larger in your imagination than the much larger number of ordinary flights completed safely. A spectacular financial fraud can make every investment seem fraudulent; a dramatic investment success can make risk seem negligible. Stories can reveal possibilities, but only appropriate data tell you how common those possibilities are.

Then there is <strong>false equivalence</strong>: concluding that because two people disagree, both positions deserve the same confidence. Sometimes they do. Sometimes one is strongly supported and the other rests on rumor. Open-mindedness requires taking seriously the possibility of error, not suspending judgment indefinitely whenever anyone objects.

## A method for testing your preferred conclusion

First, state the claim as clearly as you can. Second, write the strongest reasonable evidence <em>against</em> it before collecting further support. Third, ask whether you would judge the same evidence differently if it favored an opponent's view. Fourth, distinguish a change in the facts from a change in your values. Finally, make a provisional judgment and explain its uncertainty.

For example, suppose you believe a proposed urban policy will harm small shops. Instead of searching only for examples of business closures, look for comparable towns where the policy produced mixed outcomes: some businesses lost delivery access, others gained pedestrian customers, and effects depended on the local design. Your original worry may survive, become narrower, or change. The point of inquiry is not to punish yourself for starting with a view; it is to stop the starting view from deciding the outcome in advance.
