A witness says, “I saw the car drive through a red light.” A security camera shows a red car entering an intersection. A traffic sensor records a vehicle crossing its line at 16:42. These observations are related but not identical. A witness supplies a personal report, a camera gives a selected visual field, and a sensor produces a measurement based on calibration and software rules. None is a transparent window into everything that happened.

Human perception is useful but selective. Attention narrows what we notice. Memory is reconstructive rather than a perfect recording. When people describe an event, their language may mix what they saw with what they later learned. An honest eyewitness can be mistaken without being a liar. Conversely, a camera can show real footage that lacks the beginning or end needed to understand it. A sensor can be precise in the wrong unit or produce systematic error because it was miscalibrated.

Data are not magic facts floating above the world. Someone chooses what to measure, how to define categories, which instrument to use, and how to record missing observations. A report that unemployment was 5 percent may be accurate under a stated official definition and still omit people who wanted work but were not counted in the labor force. A school may report higher examination scores without revealing that the tested population changed. Knowing how a number was produced is part of knowing what it means.

Three useful questions are: <strong>What was actually observed? What did the measurement method exclude? What interpretation was added afterward?</strong> These questions are especially important when pictures or numbers feel self-explanatory. A photograph of an empty market tells you that the market was empty at one moment and in one frame. It does not by itself tell you whether the local economy is collapsing. A graph with a dramatic upward line does not tell you whether the vertical axis starts at zero or whether the measurement definition changed.

Observation becomes stronger when it can be checked against independent kinds of evidence. Archaeologists, for example, may combine inscriptions, material remains, settlement patterns, and environmental traces. Astronomers combine light measurements, models, and observations from different instruments. In everyday life, combine receipts with bank statements; combine a quoted speech with the full recording; combine a claimed government announcement with the actual published decision.
