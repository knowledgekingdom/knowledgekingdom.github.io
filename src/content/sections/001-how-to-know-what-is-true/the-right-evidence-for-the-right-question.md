People often ask whether a source is “reliable” as if reliability were a single property. A better question is: <strong>reliable for what?</strong> A contemporary diary can be valuable evidence that an individual experienced fear during an epidemic. It is weak evidence for the total mortality rate in an entire country. An official mortality table may help with the total but reveal little about a family's feelings. A high-quality satellite image can document a building's physical presence but cannot tell you why its architect chose a design.

Different fields require different forms of inquiry. In history, researchers examine provenance, authorship, chronology, material remains, and the relationship between surviving accounts. In experimental science, researchers might compare treatments and control groups or repeat measurements under specified conditions. In economics, they may study administrative data, surveys, natural experiments, and mathematical models. In law, they distinguish the standards for admissible evidence, burdens of proof, and the legal question being decided. In philosophy, they analyze the implications of concepts and arguments, alongside empirical findings where relevant.

There is no all-purpose ranking in which “firsthand” always defeats “secondhand” or “scientific” always defeats “historical.” The best evidence depends on the claim. A primary source can be an excellent guide to what a ruler claimed and a poor guide to the ruler's actual popularity. A later scholarly study may be more dependable because it compares the ruler's propaganda with tax records, private letters, and the accounts of rivals. The Library of Congress recommends analyzing primary sources through observation, reflection, and questioning rather than treating them as self-interpreting objects. \[<a href="/001-how-to-know-what-is-true/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]

It helps to understand <strong>provenance</strong>: the chain of origin and handling through which a source reached you. For a painting, provenance may involve ownership records. For a dataset, it means collection and processing methods. For a video, it means who filmed it, where, when, and whether the clip was edited. A plausible claim without recoverable provenance remains harder to assess than a claim tied to documented records.

Three separate tests should guide you. <strong>Relevance:</strong> does the material address the question? <strong>Credibility:</strong> is it likely to be accurate about that question? <strong>Independence:</strong> does it offer fresh confirmation, or merely repeat another source? A hundred sites repeating one mistaken report provide less independent support than two genuinely separate investigations.
