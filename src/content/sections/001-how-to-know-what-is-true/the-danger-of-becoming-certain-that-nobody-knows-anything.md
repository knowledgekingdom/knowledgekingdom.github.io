Learning about error can make every source look suspicious. You discover that witnesses can misremember, statistical analyses can mislead, experts can disagree, and scientific papers can be revised. The temptation is to conclude that knowledge is impossible and that everyone merely has opinions. But the conclusion does not follow. The fact that maps distort Earth does not mean all maps are useless. The fact that historians interpret incomplete evidence does not mean the past is unknowable. The fact that scientists revise explanations does not mean there is no difference between a serious research program and an unsupported assertion.

The solution is <strong>calibrated trust</strong>: confidence proportionate to evidence, open to revision, and aware of limits. You can accept a widely corroborated historical date while questioning a confident theory about the motives of a ruler. You can trust the overall body of evidence behind a well-established medical practice while asking whether a new treatment works for a specific patient. You can understand a model's usefulness while knowing that it leaves out some features of reality.

It is worth emphasizing that skepticism itself is not an achievement. Skepticism becomes valuable when it is disciplined by a willingness to learn and by consistent standards. Someone who challenges every claim made by an opponent but swallows every rumor supporting their own side is not thinking independently. Someone who calls every expert untrustworthy while accepting an anonymous video without checking it has not escaped authority; they have merely chosen a worse authority.

As this book travels through ancient civilizations, modern states, economics, science, and the universe, return to a small question: <strong>What kind of evidence would we expect if this explanation were true—and do we have it?</strong> Sometimes you will find abundant evidence. Sometimes you will find a strong but incomplete case. Sometimes the appropriate conclusion will be “We do not yet know.” Those are three different intellectual achievements, and a reliable thinker knows how to distinguish them.
