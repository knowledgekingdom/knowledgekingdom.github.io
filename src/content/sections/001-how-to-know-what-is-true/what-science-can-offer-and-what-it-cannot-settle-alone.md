Science succeeds not because individual scientists are immune to error, but because disciplined inquiry creates opportunities for errors to be discovered. Researchers pose questions, build models, measure, compare, criticize, and revise. Their results enter a community where instruments, assumptions, and findings can be challenged. Science is neither a collection of unquestionable facts nor a method for proving any statement with absolute certainty.

Textbooks often portray a single scientific method as a short loop: ask, hypothesize, experiment, conclude. Real inquiry is richer. An astronomer may learn from observations that cannot be repeated on demand. A geologist reconstructs deep time from rock layers and isotopes. A biologist might conduct controlled experiments. A historian of science may investigate how laboratory decisions influenced a discovery. The central shared expectation is that claims should connect transparently to evidence and be open to serious criticism.

A <strong>hypothesis</strong> is a proposal that can be tested or compared with evidence. A scientific <strong>theory</strong> is not merely a casual guess; it is a developed explanatory framework that organizes findings and makes sense of a domain. Models simplify: a climate model, a model of a chemical reaction, and a model of an economy each leave out details to illuminate selected relationships. Useful models are judged by their assumptions, performance, scope, and limitations.

<strong>Peer review</strong> can catch flaws before publication; it is not a stamp guaranteeing the result is true. A <strong>systematic review</strong> attempts to identify and assess a body of relevant studies using explicit methods; its strength still depends on the studies included and whether the synthesis was conducted properly. A single dramatic paper and a mature body of mutually supporting research should not receive equal confidence.

An important distinction from the U.S. National Academies is between <strong>reproducibility</strong>, producing consistent computational results with the same data and analytic procedures, and <strong>replicability</strong>, obtaining consistent results from new data collected to address the same scientific question. The words are used differently in some fields, so always check the author's definition. The Academies also emphasize that a failed replication is not, by itself, conclusive disproof. \[<a href="/001-how-to-know-what-is-true/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]

## Why changing one's mind is a strength

Imagine a research group initially believes an ancient settlement is 4,000 years old. New dating measurements reveal that the samples were contaminated. After correction, the best estimate changes. This revision does not show that all archaeological dates are worthless. It shows an important advantage of methods that expose assumptions: a well-supported conclusion can be improved when the evidence improves.

Some scientific claims have extraordinary stability, such as the approximate shape of Earth or that organisms inherit genetic material. Others concern the size of a subtle effect, a newly detected astronomical event, or an early-stage treatment and remain more provisional. A sensible reader asks not “Has any scientist ever been wrong?” but “What is the accumulated evidence for this particular conclusion, and how would we recognize an error?”
