Imagine a town council discussing a new public library. Someone says: “The library received 12,000 visitors last year.” Someone else says: “The building caused the town to become more educated.” A third replies: “The library was worth the money.” A fourth predicts: “If we extend opening hours, visits will increase.” All four statements refer to the same institution, but they ask for different kinds of reasoning.

The attendance number is a <strong>descriptive or empirical claim</strong>. It might be checked against counting systems and attendance definitions. The education statement is a <strong>causal claim</strong>. It needs evidence that learning outcomes changed because of the library, rather than because of a separate improvement in schools or the economy. The sentence about worth is an <strong>evaluative judgment</strong>, which depends partly on facts and partly on what people value: access, literacy, cost, fairness, and alternative uses of public money. The opening-hours statement is a <strong>prediction</strong>, requiring assumptions about people's behavior.

These categories can appear inside one sentence. “The tax cut made families richer, so it was fair” contains an empirical premise about incomes, a causal assertion about the tax change, and a moral conclusion about fairness. A thoughtful conversation becomes much clearer once the parts are separated. People who disagree about fairness may agree entirely about what happened. People who agree about values may disagree about the data.

Now consider a common habit in debates: using a fact to smuggle in a value. “Country A produces more goods per person, therefore its social system is better.” Perhaps higher output matters greatly to you. But what about health, liberty, distribution of wealth, environmental costs, security, and leisure? A numerical observation does not decide how those goals should be weighed. The reverse mistake occurs when someone rejects inconvenient evidence because it seems to favor a policy they dislike. Facts and values interact; they are not interchangeable.

<strong>Practice in the margin.</strong> Write a sentence about a topic you care about. Underline the part that is directly testable. Circle the words that express a causal explanation, forecast, or moral priority. If you cannot tell which is which, refine your sentence before defending it.
