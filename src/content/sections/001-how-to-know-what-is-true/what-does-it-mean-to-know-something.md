You probably know that Earth orbits the Sun, that the Second World War ended in 1945, and that ordinary drinking water contains hydrogen and oxygen. You do not know these facts because you personally watched Earth complete its orbit, attended the surrender ceremonies, or analyzed a water sample. You rely on an enormous network of observation, instruments, records, teachers, archives, and researchers. That reliance is not intellectual weakness. It is the condition that makes civilization-wide knowledge possible.

Philosophers have spent centuries asking what <em>knowledge</em> is. For our practical purposes, begin with three layers: a <strong>claim</strong> is something that might be true or false; <strong>evidence</strong> is information that bears on its truth; and a <strong>judgment</strong> is how strongly the evidence supports believing it. A person can sincerely believe something false. A person can correctly guess a fact without having a good reason. Being right by luck differs from having a reliable method of getting things right.

Take a simpler example: “It rained in Sofia yesterday.” The statement has a factual answer. A photograph of wet pavement is some evidence, but it could show street cleaning or melted snow. A time-stamped record from a reliable weather station directly addresses precipitation, although even instruments can fail. Independent station records and reports may strengthen the conclusion. Your certainty should rise not because the story sounds plausible, but because evidence increasingly rules out alternatives.

The word <em>true</em> does not mean “widely repeated,” “personally meaningful,” “written by an expert,” or “politically convenient.” Equally, <em>uncertain</em> does not mean “there is no answer.” Sometimes the world contains a definite fact while the evidence available to us is incomplete. A lost letter was either written or not; we may simply lack the surviving proof. A historical battle occurred in a particular setting even if the date in surviving chronicles is disputed.

An essential distinction follows: <strong>truth concerns the world; confidence concerns our information about the world</strong>. Confusing the two leads to two opposite mistakes. Dogmatism treats your current belief as unquestionable reality. Radical cynicism concludes that because some conclusions are uncertain, all explanations are equally unreliable. Good judgment occupies the demanding middle: it recognizes varying degrees of support.

## A practical ladder of confidence

Use ordinary labels rather than pretending to precision you do not possess: <strong>well established</strong>, <strong>strongly supported</strong>, <strong>plausible but contested</strong>, <strong>uncertain</strong>, and <strong>unsupported or contradicted</strong>. These are not fixed numerical categories. They are prompts to state what you know and what would change your view. “A treaty was signed on this date” may be well established. “This treaty was the single most important cause of a war” is a broader interpretation, usually requiring more argument. “A politician secretly intended that war” needs evidence of intentions, not merely knowledge of what happened afterward.

The point is not to hedge every sentence. You can speak confidently when the evidence deserves it. The point is to avoid using the same tone of certainty for a measured fact, a plausible interpretation, and a personal preference.
