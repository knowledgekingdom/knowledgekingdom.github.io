## Investigation A: the photograph that proves too much

A striking photograph shows a coastal street underwater. The caption says, “This town has been permanently abandoned because the sea level rose overnight.” A first pass identifies the factual core: a street appears flooded. A second pass asks when and where the image was taken, what the tide and weather were doing, and whether the photographer's framing excluded nearby dry streets. A third pass searches local reports and a longer sequence of images. Even if the photograph is genuine, the caption may confuse a temporary storm surge with permanent sea-level change.

The fair conclusion is not necessarily that coastal flooding is unimportant. It is that <strong>this photograph alone does not establish this particular causal claim</strong>. The real-world phenomenon and the reliability of the caption must be evaluated separately. Rejecting an exaggerated example does not prove the opposite general conclusion. Someone can misuse a real issue in an inaccurate post.

## Investigation B: “The ancients could not have built it”

A video points to the enormous stones of an ancient monument and asserts that its builders must have possessed lost technology. It may be true that modern visitors do not immediately understand how the stones were cut, transported, or positioned. But a gap in the narrator's knowledge is not direct evidence for any proposed alternative. Archaeologists would investigate tool marks, quarrying areas, unfinished structures, transport possibilities, historical records, experiments, and comparative buildings. Some details may remain genuinely uncertain. The responsible conclusion can be both admiring and provisional: ancient societies achieved remarkable feats; our reconstruction of their techniques varies in completeness.

Notice how the burden of explanation works. “I cannot imagine how they did it” is a statement about the speaker's imagination. “No people of that era could do it” is a broad empirical claim demanding substantial evidence. “Therefore a lost civilization supplied machines” is an additional causal hypothesis requiring its own independent support. Three sentences that feel like one leap are actually three distinct claims.

## Investigation C: “A tax cut paid for itself”

A minister announces that a tax reduction brought in more revenue than expected, proving that the tax reduction paid for itself. This may be true in some setting, false in another, or impossible to establish from the information given. A careful reader asks what revenue would have been without the policy, whether the economy grew for other reasons, whether inflation increased nominal receipts, whether other taxes changed, and what time period is being considered.

A strong investigation separates three questions: <strong>Did revenue rise? Did the tax cut cause the difference relative to a credible counterfactual? Was the policy desirable once other costs and benefits are included?</strong> The first is descriptive, the second causal, and the third partly evaluative. A confident speech may slide across all three without noticing the change. A careful citizen will not.
