From Arctic tundra to tropical rainforest, from high Andean plateaus to dry southwestern deserts, the Americas presented many distinct ecological settings. In Mesoamerica—the broad cultural region encompassing much of present-day Mexico and parts of Central America—maize, beans and squash became important crops. In the Andes, communities domesticated and cultivated potatoes, quinoa and other plants while managing camelids such as llamas and alpacas. Eastern North American societies cultivated several local seed crops before maize became more important in many regions. Tropical forest communities managed useful trees, wetlands and soils in ways that varied across place and time. These differences mattered enormously for settlement patterns and transport.

Unlike Eurasia, the Americas lacked a comparable collection of large domesticated riding and draft mammals. But that fact cannot explain everything, and it certainly does not measure people's intelligence. The llama, for example, was a valuable pack animal in the Andes, and humans developed technologies appropriate to their environments. Movement through deserts, mountains, lake systems, rivers, coastal waters and forests could be highly organized even where wheeled vehicles did not dominate transport. A wheel-shaped object might exist as a toy while road transport remained more effective by human porter, canoe or pack animal. Technology is a collection of responses to constraints, not a single ladder.

The human settlement of the Americas began long before any named civilization in this chapter. Archaeological evidence indicates that Indigenous ancestors reached the hemisphere in the late Pleistocene, but the timing and pathways remain active subjects of research. The people who later built Caral, Teotihuacan, Cahokia or Cusco were not members of one continuous, centralized society; their histories unfolded across millennia. Whenever a map colors an entire continent one shade, ask what time slice, political unit or cultural network the color is intended to represent.
