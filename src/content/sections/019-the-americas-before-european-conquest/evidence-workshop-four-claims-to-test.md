<strong>Claim A: 'All Maya cities were abandoned in the ninth century.'</strong> To test it, ask which cities, which regions and which archaeological indicators. Many southern lowland centers lost dynastic power around that time, but northern Maya societies and living Maya communities make the universal claim false. Better wording would identify particular southern centers and distinguish the end of royal inscriptions from total population disappearance.

<strong>Claim B: 'The Inka built a huge empire without any recordkeeping.'</strong> This ignores khipus and the evidence of complex administration. Yet the opposite assertion—that every surviving cord can be read as a complete written historical narrative—also overreaches. Records need not resemble European manuscripts to be records. Explain precisely what forms of information scholars can establish and what remains undeciphered or contested.

<strong>Claim C: 'The Amazon was empty forest before modern settlement.'</strong> Archaeological excavations, earthworks, soil studies and LiDAR survey contradict a blanket claim. But those findings do not justify a newly inflated assertion that all Amazonian regions were densely urbanized. A careful historian evaluates regional scale, dates, the distribution of surveyed areas and the difference between roads, fields and permanent residence.

<strong>Claim D: 'A handful of Spaniards defeated the greatest American empires by themselves.'</strong> This leaves out Indigenous armies, political rivalries, disease, siege, logistical constraints and the difference between capturing a capital and controlling an entire region. It also hides the long wars and negotiations that followed. Replace the heroic shortcut with a causal network explaining why events unfolded as they did and why outcomes varied.
