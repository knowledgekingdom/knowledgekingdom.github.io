Agriculture in the Americas was neither a single import nor one simultaneous event. Different communities domesticated different plants over long periods, and the transition to greater dependence on farming was uneven. In parts of Mesoamerica, maize became a dietary foundation; its history involved generations of selection from wild ancestral grasses. In the Andes, tubers, grains, irrigation and camelid herding developed in ways adapted to altitude and seasonal conditions. Human societies combined agriculture, fishing, gathering and hunting in different proportions. A large ceremonial gathering place did not necessarily require every visitor to be a full-time farmer.

Food security depended on practical knowledge: which fields held moisture, how to store harvests, when to plant in relation to rains, and how to share labor. Maize agriculture could sustain dense settlements but was vulnerable when drought, soil exhaustion or political disruption affected production. Andean terraces could reduce erosion and create usable plots on steep slopes, but they required maintenance. Fisheries and marine foods supplied some coastal communities with major sources of protein and commercial goods. The emergence of monuments often tells us about coordinated labor and symbolic authority, but it does not prove that one political institution controlled an entire region.

Some early monumental sites challenge familiar assumptions. Poverty Point in present-day Louisiana was built and used around 1700–1100 BCE by communities heavily dependent on hunting, fishing and gathering. Its large earthworks show that monumental construction could occur without the same agricultural package that supported other later cities. Caral and other sites in Peru's Supe region also remind us that early complexity took several pathways. The question is not merely 'who was first?' but what combination of food production, exchange, belief and organization sustained each place.
