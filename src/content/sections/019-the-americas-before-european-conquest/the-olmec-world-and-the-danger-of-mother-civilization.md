On the Gulf Coast of present-day Mexico, peoples whom scholars collectively call the Olmec produced major ceremonial centers and extraordinary sculpture during much of approximately 1200–400 BCE. At San Lorenzo and La Venta, artists carved colossal basalt heads, sometimes transported from distant stone sources. These objects testify to skilled labor, transport planning and powerful patrons. Other works in jade and greenstone circulated within far-reaching aesthetic and ritual traditions. Such evidence shows complexity without granting us a complete political chronicle.

Older accounts sometimes called the Olmec a 'mother culture' that single-handedly invented Mesoamerican civilization. The phrase suggests a neat family tree in which all later peoples simply copied one ancestor. The real history of influence was multidirectional: different societies borrowed, adapted and independently developed symbols, architecture, agricultural practices and institutions. There is no reason to treat every familiar Mesoamerican feature as an Olmec invention. Just as importantly, the modern name 'Olmec' does not give us the ancient people's own self-designation.

A colossal head may plausibly portray a ruler or notable individual, but that interpretation is not a complete biography. Its headdress, carving style, location, associated architecture and geological origin can be studied; the person's name often cannot be recovered. Compare this with a later Maya inscribed monument that may record a specific dynastic event. The difference reveals a central skill in ancient history: the strength of our claims depends on the surviving <em>kind</em> of evidence, not merely on how impressive an artifact looks.
