Not every influential society built a capital resembling Teotihuacan or Cusco. Along the Pacific Northwest coast, rich aquatic ecosystems, woodworking traditions and maritime technologies supported complex communities with distinct political systems and ceremonial life. In California, the Great Basin, the Plains and the Eastern Woodlands, Indigenous peoples used different food resources, trade routes and governing arrangements. Some communities were mobile, others maintained substantial settlements, and many mixed seasonal movement with lasting ties to particular places. Mobility is not a sign of political simplicity.

Across parts of the eastern Woodlands, networks of kinship, diplomacy and decision-making held communities together in forms that cannot be captured by the presence or absence of stone palaces. Confederacies, councils and clan institutions developed through diverse historical circumstances. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy is especially important to later North American history, but assigning its formation one precise archaeological founding year is difficult. Oral historical traditions and political continuity should be studied carefully rather than forced into a chronology borrowed from European states.

In the Arctic and subarctic, Indigenous societies developed technologies of clothing, shelter, fishing, hunting and navigation adapted to demanding conditions. The Thule Inuit expansion across parts of the Arctic in the later first and early second millennium CE is one important example of mobility and technological adaptation. Such histories belong to the preconquest Americas just as much as imperial capitals do. The absence of enormous temples in one ecological zone tells us little about the knowledge required to survive and maintain communities there.
