European arrival produced catastrophic and varied consequences, but it did not unfold in one identical way. In central Mexico, the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521 involved Spanish forces and numerous Indigenous allies opposed to Mexica power, as well as disease, siege and the disruption of food supplies. In the Andes, conflict within Tawantinsuyu, epidemics and political divisions shaped the Spanish invasion beginning in the 1530s. In the Caribbean, coercion, disease and displacement were especially rapid. Farther north, the terms and timing of European conquest differed substantially, and many Native polities remained independent for long periods.

None of this diminishes European violence or the destructiveness of colonial structures. It corrects an inaccurate two-sided story in which Native peoples act only as victims or as an undifferentiated army. Indigenous leaders weighed alliances, resisted enemies, protected communities, adopted new tools, or negotiated under highly unequal conditions. Their decisions did not guarantee favorable results, and written colonial records often distort their motives. Later chapters will address conquest, disease, Atlantic slavery and colonial systems in depth; this chapter aims first to establish the <strong>worlds that already existed</strong>.

The word 'discovery' is therefore misleading when used to imply a previously unknown or uninhabited continent. European explorers encountered places already known intimately to their inhabitants. Even after political defeats, Indigenous languages, identities, legal claims, agricultural knowledge and religious traditions did not simply vanish. History after conquest is not a replacement of one population by another but a difficult, ongoing story of change, persistence, violence and adaptation.
