The ancient Maya writing system combines signs representing words with signs indicating sounds. This is a sophisticated logosyllabic system, not merely a set of pictures used as decoration. Its modern decipherment has been a major scholarly achievement, allowing researchers to read names, titles, rituals and dates once inaccessible. Not every text is perfectly understood; preservation is uneven, and some signs and language forms remain disputed. Nevertheless, the writing decisively refutes the idea that Maya monuments were anonymous ruins without recorded histories.

Maya calendars are also often misunderstood. The Long Count tracks elapsed time from a conventional mythic starting point and can anchor dates in a historical framework. A 260-day ritual cycle and a 365-day solar cycle had different functions. Calendar arithmetic and astronomical observations were connected with political and religious life, but they did not amount to supernatural prediction. The much-publicized assertion that a Maya calendar 'predicted the end of the world' in 2012 was a modern distortion, not an accurate description of Maya historical knowledge.

Imagine reading an inscription stating that a ruler performed a ritual on a precise date. That supports a claim about an official record: someone wanted the act remembered. It does not automatically confirm that every subject approved of it, that the ritual produced the result the ruler claimed, or that court ideology reflected daily opinion. <strong>Decipherment increases our evidence, but source criticism still matters.</strong> The combination of scripts, archaeology and living-language research allows a far richer reconstruction than any one method alone.
