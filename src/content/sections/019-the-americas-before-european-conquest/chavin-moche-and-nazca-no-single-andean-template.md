Long after Caral's main florescence, new ceremonial and political formations emerged. Chavín de Huántar, in the Peruvian highlands, became an influential ritual center in the first millennium BCE. Its carved imagery, architecture and connections with other communities suggest the circulation of religious practices, styles and perhaps elite networks. But shared imagery alone does not prove that Chavín ruled a geographically uniform empire. We should ask whether influence moved through pilgrimage, prestige, trade, coercion or some combination rather than assuming one mechanism.

On the northern Peruvian coast, societies grouped under the label <em>Moche</em> flourished over much of the first millennium CE. Impressive adobe ceremonial structures, elaborate burials, painted ceramics and complex irrigation reveal concentrated power and technical skill. Yet the Moche should not automatically be treated as one centralized state at every moment. Regional centers had distinct trajectories. Some decorated vessels depict warfare, prisoners, ritual scenes or mythic figures; archaeology and burial contexts help scholars evaluate their meaning, but artistic scenes are not photographs of daily events.

To the south, Nazca traditions are famous for enormous geoglyphs cut or cleared into the desert surface. Researchers study their construction, visibility and relation to ritual movement, water, social identity and landscape. Extraordinary geometry is not evidence of extraterrestrial builders. A feature visible from a hillside or through planned ground-level construction need not have been designed for aircraft. The correct method is to examine location, dating, associated materials and plausible human construction processes, distinguishing promising hypotheses from unsupported certainty.
