From a political center at Cusco, the Inka rulers built Tawantinsuyu—often translated as 'the four regions together'—into a huge Andean empire during the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. Inka armies, alliances, marriage politics, road building and administrative reorganization extended imperial influence over very different peoples. The imperial state did not govern every locality in exactly the same way. Local authorities often remained indispensable as mediators, while some communities resisted or endured compulsory resettlement.

Imperial power was exercised through demands for labor and goods, public works, political symbolism and the strategic placement of administrators. The <em>mit'a</em> system organized rotations of labor obligations in state projects and production, though its operation varied by place and the word's later colonial uses require distinction. Labor could build roads, terraces, storehouses and fortifications. Redistribution from state facilities supported armies, officials and ceremonial occasions, but it did not mean that every person's needs were guaranteed equally. Hierarchy, coercion and social obligations must remain in the narrative alongside engineering achievements.

The empire's pace of expansion also created vulnerabilities. Its rulers had to manage long distances, differing political loyalties and the problem of succession. A centralized system could coordinate massive projects while creating powerful disputes over authority. By the time Spanish expeditions reached the Andes, the state was facing a civil war between royal claimants, while epidemics associated with the broader post-contact world had already destabilized many communities. Foreign conquest exploited fractures that existed inside the empire; it was not the triumph of a handful of Europeans over an otherwise united population.
