When Teotihuacan's former dominance weakened, central Mexico did not become an empty stage. Settlements, alliances, migrations and competing regional authorities reshaped its political geography. Tula became associated with Toltec traditions that later societies remembered with fascination and that modern researchers must distinguish from myths projected backward. In Oaxaca, the Zapotec history of Monte Albán and later Mixtec kingdoms reveals another powerful political and artistic world with its own dynasties, records and regional networks. A map centered only on the Basin of Mexico would erase much of this variety.

The Mixtec produced rich pictorial manuscripts, distinctive metalwork and historical traditions, while Oaxaca's highlands and valleys supported long-lived communities and complex political alliances. Different traditions of pictorial notation, carved monuments and oral remembrance existed alongside the Maya script; the Americas did not have a single uniform method of recording history. Nor did one collapse trigger the next civilization like a switch. Centuries of overlapping local developments connected some societies and separated others.

By the late postclassic era, central Mexico contained numerous powerful communities, including the Tepanec, Acolhua, Tlaxcalans, Mexica and others. Their conflicts cannot be explained as 'the Aztecs versus everyone else.' Alliances changed, political obligations were negotiated, and the eventual Spanish conquest exploited these divisions. Understanding this crowded political landscape is necessary before we can understand the Mexica-led empire.
