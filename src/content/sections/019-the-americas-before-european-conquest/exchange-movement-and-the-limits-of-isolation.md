The Atlantic and Pacific oceans separated the Americas from most Afro-Eurasian state systems for long periods, but 'isolated continents' is misleading if it suggests that American societies lacked internal connections. Trade circulated obsidian, shells, copper, feathers, cotton, ceramics, salt and many other materials across regional networks. Maya states interacted with central Mexican cities; Andean valleys exchanged goods across altitudes; Mississippian settlements participated in wider social and commercial systems; Caribbean voyagers crossed maritime spaces in canoes. Each network must be reconstructed on its own evidence.

Objects found far from their geological origin can demonstrate movement, but not automatically the route or the identity of every carrier. An obsidian blade in one city might have traveled through several markets. A style copied across regions can indicate influence without proving conquest. Textiles can preserve patterns of exchange and identity, while language families and ancient DNA sometimes provide complementary insights into historical movement. These methods cannot be used as shortcuts around questions of chronology, collection history and social context.

Communities were also connected by belief, diplomacy and conflict. A diplomatic marriage, a shared ceremonial style, a caravan route and a military occupation are different mechanisms. To describe all of them as 'cultural diffusion' without specifying how they worked is not much of an explanation. The systems-thinking habits from Chapter 7 are useful here: identify the nodes, the flows, the institutions that maintain them, and the circumstances that cause routes to expand or weaken.
