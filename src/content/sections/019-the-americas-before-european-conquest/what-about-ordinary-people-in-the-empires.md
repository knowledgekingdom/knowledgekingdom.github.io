Elite monuments dominate many surviving photographs, but empires were made and sustained by households. Farmers managed terraces or chinampas, potters shaped everyday vessels, builders organized materials, and traders or porters moved goods. Families negotiated marriages, inheritance, labor and local obligations within larger political systems. Women performed different kinds of productive, ritual and political work; roles varied by culture and social status, and the most famous royal women are not reliable representatives of everyone. Childhood, illness, hunger, seasonal work and local festivities shaped experiences no less than succession disputes.

The surviving record distorts visibility. Elite tombs are often spectacular because they required wealth; simple houses can be less immediately impressive even when they reveal more about ordinary diets and crafts. A market scene described by a conqueror may include detailed observations but also misunderstand local exchange. Archaeologists use residues in pottery, household floors, botanical remains and settlement distribution to fill gaps. A reconstructed diet for one neighborhood must not be generalized into a uniform imperial menu.

Social hierarchy also depended on coercion, and some societies practiced slavery or forms of unfree labor. We should not romanticize preconquest America as universally egalitarian, nor import European categories without examining the local institution. Tribute, local status, captive-taking and obligation could work differently in distinct communities. A serious comparison asks who controlled land, who performed labor, what rights were recognized, how authority was challenged and how we know these things.
