The Inka used <em>khipus</em>—arrangements of cords, knots, colors and positions—to record and communicate information. A great deal of research supports their role in numerical accounting and administration, and scholars continue to explore their broader informational capacities. They should not be dismissed as merely primitive 'memory aids' because they do not look like alphabetic books. Equally, we should not claim that every khipu has been deciphered as a full narrative text. Evidence and interpretation must be carefully separated.

Spanish-era chroniclers described khipu specialists and administrative practices, but their accounts were written after conquest and often served political or religious purposes. Surviving khipus, archaeological contexts and Indigenous knowledge are therefore invaluable points of comparison. Another limitation is that European colonial destruction and later collection histories have affected what survives. Absence of a readable archive is not proof of absence of organized government, while the presence of state records does not give us direct access to everyone governed.

A useful comparison is with Maya writing. Maya inscriptions can preserve particular names and historical dates, while khipus frequently encode structured numerical relationships that modern scholars can analyze differently. Neither medium can be understood by reading its appearance alone. Remember the core principle from Chapter 4: <strong>a source's form determines which claims it can support</strong>. Comparing these technologies broadens the definition of historical information without treating every unfamiliar system as interchangeable with alphabetic writing.
