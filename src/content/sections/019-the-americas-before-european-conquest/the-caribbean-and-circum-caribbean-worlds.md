Before 1492, the Caribbean islands were home to numerous communities with diverse histories and identities, including peoples commonly described in sources as Taíno and Kalinago. Agricultural production, fishing, canoe travel, exchange and networks of settlement connected islands to one another and to mainland regions. Cassava processing, cotton production, ceramics and ceremonial spaces were significant in various local settings. Older colonial stereotypes that portray island peoples as uniformly passive or permanently hostile obscure that diversity.

European accounts begin to proliferate at precisely the moment of violent disruption. Their descriptions are valuable sources but were shaped by conflict, conversion campaigns, imperial ambition and language barriers. The political and social histories of islanders cannot be reconstructed solely from what outsiders said they encountered. Archaeology, comparative linguistics and living Indigenous identities complicate earlier narratives of total disappearance. Descendants and people asserting Indigenous Caribbean identities remain part of the region's present.

The Caribbean deserves its own place in this chapter because its story is not simply a preface to Columbus. People navigated sea routes and maintained economies long before European ships arrived. The violence of colonization will be examined in later chapters, but the intellectual starting point must be that a complex world existed beforehand.
