In the region now divided among parts of the United States and Mexico, ancestral Pueblo communities developed irrigation, dry farming, distinctive architecture and complex social and ceremonial connections. Chaco Canyon, in present-day New Mexico, became an important regional center especially between approximately 850 and 1250 CE. Great houses, roads and shared material traditions show coordinated labor and long-distance connections. The site's exact balance of political, ritual and economic functions remains debated, and modern descendant communities have their own knowledge and relationships with the landscape.

Mesa Verde and other settlements preserve evidence of how communities adapted to changing conditions. Large cliff dwellings are visible today, but people also occupied open villages and farmed wider landscapes. During the late thirteenth century, major settlement shifts occurred in parts of the region, influenced by drought, social change, conflict and other pressures studied by archaeologists. This was migration and reorganization, not the disappearance of 'mysterious people.' Modern Pueblo communities, including Hopi and others, retain traditions linking them to ancestral places.

The phrase 'Ancestral Pueblo' is generally preferable to obsolete outsider terminology, although names and affiliations should still respect specific communities. An archaeological site is not only an object for tourists: it may remain culturally and spiritually significant to descendants. Historians can learn from architecture and dates while also recognizing that the descendant community's relationship to a place is not reducible to a laboratory result.
