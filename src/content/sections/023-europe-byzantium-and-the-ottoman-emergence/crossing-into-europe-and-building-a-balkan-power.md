Ottoman access to the European side of the straits developed through involvement in Byzantine conflicts and military footholds. Gallipoli became a durable Ottoman base after 1354, when an earthquake and associated disruption affected the area. Geography was crucial: the Dardanelles offered a route between Anatolia and the Balkans, and reliable crossing points enabled reinforcements, settlement and communication. Ottoman commanders took advantage of regional rivalries while competing Bulgarian, Byzantine and Serbian leaders often lacked the resources or shared aims needed to resist together.

Under Murad I and Bayezid I, the Ottomans extended influence through campaigns, vassal relationships, garrisons, marriage and administration. Edirne (Adrianople) became a major Ottoman political center in Europe. Military success mattered, but an expanding realm also needed arrangements for land revenue, local elites, religious communities and permanent armed forces. The precise timing and form of practices later associated with timars, household troops and other institutions evolved over time; we should not imagine they sprang into existence fully developed in a single reign.

For many communities, the transition involved burdens and adaptations rather than one uniform moment of conquest. Some elites entered Ottoman service; some towns resisted; some populations experienced violence, forced movement or taxation, while existing religious and legal institutions continued in altered circumstances. A policy described in a central decree may not have been applied identically in every district. Ottoman rule in the Balkans was a historical process made through local decisions, not a photograph of unchanging domination.
