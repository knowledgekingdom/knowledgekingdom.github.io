The people whom modern textbooks call Byzantines generally identified their polity as Roman. The label Byzantine is a later historical convenience derived from the older name Byzantion. Its capital, Constantinople, was founded as an imperial center in the fourth century. Greek became the dominant language of government and much of culture, yet Roman law, state traditions and imperial claims endured. This continuity should not erase transformation: the society of Basil II around 1000 differed enormously from Justinian's sixth-century world, and both differed from the city under Constantine XI in 1453.

An imperial capital concentrated court ritual, military leadership, church authority, diplomacy and economic activity. Hagia Sophia embodied religious and political prestige, but the city was also a place of artisans, markets, sailors, migrants and competing interests. Imperial families used marriage, appointments, ceremonial precedence, gifts and diplomacy to negotiate with neighbors. Byzantium was not merely defensive; at some moments it conquered extensive territory, while at others it preserved power by recognizing limits and playing rivals against one another.

This history teaches a caution about retrospective labels. Modern national stories sometimes portray Byzantium as exclusively Greek, exclusively Orthodox or as a foreign power in an otherwise nationally defined Balkans. Its population and political network were more varied. Likewise, the empire's opponents cannot be reduced to undifferentiated outsiders. A careful account follows how rulers and subjects described themselves, while recognizing that their descriptions were claims that did not settle every question of identity or loyalty.
