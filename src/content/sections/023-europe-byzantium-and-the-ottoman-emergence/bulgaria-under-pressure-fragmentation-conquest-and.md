After the death of Ivan Alexander in 1371, the Bulgarian political landscape was divided among multiple centers, including the Tarnovo and Vidin tsardoms, as well as regional powers along the Black Sea. This fragmentation cannot serve as the sole explanation of Ottoman success: Ottoman logistical and military capacity, neighboring rivalries, shifting vassal obligations and the resources of rulers all mattered. But it reduced the likelihood that a unified response could be mobilized. The Ottoman capture of Tarnovo in 1393 was a major political and symbolic rupture. The fate of Vidin is connected with the defeat of the crusading army at Nicopolis in 1396, although territorial and dynastic consequences unfolded in stages.

Neither 1393 nor 1396 describes every local community's immediate experience. Different districts had accepted tributary dependence, suffered campaigning or negotiated continuity at different times. Church and monastic institutions, vernacular traditions and family networks survived in altered forms. At the same time, the loss of sovereign courts, changes in land and tax relations and the realities of conquest profoundly affected lives. It is anachronistic to speak as if a modern nation-state were occupied all at once under a single national border; it is equally wrong to downplay the destruction of political autonomy because medieval government differed from modern nationalism.

The Bulgarian case can be studied without turning history into a competition of victimhood. Compare fiscal records, chronicles, later monastic memory and material remains. What do they say about government, religious practice and ordinary households? How did accounts written centuries after conquest reframe earlier events? The central teaching point is continuity through transformation: political sovereignty could disappear while languages, local identities and institutions underwent adaptation rather than instantaneous erasure.
