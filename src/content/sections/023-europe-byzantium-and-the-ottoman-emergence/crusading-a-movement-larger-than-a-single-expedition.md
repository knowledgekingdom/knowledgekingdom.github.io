In 1095 Pope Urban II called for an armed expedition associated with aid to eastern Christians and the recovery of Jerusalem. The First Crusade reached and captured Jerusalem in 1099, establishing Latin-ruled states in parts of the eastern Mediterranean. Crusading developed into a religiously authorized practice with vows, institutions and varied targets, not one uninterrupted war. Its participants could seek spiritual reward, patronage, land, adventure, family obligation or escape from local conflict; motivations changed among individuals and expeditions. Those explanations must not imply that all participants had the same reasons or that their goals were morally equivalent to the suffering inflicted.

The Muslim societies confronted by crusaders were divided into different principalities and dynasties, not a single coordinated bloc. Rulers including Nur al-Din and Saladin later built coalitions, while their priorities also involved local political struggles. Jerusalem's recovery by Saladin in 1187 was a turning point, but crusading continued. The movement reached Iberia, the Baltic and sometimes other Christian populations, demonstrating that an ideology can be extended far beyond its initial context. Massacres, assaults on Jewish communities, siege devastation and forced dispossession belong to this history alongside diplomacy, trade and cultural contact.

Sources need careful reading. Latin chronicles describe vows and victories from their authors' perspectives; Arabic narratives and Jewish accounts may remember different forms of terror and loss. Artifacts and legal records illuminate enduring communities better than a soldier's heroic account. The central causal problem is how a movement justified as holy warfare became entangled with dynastic ambition, commerce and the rivalries of churches and states.
