Under Orhan, Osman's successor, Ottoman power expanded beyond a cluster of frontier strongholds. Bursa fell to Ottoman forces in 1326 and became an important center for rule and patronage. Other towns and fortresses followed, among them Nicaea (İznik) in 1331 and Nicomedia (İzmit) in 1337. Dates are useful markers, but what mattered institutionally was the conversion of military success into revenues, permanent administration and urban networks. Managing a town required officers, markets, judges, religious institutions and relationships with local inhabitants.

The rulers built on multiple traditions rather than inventing government from nothing. Turkic, Islamic, Persianate, Byzantine and local administrative practices furnished tools and personnel. Scholars disagree on the precise early chronology of some institutions; later descriptions can project fully developed systems onto a much less formal fourteenth-century frontier state. A cautious narrative therefore traces changes across reigns rather than attributing every successful practice to Osman or Orhan. Ottoman authorities learned to mobilize forces and collect revenues on a scale that made further conquest possible.

The state also entered Byzantine dynastic politics. A marriage alliance between Orhan and Theodora, a daughter of John VI Kantakouzenos, illustrates how cross-border relations involved negotiation as well as warfare. Ottoman military support could help one Byzantine faction against another and simultaneously increase Ottoman opportunities. The lesson is uncomfortable for simplistic civilizational stories: the rising Muslim-ruled dynasty was not always separated from Christian imperial politics by a solid barrier. Their leaders could be allies in one crisis and adversaries in the next.
