Chapter 22 introduced Mongol military campaigns and the western Eurasian polity commonly known as the Golden Horde. Their relevance to southeastern Europe did not end with the invasion waves of the 1240s. Tribute demands, diplomatic ties, regional military pressure and the movement of mobile groups influenced Balkan governments for decades. Bulgarian rulers in particular negotiated with or fell under pressure from powers associated with the Jochid sphere. Yet no simple statement that the Golden Horde directly ruled all Bulgaria accurately captures these relationships.

The Black Sea and lower Danube formed routes connecting merchants, armies and pastoral communities. Cumans and other mobile groups participated in the military and political histories of Byzantine and Bulgarian rulers, while later Tatar communities were integrated into changing local arrangements. The geography that made steppe cavalry formidable also encouraged exchange between coastal ports, river valleys and inland pastures. Control over a crossing or market could be as important as possession of a distant symbolic capital.

This regional perspective complicates a familiar map. The Balkans were not only a meeting place between Latin Europe, Orthodoxy and Islam. They were also connected to wider northern Eurasian politics. Population movements, taxation and succession struggles reshaped local opportunities. We should distinguish an invasion, tributary dependence, dynastic marriage, mercenary service and long-term settlement, rather than using one word—Mongol influence—to imply the same experience everywhere. The theme connecting this chapter with the previous one is that empires could shape political behavior well beyond territories they directly administered.
