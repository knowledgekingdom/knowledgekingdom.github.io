Imagine a historian comparing three different kinds of surviving material: an account by a participant or near-contemporary observer; a later Ottoman narrative celebrating the sultan's achievement; and architectural evidence from the city's fortifications. These records do not answer the same questions. A participant's testimony may preserve the fear and sequence of particular moments while exaggerating enemy numbers or misremembering timings. A later dynastic history may reveal official political ideals better than the precise conduct of every soldier. Masonry can demonstrate repair, damage and construction phases without naming the commander responsible.

A responsible investigation begins by separating observations from interpretation. When did the author write? Who was the intended audience? What could the witness see? Did later authors copy one another? What does the material record independently establish? Disagreements do not automatically mean that nothing can be known: the date and overall outcome of the conquest are well established, while particular episodes remain debated. The same method applies to Manzikert, Kleidion, Adrianople and Kosovo, whose later legends can sometimes obscure what contemporary evidence actually supports.

Construct a claim of your own: 'Ottoman artillery was the only reason Constantinople fell.' Ask which comparisons would test it. Would the city have survived with more soldiers, resupply or relief? Could the besiegers sustain operations without secure sea crossings? Those counterfactuals cannot be observed directly, but they show why a single technological explanation is inadequate. Historical understanding grows when several types of evidence are made to challenge a tidy story.
