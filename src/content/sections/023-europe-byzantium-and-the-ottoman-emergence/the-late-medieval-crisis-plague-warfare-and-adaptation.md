From the fourteenth century onward, famine, warfare and epidemic placed enormous strain on parts of Europe. A series of poor harvests in the early fourteenth century contributed to severe food shortages in regions of northern Europe. The Black Death reached western Eurasia in the later 1340s and killed an exceptionally large share of the population, though the percentage varied geographically and estimates remain uncertain. Plague was not one strictly European event; it belonged to the connected Afro-Eurasian world described in Chapters 21 and 22. Later epidemic waves continued to affect communities.

Population loss altered labor supply and bargaining. Some surviving workers demanded better terms or moved; some landlords sought to impose wage or mobility restrictions. Different governments responded through taxes, statutes, courts and coercion. In England and France, the Hundred Years' War was not continuous fighting every day for a hundred years; it was a long sequence of conflicts involving dynastic claims, shifting alliances and social costs. Sieges, raids and taxation damaged communities, while military techniques and administrative capacity also developed. Popular revolts expressed grievances that combined fiscal burdens, elite power and local pressures.

Neither catastrophe nor recovery had a single trajectory. Mortality could increase the bargaining power of surviving laborers in some areas while reinforcing forms of dependency elsewhere. Towns could prosper despite disease, and families could be ruined while rulers expanded fiscal institutions. We should not describe the crises as the straightforward death of medieval society or as a beneficial economic reset. Lives lost cannot be converted into progress merely because later labor markets changed.
