Historians divide European history into early, high and late medieval periods, but these labels organize evidence rather than describe borders that people themselves observed. A customary sequence places the early Middle Ages after the political fragmentation of the western Roman empire; the high Middle Ages around the eleventh through thirteenth centuries; and the late Middle Ages through the fifteenth century. Regional developments rarely obeyed the same timetable. In the east, the Roman government in Constantinople continued. In the west, rulers and bishops assembled new institutions from Roman legacies, local custom and relationships with neighboring peoples.

Our focus is approximately 800–1453, with earlier background where essential. The word Europe did not name a single medieval state, religion or culture. Latin Christian kingdoms and principalities coexisted with Greek-speaking Orthodox communities, Muslim polities in Iberia, Jewish communities in many towns, pagan and newly Christianized societies around the Baltic, and many borderlands of mixed practice. The Baltic coast, Iberian Peninsula, northern Italy, Anglo-Norman lands, Balkans and Anatolia had different ecologies and political structures. Their populations also changed through migration, conquest, commerce and cultural borrowing.

Maps of medieval Europe are often misleadingly neat. A king could claim a territory without holding every castle; a monastery might own land scattered across jurisdictions; a town might enjoy privileges inside a lord's principality; and one ruler might recognize an emperor or another monarch for one purpose but not another. The first practical lesson is to separate a colored map's statement of nominal lordship from evidence about who actually collected taxes, settled disputes and could send soldiers.
