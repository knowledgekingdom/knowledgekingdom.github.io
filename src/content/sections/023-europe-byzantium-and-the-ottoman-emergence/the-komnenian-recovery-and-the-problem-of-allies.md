The accession of Alexios I Komnenos in 1081 marked a major reorganization of Byzantine government and foreign policy. His successors, including John II and Manuel I, rebuilt military and diplomatic influence in important regions. Rather than treating the twelfth century as a flat descent toward 1204, we should recognize periods of recovery and sophisticated statecraft. Emperors navigated the Seljuk frontier, aristocratic factions, Balkan rivals, Italian maritime cities and crusader armies. Venice and other commercial powers obtained privileges that could provide ships and assistance while reducing imperial control over parts of trade.

The First Crusade moved through imperial territory after Alexios had sought assistance in a changed strategic environment. Byzantine and Latin leaders made promises, contested conquered cities and often misunderstood one another's objectives. Some crusader commanders recognized temporary Byzantine claims; others developed their own states. Cooperation was possible, but it rested on fragile expectations. The account written by Anna Komnene, Alexios' daughter, provides a remarkable learned court perspective on this world, yet its admiration for her father and political viewpoint require scrutiny.

Diplomacy across the Balkans could involve Hungarian, Serbian, Bulgarian and Cuman interests, while the empire managed rivalries with western powers. A strong Byzantium was not necessarily a conqueror of every neighbor; it could be a broker of alliances and a supplier of prestige. By the end of the twelfth century, internal divisions and external pressures intensified. The transition to 1204 therefore illustrates that capable institutions and impressive cultural life do not guarantee security when the fiscal, dynastic and military foundations of a state come under strain.
