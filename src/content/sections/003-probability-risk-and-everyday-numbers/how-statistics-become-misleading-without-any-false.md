Numbers can be accurate and still frame reality selectively. Consider a headline: “A village's thefts have doubled!” If reports rose from two to four, the increase is indeed 100 percent. But the absolute count is small, and the change may reflect variation or altered reporting. Another headline says “City A has twice the accidents of City B.” Without population, traffic volume, and severity, the comparison is incomplete. No calculation has to be wrong for the impression to be misleading.

<strong>Selection bias</strong> appears when the people or events in a dataset are systematically different from the group about which we want to generalize. A survey about satisfaction answered only by enthusiastic customers can overstate average satisfaction. A study of successful entrepreneurs cannot tell us how often their strategies succeed unless we also examine those who tried similar strategies and failed. This is <strong>survivorship bias</strong>: the missing unsuccessful cases can be crucial.

A <strong>confounding variable</strong> is connected to both an apparent cause and an outcome in a way that complicates causal inference. Ice-cream sales and drownings may rise in summer not because ice cream causes drownings, but because warmer weather influences outdoor swimming as well as purchases. A correlation can be genuine without the most tempting causal story being true. Chapter 1 introduced causal evidence; Chapter 6 will explain statistical techniques for comparing groups.

Even genuine rates can reverse in the aggregate when groups have different compositions—a pattern sometimes called <strong>Simpson's paradox</strong>. Imagine two hospitals treating different proportions of easy and difficult cases. The hospital with higher success within each difficulty group could nevertheless show a lower success rate overall if it treats many more difficult cases. This does not make arithmetic unreliable. It shows why the group structure matters. Always ask whether a comparison is like-for-like and what categories have been combined.

When a politician, advertisement, or influencer makes an exciting claim, take the numbers seriously enough to ask what they exclude. Beware the false choice between gullibility and cynicism. We do not need to dismiss all statistics because some are misleading. We need to reconstruct them and ask whether they support the intended conclusion.
