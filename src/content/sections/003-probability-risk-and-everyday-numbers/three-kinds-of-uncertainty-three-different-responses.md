A business owner worries about tomorrow's weather. A historian wonders how many people lived in a poorly documented ancient city. A scientist confronts a new phenomenon without enough observations. Each faces uncertainty, but of different kinds. For everyday reasoning, it helps to separate <strong>random variation</strong>, <strong>limited information</strong>, and <strong>uncertainty about the model itself</strong>.

Random variation occurs even in a well-understood process. Fair coins produce unpredictable short sequences despite having a simple probability model. Limited information occurs when we know too little about the particular case or parameters: perhaps we have only six months of sales data. Model uncertainty arises when we are unsure whether our assumptions are right: perhaps next year's economy will look unlike previous years, or a security threat changes the behavior we were measuring. No amount of algebra can force a weak model to become reliable.

The responses differ. To reduce sampling uncertainty, gather more representative data. To improve a model, test assumptions, examine alternatives, and use external validation. To protect against unavoidable randomness, diversify, build reserves, or design robust procedures. Some risks can be measured reasonably well; others are genuinely difficult to quantify. The phrase “we do not know” can be the start of disciplined inquiry rather than the end of it.

Imagine two proposed bridges. One has a known small risk of closure during seasonal floods. The other's foundations rely on an untested geological assumption. The first problem may be addressed with well-understood engineering probabilities; the second demands more investigation. Treating both as if they were merely numbers in a spreadsheet could hide the more important issue: whether the evidence justifies the calculation.
