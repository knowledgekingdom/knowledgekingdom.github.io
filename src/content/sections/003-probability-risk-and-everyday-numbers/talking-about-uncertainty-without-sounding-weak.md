People sometimes avoid admitting uncertainty because they fear sounding uninformed. The opposite is often true. A carefully qualified conclusion demonstrates that you know where the evidence ends. Compare “this will definitely happen” with “the evidence suggests this is more likely than not, although the estimate depends on a few assumptions.” The second is more useful if those assumptions can be named and checked.

Try using three levels of expression. For a well-established fact, be direct: “A fair die has six faces.” For a plausible interpretation, say why: “On the available data, longer opening hours may help this shop, but staffing costs could cancel the gain.” For a genuinely unsettled question, identify competing explanations and the evidence that could distinguish them. Avoid mechanical percentages when there is no respectable way to produce them. Saying “I am 73.4 percent certain” does not improve a judgment that rests on a hunch.

A thoughtful conversation about a risk usually needs at least five pieces of information: what event is being considered, over which time period, in which population, compared with what baseline, and with what consequences. A sixth is often essential: how trustworthy is the estimate? If any of these are missing, the first job is clarification rather than persuasion.

Uncertainty also invites intellectual humility. Your first estimate may be wrong. New evidence may justify a revised view. In an honest forecast, changing your mind after learning something is a feature, not a defect. The same habit makes you a better reader of history and a better participant in political discussions, where confident stories often run ahead of what evidence can support.
