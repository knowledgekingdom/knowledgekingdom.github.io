Suppose your friend says there is a 70 percent chance of rain tomorrow. The following day is dry. Was your friend's estimate necessarily wrong? No. A prediction of 70 percent rain still allows a 30 percent chance of no rain. Judging a probabilistic forecast by a single outcome is like deciding that a fair die is defective because one roll showed a six. To test the forecast, we would need many comparable occasions for which the forecast was 70 percent, then examine how often rain actually occurred.

A useful distinction is between an <strong>event</strong> and a <strong>forecast about the event</strong>. The event either occurs or does not, but the forecast attempts to summarize what is known before the outcome. A weather forecast might incorporate atmospheric observations and an ensemble of mathematical models; a prediction about a game could rely on historical performance; a rough estimate of business demand might combine past sales and a special-event calendar. All three are attempts to describe uncertainty, but the models, evidence, and assumptions differ greatly.

Probabilities range from 0 to 1, often written as percentages. Under a clearly specified model, a probability of 0 means an event cannot occur, and 1 means it must. A probability of 0.2 means a 20 percent chance, or 1 chance in 5. But do not confuse the number with a schedule. A 20 percent chance does not mean the event must occur once every five opportunities. Chance has no duty to produce a perfectly even short sequence.

Even the question “What probability should I assign?” has more than one sensible approach. In a fair six-sided die, symmetry gives six equally likely faces, so any specified face has probability 1/6. With football, inflation, a diplomatic crisis, or a new business, probabilities are usually estimates built from incomplete information. Some analysts use long-run frequencies, others statistical models, and others judgments updated as evidence arrives. The important habit is to explain what the number refers to and how much trust its method deserves.

A forecast also has a time horizon. “A recession has a 30 percent chance” is incomplete without an economy, a definition of recession, and a period. “A 30 percent chance of measurable rain at this location during the next 12 hours” is much more testable. Probability is not a magic number attached permanently to an event. It is always attached to a particular question and body of information.
