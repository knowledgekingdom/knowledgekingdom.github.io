A person may willingly pay a premium whose price exceeds the expected amount of money they will claim. At first that seems mathematically foolish. But imagine a house that could suffer a €100,000 loss with a probability of 0.2 percent in a particular year. The expected loss is 0.002 × €100,000 = €200. If a reliable insurer offers suitable coverage for €350 annually, the premium exceeds the simple expected claim by €150. Why might someone pay it? Because losing €100,000 could be devastating while paying €350 is manageable. Insurance is partly a way to exchange an uncertain catastrophic cost for a predictable smaller one.

Economists describe preferences like these using <strong>utility</strong>: the value a person attaches to an outcome, which need not rise linearly with money. An extra €1,000 may mean little to a millionaire but a great deal to a family facing eviction. Losses can be especially painful when they jeopardize essential needs or future options. Someone can prefer a lower expected return with better downside protection without being irrational. Indeed, the more fragile their financial situation, the more valuable that stability may be.

Insurance introduces additional considerations: exclusions, deductibles, claim limits, insurer reliability, legal obligations, and the possibility that hazards occur together. A deductible means that the policyholder pays a specified initial portion of a covered loss. The cheapest premium may not buy the protection you think it does. Moreover, no insurer can make all forms of disaster disappear. The prudent question is not simply “Is the premium mathematically profitable?” but “What severe losses can I absorb, and which ones would threaten my ability to recover?”

The same principle extends beyond insurance. A business can keep emergency cash rather than invest every euro in high-yield opportunities. A city can maintain redundant infrastructure. A person can back up important files. These decisions may look inefficient when nothing bad happens, yet resilience has value precisely because the future is uncertain. <strong>Risk management is not always about maximizing the average outcome; sometimes it is about preserving your ability to continue.</strong>
