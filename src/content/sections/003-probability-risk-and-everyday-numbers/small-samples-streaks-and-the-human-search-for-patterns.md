Imagine flipping a fair coin five times. The sequence H–H–H–H–H occurs with probability 1/32, about 3.125 percent. It feels remarkable. Yet every <em>specific</em> five-flip sequence—including H–T–T–H–T—has the same probability under the fair independent model. The all-heads result attracts attention because it is visually simple, not because the coin is obliged to avoid it. Real randomness often looks irregular, and ordinary human intuition is a poor judge of what irregularity should look like.

After five heads, the next fair independent flip is still 50 percent heads. Believing that tails is “due” is the <strong>gambler's fallacy</strong>. Believing that heads is “hot” and must continue can be a closely related mistake in settings where outcomes really are independent. The coin does not remember its history. But beware a simplistic lesson: some processes <em>do</em> have memory. A machine that overheats after repeated use, a team with an injured player, or a factory with a defective batch may have correlated outcomes. Before declaring a streak meaningless, ask whether independence is plausible.

A sequence of ten excellent sales days might be evidence that a business has improved. It might also reflect a holiday, good weather, an advertising campaign, or pure fluctuation. The way to learn more is to compare with a relevant baseline and repeat observations under varied conditions. Streaks alone are rarely enough. If an extreme month is followed by an ordinary one, the explanation may simply be <strong>regression toward the mean</strong>: unusually high or low observations often contain a temporary component and are less extreme on subsequent measurements, even without a new intervention.

Consider a school that gives extra tutoring to students immediately after their worst-ever test. Scores improve the next week. Some improvement may be due to tutoring, but some students would have improved naturally because the original score was unusually low. This is not a reason to dismiss teaching; it is a reason to compare outcomes fairly, perhaps with a suitable comparison group. Statistical reasoning and the causal reasoning from Chapter 1 meet here.

There is also the <strong>law of large numbers</strong>. In a long sequence of independent, identically distributed fair coin tosses, the proportion of heads tends to approach one half. This does not mean the absolute difference between heads and tails must be tiny, nor does it guarantee that a particular short sequence will look balanced. Larger samples usually stabilize proportions, but they cannot correct a systematically biased way of collecting data. A million answers from people who chose to click on a poll may be less representative than a much smaller carefully selected sample.
