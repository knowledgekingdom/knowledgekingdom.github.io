Nearly every statistical misunderstanding has a counting problem hiding inside it. If 70 people report that a new policy helped them, is that strong evidence? It might be if the survey reached 75 people. It might be weak if 7,000 were surveyed. A <strong>numerator</strong> names the cases that meet a condition; a <strong>denominator</strong> names the larger group to which they belong. A proportion is the numerator divided by its denominator.

Try the following example. A village has 8,000 residents and recorded 96 reported thefts last year. A second village has 20,000 residents and recorded 180. The first village has fewer thefts in total; the second has a lower rate per resident. For the first village, 96 / 8,000 × 1,000 = 12 reported thefts per 1,000 residents. For the second, 180 / 20,000 × 1,000 = 9 per 1,000. Neither figure tells us whether every theft was reported, how serious the incidents were, or where they occurred. But they answer different legitimate questions. Without the denominator, the comparison invites a false conclusion.

Percentages introduce an additional trap. If a shop sold 100 notebooks last week and 125 this week, sales rose by 25 percent: (125 − 100) / 100 = 0.25. If sales then fall back from 125 to 100, the drop is 25 / 125 = 20 percent, not 25 percent. The starting value has changed. Percent increases and decreases are not symmetrical. A 50 percent fall followed by a 50 percent rise leaves you at 75 percent of the original amount.

Watch units. Saying that a country's inflation rate rose from 3 percent to 4 percent describes a <strong>one-percentage-point</strong> increase, which is a <strong>33.3 percent relative increase</strong> in the rate itself. News reports sometimes slide between these two descriptions as if they were interchangeable. A careful reader can name both and say why each might matter. The same care is necessary with rates per person, per year, per kilometre, or per transaction. Different units produce different impressions.

A useful everyday question is: <strong>“Of exactly which group?”</strong> If you see “eight out of ten doctors recommend,” ask how they were selected and what they were asked. If you see “fatalities fell by 20 percent,” ask compared with what starting level and time period. If you see “this test is 95 percent accurate,” ask which kind of accuracy, for whom, and in what setting. Every percentage is an invitation to reconstruct the underlying counts.
