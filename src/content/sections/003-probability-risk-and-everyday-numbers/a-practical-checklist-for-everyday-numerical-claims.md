Before believing a dramatic statistic, run a short sequence of questions. <strong>What is the event?</strong> A case of “crime,” “success,” “infection,” or “growth” needs a stable definition. <strong>What is the baseline?</strong> A 50 percent increase from two cases differs from a 50 percent increase from two million. <strong>What is the denominator?</strong> Are we comparing the same people, transactions, years, or geographic areas? <strong>What is the time horizon?</strong> A lifetime risk cannot be compared casually with a yearly risk.

Next ask: <strong>Is the claim conditional?</strong> If a system “detects 90 percent of fraud,” does that mean 90 percent of its alerts are fraud? No. <strong>How much uncertainty is there?</strong> A tiny survey or a rare event may justify a wide range of estimates. <strong>What matters beyond the average?</strong> A small probability of catastrophe may dominate a decision. <strong>Who bears the consequences?</strong> A policy can produce a positive overall expected outcome while distributing its burdens unfairly. And finally, <strong>what would change my mind?</strong> A claim that cannot be revised in light of evidence is not being handled scientifically.

This checklist takes less than a minute in a casual conversation. It can also guide a much longer investigation into a major investment, a medical claim, or a public-policy dispute. The aim is not to be the person who objects to everything. It is to be the person who can distinguish a good argument from a seductive number.
