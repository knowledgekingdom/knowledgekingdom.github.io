The League of Nations faced severe tests in the 1930s. Japanese occupation of Manchuria in 1931 exposed the difficulty of compelling a major power to withdraw from a distant territory. Italian invasion of Ethiopia in October 1935 posed a second challenge. Ethiopia was a League member and one of the few independent African states, yet international sanctions and diplomacy failed to protect it from conquest. Ethiopian resistance continued under occupation; Haile Selassie appealed to the League in 1936. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-6" class="cite">6</a>\]

Failure resulted not only from missing enforcement tools but from political calculations. Britain and France feared wider war, balanced European security and imperial priorities, and were reluctant to undertake measures that might endanger their own interests. Selective sanctions created incentives for aggressors to doubt collective commitments. It would still be wrong to say that the League did nothing useful anywhere; the precise point is that its security mechanisms broke down in some of their most consequential tests. International institutions can facilitate cooperation, but states determine whether commitments are defended when the costs become real. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-6" class="cite">6</a>\]
