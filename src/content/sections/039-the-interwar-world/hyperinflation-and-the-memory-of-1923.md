The crisis of 1923 was particularly dramatic in Germany. The French and Belgian occupation of the Ruhr, undertaken after disputes over reparations deliveries, was met by German passive resistance. The government financed resistance while production and revenues were disrupted, intensifying monetary breakdown already developing from earlier wartime and postwar conditions. Notes lost purchasing power at astonishing speed. Workers struggled to turn wages into food before prices rose again; savers could lose the practical value of their holdings. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]

Inflation redistributed wealth in ways that created enduring anger, but it did not affect every debtor, property owner, worker, or business identically. The subsequent stabilization through currency reform and fiscal changes brought relief. Later Nazi propaganda repeatedly exploited the memory of national humiliation and economic disorder. Yet the distinctive financial crisis that enabled the Nazis' largest electoral gains would be the deflationary unemployment and banking collapse after 1929, not hyperinflation alone. Confusing the two crises gives a false explanation of Hitler's ascent. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]
