The Ottoman Empire's defeat did not create a settled political arrangement overnight. Allied occupation, competing territorial projects, and the proposed Treaty of Sèvres prompted Turkish nationalist resistance under Mustafa Kemal and his allies. The war of independence led to the abolition of the sultanate in 1922, the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923, and the proclamation of the Republic of Turkey later that year. Population exchanges, flight, violence, and dispossession profoundly affected Greek, Turkish and other communities. Diplomatic success for a new state did not mean every inhabitant experienced the transition as liberation. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

The republic embarked on centralizing reforms concerning law, education, language, dress, the alphabet, and the relationship between state and religious institutions. The abolition of the caliphate in 1924 carried international symbolic significance, even though the political power of that office had already changed. Reforms opened new possibilities and restricted certain forms of public religious authority; they also took place in a political system with limited pluralism and coercive approaches to opposition. Turkey illustrates how republican modernization and authoritarian state-building could coexist. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]
