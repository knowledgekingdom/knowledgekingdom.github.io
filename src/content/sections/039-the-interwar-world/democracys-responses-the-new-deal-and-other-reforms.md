Franklin D. Roosevelt took office in the United States in March 1933, during a banking emergency. The New Deal combined measures to stabilize banks, provide relief, employ workers, support agriculture, regulate markets, and expand the federal government's social and administrative reach. Important reforms included deposit insurance and later Social Security legislation. Programs varied in effectiveness and design, and some generated legal conflicts or were struck down by courts. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]

The New Deal did not simply end the Great Depression by itself; unemployment remained serious into the late 1930s, and wartime production later changed the economy further. Nor did reform reach every group equally. Racial discrimination, unequal local administration, and exclusions in the design or delivery of programs limited benefits for some Black Americans and agricultural or domestic workers. In Britain and parts of Scandinavia, governments pursued different combinations of monetary adjustment, welfare reform, public works, and coalition politics. Democratic institutions were capable of policy innovation, even when their responses were incomplete. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]
