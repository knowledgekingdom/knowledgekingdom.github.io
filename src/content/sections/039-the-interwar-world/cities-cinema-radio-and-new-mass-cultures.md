The interwar years were also a period of cultural energy. Radio entered homes, film became a major mass medium, and popular music crossed borders through recordings and performance. The rise of sound film changed entertainment industries. Advertising, newspapers, illustrated magazines, sports crowds, department stores, and new forms of leisure connected consumption to politics. Urban cultural scenes in Berlin, Paris, New York, Shanghai and other cities flourished in different ways. Artists experimented with modernism, new architectural forms, and unsettling responses to war. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]

Culture, however, was never a single liberal tide. New opportunities for some women in employment, education, public life, and fashion were met by moral backlash and persistent inequalities. Rural households might not share a city's consumer abundance. Mass media also enabled organized political persuasion and disinformation. A radio set could deliver music, public education, election speeches, or state propaganda. The important change was not that technology naturally supported freedom or dictatorship, but that institutions and governments acquired powerful tools for reaching large audiences. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]
