It would be wrong to write the interwar history of Europe as a single march from democracy toward dictatorship. Britain retained competitive parliamentary politics through economic troubles; France experienced rapid cabinet turnover but did not become a fascist state before the war; Scandinavian societies developed important social-democratic and coalition responses. Czechoslovakia maintained a democratic order for much of the interwar period, though its complex nationality issues made it vulnerable to international pressure. Other countries, including parts of eastern and southern Europe, experienced authoritarian or military-dominated governments. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

Survival was never proof that democracies had solved all their social problems. Imperial rule, racism, anti-immigrant hostility, exclusion from full citizenship, and political violence remained. Yet differences in civic organization, legal institutions, political bargaining, and the armed forces mattered. Historical comparison should avoid false equivalence between an imperfect constitutional democracy and a regime systematically abolishing competition and persecuting entire populations. It should also avoid complacency: strong institutions require political choices and public commitments to remain strong. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]
