The League of Nations, founded in 1920, aimed to reduce the likelihood of war through consultation, arbitration, and collective action against aggression. Its members established procedures for disputes and worked through specialized organizations on public health, refugees, labor conditions, and trafficking. The International Labour Organization, created in 1919, addressed working conditions through a related institutional framework. These activities mattered to people whose lives were not defined by a military crisis. Judging the League solely by 1939 conceals what international cooperation sometimes achieved. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]

Nevertheless, collective security demanded more than a charter. Members needed to agree that aggression had occurred, accept costs, and be willing to enforce decisions when powerful states resisted. The United States never joined the League; important powers entered and left at different times; Britain and France balanced international obligations against domestic priorities and imperial interests. The League had no independent army capable of compelling members to act. This design did not make failure certain, but it created serious vulnerabilities when governments treated shared security as someone else's responsibility. Its successes and failures must both be included in an evaluation. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-6" class="cite">6</a>\]
