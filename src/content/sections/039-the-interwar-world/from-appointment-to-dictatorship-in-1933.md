The Nazi regime dismantled pluralism rapidly. After the Reichstag fire in February 1933, an emergency decree suspended fundamental liberties. Police repression and violence targeted communists, social democrats, trade unionists, journalists, and other opponents. The March election occurred under intimidation, and the Enabling Act, adopted on 23 March 1933, permitted the government to legislate without ordinary parliamentary approval, including laws inconsistent with the constitution. Some opposition deputies were imprisoned or excluded, while conservative and centrist support helped secure the law. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-26" class="cite">26</a>\]

In the following months the regime banned or absorbed political organizations, brought regional institutions under Nazi control, and built a dictatorship around Hitler. Concentration camps were established to imprison political opponents and others targeted by the regime. The process was a combination of state power, political violence, legal manipulation, propaganda, and elite collaboration. It shows why the existence of elections and formal legislation does not by itself guarantee a functioning democracy when the freedom to compete, organize, report, and dissent is destroyed. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-26" class="cite">26</a>\]
