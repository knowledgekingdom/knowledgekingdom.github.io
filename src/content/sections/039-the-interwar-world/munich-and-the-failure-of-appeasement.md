In March 1938 Germany annexed Austria. Later that year Hitler demanded the Sudetenland, a border region of Czechoslovakia with a large German-speaking population and important defensive positions. At Munich on 29–30 September 1938, the leaders of Germany, Italy, Britain and France agreed to its transfer without Czechoslovakia participating as an equal negotiator. British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain hoped concessions would preserve peace; critics argued that the arrangement rewarded coercion and undermined an ally. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-37" class="cite">37</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-38" class="cite">38</a>\]

Appeasement is best understood as a policy formed under constraints and assumptions, not as a magic word that explains everything. Some British leaders wanted time for rearmament; others considered aspects of Versailles unjust or hoped that limited concessions would satisfy Germany. The policy's central failure became especially clear in March 1939, when Germany occupied the Czech lands beyond the earlier ethnic-border dispute. An event praised at the moment as saving peace could appear, within months, to have made war more likely. Historians should compare contemporary evidence, available alternatives, and subsequent outcomes instead of assuming that hindsight was available in 1938. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-37" class="cite">37</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-38" class="cite">38</a>\]
