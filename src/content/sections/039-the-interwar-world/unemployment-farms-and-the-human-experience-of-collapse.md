Unemployment was not simply a statistic. It meant children leaving school, families moving in with relatives, men and women queuing for relief, and the loss of confidence in social status built over decades. In the United States unemployment reached roughly a quarter of the labor force at the Depression's worst point under common historical estimates, though the figure changes with definitions. Elsewhere, rural households confronted collapsing crop prices, debt enforcement, and migration. Many people continued to work for reduced pay or fewer hours, so an unemployment rate alone misses part of the distress. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]

Some business owners and property holders also failed; others acquired assets cheaply or adapted successfully. Gender shaped the experience of unemployment and relief, as did race, citizenship, and access to public assistance. In colonies, the value of an exported crop could fall while tax demands remained difficult to satisfy. Distress was not automatically transformed into extremism: communities also organized mutual aid, union campaigns, cooperative associations, and demands for democratic welfare reform. A serious account must compare these different political responses rather than treating economic crisis as a machine that mechanically manufactures dictatorship. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]
