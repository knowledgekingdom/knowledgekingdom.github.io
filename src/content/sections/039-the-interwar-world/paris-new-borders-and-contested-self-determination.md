The Paris peace settlement produced several treaties rather than one universal agreement. Versailles addressed Germany; Saint-Germain dealt with Austria; Trianon with Hungary; Neuilly with Bulgaria; and Sèvres attempted to settle the future of the defeated Ottoman state before being superseded for the Turkish republic by Lausanne in 1923. Borders were redrawn, military forces restricted, and reparations or other obligations imposed. Some peoples gained new international recognition, but other groups remained minorities within states they had not chosen. Any map of the period must distinguish treaty lines, actual administration, and later changes. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]

Woodrow Wilson's language of self-determination appealed to national movements, yet it never offered an agreed rule for deciding the claims of every community. A province could contain several languages, religions, landholding patterns, and competing political loyalties. New states such as Czechoslovakia and Poland inherited internal diversity and difficult borders. Former colonies and conquered Ottoman territories were partly reorganized as League of Nations mandates, administered by imperial powers rather than immediately becoming independent. International law created a new vocabulary of protection without abolishing unequal power. Peace was simultaneously a diplomatic achievement, a source of grievance, and a contested starting point. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]
