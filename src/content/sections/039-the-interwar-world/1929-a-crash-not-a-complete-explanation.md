The Wall Street crash of October 1929 became a powerful symbol of the Great Depression. Shares fell sharply, fortunes were damaged, and confidence deteriorated. But a stock-market crash is not itself a full theory of why global production, investment, employment, and prices fell for years. The U.S. banking system was vulnerable; earlier economic imbalances mattered; subsequent bank failures reduced credit; and restrictive monetary responses deepened contraction. The downturn spread through international trade and finance. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]

It is also important to distinguish financial wealth from everyday economic conditions. An unemployed laborer might own no shares at all and still lose work because customers stopped buying and firms could not secure financing. A bank run could damage otherwise viable enterprises by cutting off payment and credit. Falling prices often sound attractive until wages, rents, debts, and business revenues are considered together. A family with fixed nominal debt might find repayments harder as income declined. History becomes intelligible when we follow these mechanisms rather than attributing every later event to a single famous photograph of a stock exchange. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]
