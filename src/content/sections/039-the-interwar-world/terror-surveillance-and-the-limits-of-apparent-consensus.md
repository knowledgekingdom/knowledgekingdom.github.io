The Soviet state under Stalin expanded secret-police surveillance, forced-labor camps, and political prosecution. During the Great Terror of 1937–1938, purges and mass operations targeted officials, military officers, alleged opponents, and broad categories of citizens. Confessions obtained under duress were presented as proof of conspiracies. The terror did not affect every individual in the same way, but its uncertainty reshaped how people spoke, worked, formed relationships, and understood public life. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

Propaganda celebrated industrial achievements, heroic workers, and the regime's claims to equality. Some people benefited from education and career mobility and believed in the system, while others adapted outwardly from fear or necessity. It is difficult to reconstruct public opinion under dictatorship when surveys, press freedom, and open opposition are absent. A cheering crowd is evidence of a staged public event, not a numerical measure of sincere consent. Understanding how authoritarian politics works requires distinguishing overt obedience, private belief, practical accommodation, and organized resistance. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]
