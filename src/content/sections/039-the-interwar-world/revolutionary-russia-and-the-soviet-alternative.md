The Russian Revolutions of 1917 were followed by civil war, foreign interventions, economic disruption, famine, and struggles over the new state's territorial reach. The Bolsheviks retained power, and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was formally established in 1922. War Communism had involved extraordinary requisitioning and centralized controls. In 1921 Lenin's New Economic Policy permitted a partial return of markets, especially in small-scale commerce and agriculture, while the ruling party retained the commanding political position. The result was neither capitalist restoration nor a fully stabilized socialist economy. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]

The Soviet example influenced political debates around the world. Supporters claimed it showed a path beyond capitalism and imperialism; opponents pointed to one-party power, violence, restrictions on political competition, and the suppression of dissent. Communist parties emerged within an international movement, although their priorities and national circumstances differed. Soviet policy would later change dramatically under Stalin. It is historically misleading to assume that the whole trajectory from 1917 to the terror of the 1930s was visible to every participant from the beginning. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]
