After Lenin's death in 1924, conflicts within the Soviet Communist Party eventually brought Joseph Stalin overwhelming political power. At the end of the 1920s the leadership abandoned important features of the New Economic Policy. Five-Year Plans emphasized heavy industry, state investment, production targets, and rapid mobilization of labor. The Soviet Union built major industrial capacity, changed the composition of its workforce, and promoted technical education, especially in expanding industrial centers. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]

These achievements came through extreme coercion and upheaval. Workers faced discipline, shortages, and dangerous conditions; administrators manipulated statistics to satisfy targets; agricultural policy was transformed through compulsory collectivization. Official production figures require careful interpretation because quotas, definitions, and politically distorted reporting affected their quality. Industrial growth was real, but it did not make the costs morally negligible, nor did it justify describing the Soviet system as merely an efficient machine for modernization. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]
