The Nazi Party began winning much larger shares of the German vote as the Depression intensified. Its propaganda combined promises of employment and national revival with attacks on parliamentary government, communism, and Jews. Violence by paramilitary groups intimidated political opponents. By July 1932 the Nazis were the largest party in the Reichstag, but their roughly 37 percent vote share was not a majority. Subsequent electoral changes did not erase the fact that the republic's final crisis involved negotiations among conservative elites and the use of presidential authority. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]

On 30 January 1933 President Paul von Hindenburg appointed Adolf Hitler chancellor in a coalition cabinet. Conservative politicians imagined that they could control or exploit him; this calculation was disastrously mistaken. The decision was not the direct outcome of one nationwide referendum in which Germans endorsed dictatorship. It occurred within a constitution increasingly governed through emergency practices and backstage agreements. Understanding the mechanisms does not reduce responsibility for the choices; it makes that responsibility clearer. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]
