China's twentieth-century political crisis had roots in the collapse of the Qing dynasty in 1911–1912. Regional military leaders, the Nationalist movement, and the Chinese Communist movement competed for power and models of modernization. The May Fourth protests in 1919 combined opposition to decisions at Paris concerning Shandong with wider demands for cultural and political renewal. The Nationalists, or Guomindang, sought political unification, while the Communists developed their own revolutionary organization. Their temporary cooperation ended in violent division. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-31" class="cite">31</a>\]

Nationalist rule under Chiang Kai-shek extended central authority in parts of China but remained challenged by regional leaders, social conflict, the Communists, and Japanese pressure. The Chinese Communist movement adapted to rural warfare after major urban defeats; the Long March of 1934–1935 became an enduring part of its political memory. The intensified Japanese war after 1937 created a difficult and incomplete form of renewed cooperation against invasion. Chinese people were not passive victims of outside powers; they pursued competing answers to the problems of sovereignty, justice, and national survival. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-31" class="cite">31</a>\]
