The word <em>fascism</em> is often used casually to mean any harsh or authoritarian regime. For historical analysis, that is too vague. Italian Fascism combined ultranationalism, militant anti-liberalism, hostility to socialism, a cult of regenerative national unity, organized political violence, and the ambition to mobilize society under a one-party state. Other far-right movements borrowed or modified aspects of this model. Fascist regimes were not identical, and scholars debate which features define a broader fascist family. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

Nazism overlapped with fascism in its contempt for parliamentary democracy, racialized national rebirth, leader authority, propaganda, and political terror. But Nazi ideology made biological racism, antisemitism, and territorial expansion into eastern Europe particularly central. Soviet Stalinism was also dictatorial, violent, and based on a single ruling party, yet its official doctrine and economic structure differed fundamentally. Comparison is useful only if it identifies both similarities and distinctions. Calling every regime 'the same kind of tyranny' may express moral condemnation but obscures causation and institutional mechanisms. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]
