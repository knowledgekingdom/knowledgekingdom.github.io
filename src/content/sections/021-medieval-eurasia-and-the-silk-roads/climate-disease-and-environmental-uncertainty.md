Medieval Eurasian history was also shaped by environmental conditions. Drought could reduce the productivity of an oasis; excessive flooding could damage canals; severe winter weather could limit grazing and animal movement. Over the long term, rivers shifted and human water management changed landscapes. Yet it is dangerous to name “climate change” as the explanation for every dynastic decline. A drought matters through the institutions, stored resources and political conflicts that determine who can respond.

Diseases moved among people and animals in connected worlds, and merchant travel was among the many activities that could contribute to spread. The great mid-fourteenth-century <strong>Black Death</strong> belongs principally to a later phase of Eurasian connectivity, after the main chronological focus of this chapter. It will appear again when we consider the Mongol-era world. We should not retroactively attribute every earlier epidemic to the same pathogen or mechanism. Written descriptions of fever and mortality rarely identify a disease with modern clinical certainty.

Environmental historians combine chronicles with archaeological data, tree rings, lake sediments, pollen and other evidence. Every method has limitations of scale, date and interpretation. A dry year in one valley is not necessarily a continental drought. A caravan route that shifted after a conflict might have done so because of security, prices, taxes or climate together. <strong>Systems thinking from Chapter 7</strong> is especially useful here: environmental events influence people through social and political connections, not through a single automatic chain.
