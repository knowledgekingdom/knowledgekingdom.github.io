Consider the decimal place-value system. It developed in South Asian mathematical traditions, was studied and adapted by scholars writing in Arabic, and eventually entered wider European mathematical practice through multiple channels. This familiar history becomes misleading when reduced to the statement that “one civilization invented mathematics and another copied it.” South Asian mathematicians worked within earlier mathematical histories; Arabic-language scholars solved new problems, translated and taught; later scholars changed notations and methods in their own settings.

What made the system attractive? A positional decimal notation made many calculations efficient compared with methods that relied on cumbersome numerical symbols. But its success still required scribes, teachers, manuscripts, merchants and institutional users. A mathematical idea is not the same as a tool purchased in a market. It can survive by being explained, copied, criticized and practiced even when the original manuscript is lost.

Apply the same reasoning to paper, Buddhism or an artistic motif. Ask where early evidence occurs, which travelers and institutions might have transmitted it, what changed at the destination, and what uncertainty remains. <strong>Transmission is a historical process, not an arrow on a map.</strong> The fact that ideas move does not erase the differences among those who adopt them.
