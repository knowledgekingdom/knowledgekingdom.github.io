We often picture a “Silk Road traveler” crossing Eurasia from edge to edge. A few envoys, pilgrims and extraordinary merchants did make long journeys. Most exchange, however, occurred in overlapping stages. To see the difference, imagine a piece of fabric that changes owners in four towns. It may travel 3,000 kilometers while no single merchant travels more than 800. The number of transactions is not the same as the number of long-range travelers, and a good's arrival is not evidence that its original producer understood its destination.

A simple travel model makes the point. If a caravan moves on average 25 kilometers per travel day, then 1,000 kilometers requires 40 travel days <strong>before</strong> rest, weather, taxes, markets, delays and detours. If travel is possible on only five days out of seven, the same distance requires approximately 56 elapsed days under the simplified assumption. Real routes might cross a desert more slowly or move on favorable stretches more rapidly. Such approximations can illuminate logistical scale but should never be passed off as an authentic itinerary.

The network metaphor can be made precise. A <strong>node</strong> is a town, port, oasis or court where travelers meet and goods change hands. A <strong>link</strong> is a route that connects two nodes. A <strong>bottleneck</strong> is a narrow connection whose disruption affects many alternatives. Routes can be <em>redundant</em> if other links carry traffic when one fails. Networks explain both resilience and vulnerability; the loss of one city need not end exchange if many alternate paths survive, but a sudden closure can raise costs throughout a large region.
