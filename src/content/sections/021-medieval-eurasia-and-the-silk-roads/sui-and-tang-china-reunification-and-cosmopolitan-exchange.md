The Sui dynasty (581–618) reunited much of China after centuries of division, while the Tang dynasty (618–907) presided over an influential imperial order. The Sui and Tang supported roads, canals, postal routes and administrative institutions, though the burdens of major projects and war could be heavy. The <strong>Grand Canal</strong> connected important regions of northern and southern China, facilitating movement within the empire rather than constituting a simple East–West Silk Road. Internal transport mattered because trade arriving in the capital relied on a productive and connected hinterland.

The Tang capital <strong>Chang'an</strong>, near present-day Xi'an, was famous for its international residents and influences. Traders, envoys, performers, monks and administrators brought Central Asian and other cultural practices into the court's orbit. Tang power reached into parts of the Tarim Basin at certain times, but its frontier control was variable and contested by neighboring powers. A Tang-era city could exhibit Persian or Sogdian fashions while the dynasty also imposed restrictions on particular foreign communities or religions. <strong>Cosmopolitan does not mean uniformly tolerant.</strong>

The <strong>An Lushan rebellion</strong> beginning in 755 severely weakened the dynasty and reshaped its military and fiscal position. Its broader consequences helped redirect some patterns of trade and power, though exchange did not simply stop. The rebellion itself involved a military leader with frontier connections; it should not be reduced to an ethnic conflict or blamed on the existence of foreign merchants. Understanding the Tang requires looking at court politics, regional armies, taxation and social change alongside international commerce.
