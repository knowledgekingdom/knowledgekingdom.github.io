The name <em>Silk Road</em> was popularized by the nineteenth-century geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen. It is useful shorthand, but it can mislead. No organization owned all the roads; no universal customs authority set prices; and most people trading along them did not make a continuous journey from China to the Mediterranean. A cloth might leave a Chinese producer, move to a regional market, be sold by a merchant to another caravan operator, pass to a city warehouse, and travel farther only when demand and security justified the cost. Different languages and currencies could be involved at each transaction.

Historians distinguish <strong>direct exchange</strong>, where people meet and trade, from <strong>indirect transmission</strong>, where a good moves through successive intermediaries. If a Persian glass vessel appears in an East Asian grave, it proves that the object or its materials traveled. It does not by itself prove a Persian glassmaker visited the grave's owner. The object might have been inherited, imitated, gifted or acquired from a merchant who knew nothing about Persia. Similar cautions apply to imported coins, depictions of foreign animals and travel accounts describing lands known only by hearsay.

It is equally wrong to imagine that valuable exotic luxuries formed the entire economy. Many residents traded everyday grain, fuel, pottery, wool and animals locally or regionally, while only a fraction of production entered distant circuits. <strong>Long-distance trade depended on local economies.</strong> An oasis supporting an international caravan first had to feed its households and maintain its canals. Long-range networks are easier to see in glamorous museum collections than in ordinary food systems, but the latter kept the former alive.
