It is easy to call Samarkand a “Silk Road city” and leave it there. But that label does not explain how it survived when trade volumes fluctuated. Its surrounding agriculture, irrigation systems, craftspeople, markets, religious institutions and regional political relationships sustained an urban population. Bukhara likewise combined religious learning with local production and long-distance links. <strong>Merv</strong>, in the oasis landscape of present-day Turkmenistan, connected agriculture and multiple historical urban settlements with routes across northeastern Iran and Central Asia. None of these cities was simply a hotel beside a road.

Urban prosperity also carried risks. A government that taxed merchants excessively might reroute exchange; a canal failure could damage harvests; a succession struggle could disrupt commercial credit. Yet a city could recover because households, craft skills, waterworks and networks of scholars persisted. Long-term urban history is therefore not reducible to a list of conquerors, even when conquest was traumatic.

Archaeology also forces us to ask <strong>whose prosperity</strong> we are describing. A decorated bowl may reveal skilled labor and wealthy consumers, while saying little about an indebted farm household. A poet's praise of a prosperous city may be partly literary convention. A tax document can show what the state demanded, not necessarily what people actually paid. Combining objects, landscapes and texts turns a glamorous “crossroads” into a historically living place.
