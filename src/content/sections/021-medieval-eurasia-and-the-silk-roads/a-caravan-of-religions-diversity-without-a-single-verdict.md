The Silk Roads carried multiple religious traditions, including forms of Buddhism, Christianity, Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism and Islam. The <strong>Church of the East</strong> developed communities across western and central Asia and reached Tang China; the inscription on the <strong>Xi'an Stele</strong> of 781 commemorates the history of a Christian community there. Manichaeism, founded by Mani in the third century, also spread far beyond its Iranian origins. Zoroastrian communities were influential in parts of Central Asia, particularly within Sogdian society, even as religious landscapes shifted.

The temptation is to imagine peaceful faiths meeting at a desert bazaar and producing an ideal of modern multicultural tolerance. The historical record is more complicated. Religious groups translated and borrowed, but also argued, competed for patrons and faced persecution. Tang policy toward foreign and Buddhist institutions changed sharply in the ninth century. In many Islamic lands, Christians and Jews retained communities and roles, though fiscal obligations and legal standing could differ from those of Muslims. Local patterns were varied, and an official proclamation cannot describe every encounter.

We should distinguish <strong>coexistence</strong>, <strong>exchange</strong>, <strong>assimilation</strong>, and <strong>equality</strong>. Two communities may live beside one another without equal rights; a religion may borrow artistic motifs without adopting another religion's doctrines; and an individual's conversion may be sincere without erasing earlier family customs. The evidence rarely justifies the sweeping verdict that the Silk Roads were either an uninterrupted harmony or only a history of religious conflict.
