By about 1200, Eurasia already contained deeply connected commercial and intellectual regions. Some political arrangements were stable, others fragile. Song China maintained a powerful economy while competing with northern states. Central Asian cities participated in Islamic and Persianate worlds. Steppe confederations contested pasture, tribute and trading access. Maritime networks connected China, Southeast Asia, India, Arabia and East Africa. Byzantine, Balkan, Rus' and other European societies participated in overlapping systems of diplomacy and exchange.

The rise of the Mongols in the early thirteenth century would transform many of these relationships. Their conquests destroyed cities and communities, but their empire also created new political conditions for travel and diplomacy across enormous areas. Later accounts of unusually long-distance journeys should not be projected backward onto every earlier century. <strong>The Mongols did not create the Silk Roads out of nothing</strong>; they reorganized networks that had a long and uneven history. Chapter 22 will investigate both the violence and the connectivity of this transformation.

At this boundary it is useful to resist a popular myth: that the medieval world was isolated until one empire or one European explorer “connected” it. People in western Asia, Central Asia, East Asia, Africa and Europe were linked through many intermediaries for centuries, even while most inhabitants lived locally. Connection does not require every village to meet the globe. It requires routes, institutions and people capable of carrying consequences beyond their starting point.
