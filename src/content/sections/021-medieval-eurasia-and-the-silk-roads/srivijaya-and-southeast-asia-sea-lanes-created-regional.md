In maritime Southeast Asia, the <strong>Srivijaya</strong> political network, centered at different times around southeastern Sumatra, was influential from the seventh century onward. Its history illustrates a form of power unlike a state that controls every village in a continuous block of land. Regional rulers and ports could profit from strategic passages, river mouths, coastal relationships and diplomatic prestige. Their influence could be strong in some harbors yet disputed or limited in nearby interior areas. It is more accurate to study changing alliances and access to routes than to paint a modern national border around Srivijaya.

Chinese Buddhist traveler <strong>Yijing</strong> spent time in the Srivijaya region and described it as a setting for Buddhist learning and preparation for study in India. His accounts are valuable evidence of intellectual traffic as well as trade. Ships and travelers linked the region to India and China, while communities adapted religious and artistic influences in distinctive ways. A Sanskrit inscription or Hindu-Buddhist temple does not indicate that a local society became “Indian” in an ethnic sense. Cultural forms were deliberately selected and reinterpreted by local patrons and artisans.

The region's commercial importance made it a target of rivalry. In 1025 the Chola ruler <strong>Rajendra I</strong> launched a notable expedition against Srivijaya-linked centers. Historians continue to debate aspects of its aims and consequences. The event nevertheless demonstrates that the sea was a political arena: naval force, port access and diplomatic recognition affected commercial networks. Maritime exchange was neither outside statecraft nor controlled fully by any one court.
