An island can be an isolated community, a trading hub, a military position, or all three. Its strategic value depends on the surrounding routes, available harbours, local population, climate and political arrangements. A remote island might help monitor maritime activity or support aircraft, but its supply lines can be vulnerable. An archipelago creates more waterways and overlapping jurisdictions than a single compact coastline.

For island societies, the sea is often a connector rather than an empty boundary. Communities across the Pacific, Indian Ocean and Caribbean developed sophisticated navigation and social networks long before modern global shipping. Analyses that depict islands solely as naval stepping stones erase the interests and rights of their residents. It is ethically and analytically important to ask who benefits from a base and who bears its costs.

One cannot infer a state's strategy simply from the number of islands it holds. A cluster near major routes can be influential, whereas distant possessions may be financially burdensome. Satellite communications and aircraft change some distances, but food, freshwater and physical goods still have to arrive. Geography here is a balance between connectivity and dependence.
