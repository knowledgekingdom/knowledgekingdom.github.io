A city becomes influential partly through its connections. Ports move cargo, airports bring passengers and high-value goods, universities attract skilled people, and telecommunications networks coordinate businesses over long distances. The location where networks meet is called a node. Some nodes are important because they connect many routes; others because there is no practical replacement for their specialized functions.

Consider two hypothetical coastal cities with equal populations. City A has a modern container terminal but poorly managed customs and limited inland rail. City B has a smaller port linked to a productive manufacturing region and efficient logistics services. B may become the stronger commercial node despite its smaller waterfront. Institutions alter geographic opportunity by reducing delays, uncertainty and transaction costs.

A network can also create asymmetric dependence. A country may own a port used heavily by its neighbors, while depending on their factories, consumers and transit fees. If conflict disrupts trade, both sides may suffer, but not equally. Mapping the direction of flows—goods, electricity, information and people—reveals relationships that political borders alone conceal.
