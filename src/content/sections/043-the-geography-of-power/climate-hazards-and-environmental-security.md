Temperature, rainfall, drought, storms and sea level shape settlement and production. Climate change adds pressure through rising heat, shifting precipitation, coastal flooding and changes to ecosystems. The IPCC assesses that climate-related hazards interact with poverty, inequality, food systems, institutions and existing conflict, creating compound risks. It is misleading to say that a dry year inevitably causes a civil war.

A flood may damage crops while also destroying roads needed to deliver aid. A heat wave may reduce labour productivity just as electricity demand rises. These are cascading effects: several systems fail together. Strong early warning, resilient infrastructure, public health and accountable government can reduce damage even in physically exposed locations. Conversely, social exclusion can turn a manageable hazard into a disaster.

Climate adaptation is therefore part of geopolitics, but it should not reduce vulnerable communities to threats. Water-sharing agreements, scientific monitoring, emergency cooperation and planned relocation require political decisions. The productive question is which exposures are changing and which institutions can reduce the resulting risks—not which region's climate supposedly makes conflict unavoidable.
