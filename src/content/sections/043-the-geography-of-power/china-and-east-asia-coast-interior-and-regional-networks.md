China's economic geography includes dense coastal urban-industrial networks, major river basins, inland cities and extensive western uplands. The Yangtze and other transport corridors connect production and markets, while maritime routes link manufacturing centres to global buyers. The relationship between the coast and interior has changed through infrastructure investment, industrial policy, migration and political choices.

Maritime access creates opportunities and vulnerabilities. East Asian supply chains rely on shipping passages, ports, sophisticated industries and cross-border commercial connections. A disruption to a nearby strait may have effects far beyond the states bordering it. Yet it is misleading to infer from a map that a conflict is unavoidable. Economic interdependence, diplomacy, legal disputes, domestic politics and military deterrence are separate factors.

Some analyses treat East Asia as one continuous coastline controlled by competing great powers. That erases the interests of Japan, Korea, Southeast Asian states, Taiwan and other communities with distinct histories and legal positions. A meaningful geopolitical map should represent multiple perspectives and show commercial links as well as military deployments. Regional geography explains the stakes, not the outcome.
