Digital information travels through a physical network. Undersea fibre-optic cables, terrestrial links, data centres, exchanges and electricity grids support what users experience as almost instantaneous communication. The fact that a message crosses an ocean quickly does not mean distance has disappeared. Cables must land somewhere; repairs require ships and specialists; and disruptions can force traffic onto alternative routes with different capacities.

An undersea cable is not strategically important merely because it lies on a map. Its role depends on how much traffic it carries, the number of alternative paths, the operators maintaining it and the services that depend on it. Networks with multiple independent routes can be more resilient than systems where several links converge in one easily disrupted location. This distinction between redundancy and apparent connectivity is central to modern infrastructure planning.

A second layer is political and legal. Data protection rules, ownership, cybersecurity practices and control over internet exchange points affect who can access information. A state with many cables but poor institutions may still face substantial digital insecurity. Treat cyberspace as an infrastructure system with human governance, not as a magical realm outside geography.
