Strategic analysis often uses terms such as 'buffer,' 'depth,' 'access' and 'control.' These may help describe military calculations, but can also obscure the people who inhabit the places being discussed. A village near a border is a community with homes and histories, not merely a military obstacle. A territory described as 'indispensable' to one power may be the sovereign homeland of another.

Causal explanation is not moral permission. One can investigate why a government fears the loss of a corridor without accepting an invasion as legitimate. One can recognize how a blockade would operate without denying the suffering it might cause. This distinction is essential when evaluating claims about security and national survival. It prevents geographic reasoning from becoming a language in which the strong appear entitled to govern the weak.

A more responsible vocabulary includes the interests of residents, legal rights, ecological costs, alternative policies and the distribution of benefits. The best strategic decisions are not necessarily those that maximize territorial reach. They may be the ones that build secure connections, reduce coercive dependencies and preserve the ability of people to live safely.
