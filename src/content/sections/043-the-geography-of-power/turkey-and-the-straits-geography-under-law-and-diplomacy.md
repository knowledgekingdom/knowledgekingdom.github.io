The Bosphorus and Dardanelles connect the Black Sea to the Mediterranean via the Sea of Marmara. This narrow system has long mattered to the people of the region and to states trading through the Black Sea. But it is analytically wrong to say that the geographical position alone gives any one government unlimited discretion over all transit. International arrangements, including the 1936 Montreux Convention, shape the passage of merchant and military vessels under specified conditions.

Legal rules distinguish normal commercial navigation from particular restrictions affecting warships and certain circumstances of conflict. Their application requires careful reading rather than a slogan about 'control of the gate.' Naval capabilities and political relationships influence practice as well. Ships using the Turkish Straits connect the food, energy and industrial economies of countries far beyond the immediate coastline.

This is a model for understanding why chokepoints require interdisciplinary study. Physical geography identifies a narrow passage; legal geography describes rights and restrictions; political analysis examines relationships among states; economic geography measures dependence on the route. No single layer answers all four questions.
