Mountain ranges can complicate invasion, administration and trade. Steep gradients slow heavy vehicles, weather can close roads, and narrow valleys concentrate movement. Yet mountains do not form impenetrable walls. Armies and merchants have crossed the Alps, Andes, Hindu Kush and other ranges through specific passes, often relying on guides and local communities. The relevant question is not whether a mountain exists but where usable routes run and who can sustain movement through them.

Mountains can protect communities from outside forces while making internal government expensive. Roads, electricity lines and emergency services cost more in difficult terrain. Borders along watersheds may appear convenient on maps but divide connected communities and grazing systems. Mountain warfare can favor defenders without guaranteeing victory; aircraft, tunnelling and precision reconnaissance alter the balance but do not erase winter, altitude or supply difficulties.

Consider Switzerland as a reminder that geography interacts with choices. Alpine terrain helped shape defense planning, while diplomacy, financial institutions, trade and political arrangements also shaped the country's position. To say that mountains alone produced neutrality would mistake a partial constraint for a complete history. The same caution applies to any mountainous state.
