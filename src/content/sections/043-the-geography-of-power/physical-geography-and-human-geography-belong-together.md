Physical geography studies terrain, climate, water, ecosystems and other aspects of the earth's surface. Human geography examines settlements, migration, economic activity, borders, social relations and the ways people understand places. Strategic decisions sit at their intersection. A population living along a fertile river may support agriculture and trade, but the political consequences depend on land law, flood control, education and relations with neighbors.

Scale changes the answer. On a continental map, the Himalayas look like one formidable barrier. On a regional map, valleys, passes and high plateaus appear; on a local map, seasonal snowfall, roads and bridges matter. A map drawn for a military headquarters may prioritize different features than one prepared for farmers or disaster-response teams. There is no single view that contains every relevant fact.

Be careful with the word natural. Borders are negotiated and enforced, even when they follow a river or mountain ridge. A desert is not an empty political space merely because settlements are sparse; people maintain routes, communities and rights across it. Thinking geographically means studying inhabitants, not treating landscapes as vacant chessboards.
