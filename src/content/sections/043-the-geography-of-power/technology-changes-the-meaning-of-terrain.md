A mountain remains steep, but the cost of crossing it changes when engineers build a tunnel. An ocean remains wide, but a container ship, aircraft, fibre-optic cable and satellite connect shores in different ways. The importance of geography is therefore historically contingent: technology changes access, response times and the strategic value of particular places without eliminating physical distance.

In the nineteenth century, railways altered the speed at which states could supply armies and connect inland industries. In the twentieth century, aircraft reached across barriers that had constrained armies on foot. Missile systems and satellites now allow states to observe or threaten distant locations, while cyber networks make some activities less dependent on physical proximity. Yet the people who operate these systems still need electricity, skilled workers, spare parts and physical facilities.

Technological innovation also creates new chokepoints. A data-centre campus, undersea cable landing station or rare specialist factory may become essential to many countries. A policy focused only on canals and mountain passes misses these dependencies. The sensible comparison asks how physical geography, technology and institutions combine to create or reduce vulnerability in each period.
