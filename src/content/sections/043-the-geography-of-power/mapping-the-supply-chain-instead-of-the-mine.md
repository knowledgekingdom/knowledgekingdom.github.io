Suppose a region contains a deposit of a critical mineral. A sensational map might label the region 'the key to global power.' A careful map would follow the mineral through extraction, transport, refining, component manufacturing, assembly and final use. It would mark alternative suppliers, skilled labour, energy requirements and the cost of switching between them.

Strategic vulnerability may be concentrated at a refining plant rather than the mine. A company may rely on specialist chemicals produced in a third country, or a port through which several raw materials must pass. Geographic advantage is consequently relational: what matters is where the whole productive network can function. Possessing a resource does not imply control over the finished products that use it.

This logic can help readers assess headlines about batteries, semiconductors, fertilizers or medicines. Ask which stage is actually scarce, how long substitute capacity takes to build, and who bears the cost of disruption. The answer may reveal a strategic bottleneck or may show that a dramatic map has confused a single deposit with an entire industry.
