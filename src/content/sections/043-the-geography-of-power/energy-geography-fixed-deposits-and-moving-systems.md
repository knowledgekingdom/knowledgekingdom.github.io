Energy has an awkward geography. Coal and fossil fuels are located in particular geological basins; sun and wind vary across time and space; hydropower depends on rivers; and electricity moves through networks requiring constant coordination. Energy security therefore concerns the full system—generation, transmission, storage, imports and demand—not merely the location of oilfields or power plants.

Pipelines, liquefied natural-gas terminals, refineries, power grids and storage facilities create dependencies and possible bottlenecks. A pipeline may be efficient but routes flows through specific countries. A power grid may share reserves across borders and lower costs, yet require technical rules and mutual trust. A government deciding between domestic generation, multiple imports and additional storage faces a portfolio problem rather than a single geographic answer.

Renewables do not abolish geopolitics. They can reduce dependence on imported fossil fuel but increase the importance of transmission capacity, critical minerals, manufacturing equipment and electricity storage. Nor is fossil-fuel geography permanently decisive: technologies, regulations, prices and consumer choices change the value of deposits. A useful analysis separates physical resource potential from the institutions that deliver reliable energy to households and industry.
