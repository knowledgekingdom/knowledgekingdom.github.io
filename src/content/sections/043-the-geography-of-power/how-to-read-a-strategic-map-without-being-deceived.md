Before drawing conclusions from a map, examine the projection, legend, date, scale and categories. A Mercator world map greatly enlarges some high-latitude land areas relative to those near the equator. A map of trade routes may omit alternative passages because the author wants to emphasize one risk. A military map may show deployments without revealing readiness, maintenance or political permission to use them.

Distinguish the visible fact from the inferred explanation. A canal's location may be undisputed, while a claim that it gives one state decisive global leverage requires evidence about shipping, alternatives and institutions. Colouring an entire region with the flag of an empire can conceal the uneven reach of its actual administration. Colouring contested territory without a legend can turn a disputed claim into an apparent fact.

Cross-check spatial claims against legal texts, transport statistics, topographic data, satellite images where appropriate and independent historical scholarship. Mark uncertainty. Ask which groups and networks the map leaves out. Good geographic literacy is not memorizing colourful borders; it is learning what the representation can and cannot show.
