The continental United States benefits from access to the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans and possesses extensive agricultural regions, navigable waterways and internal transport networks. The Mississippi basin has historically provided connections between inland production and Gulf ports. But its economic development also involved institutions, technological change, capital, immigration, forced labour, Indigenous dispossession and industrialization. Geography supplied opportunities within a much more complicated history.

Oceans have offered a measure of distance from some external military threats, while the country has also projected force far from its shores. Ships, bases, alliances, aircraft and communications make power projection possible; geography alone cannot explain the resources and political choices involved. Nor should the map suggest that relations with Canada, Mexico and Caribbean states are secondary merely because oceans dominate national narratives.

This case helps distinguish natural endowments from the political creation of scale. A river is useful, but locks, dredging, ports, law and finance make it an integrated market. A large landmass is not automatically a unified economy. The same principle applies to smaller states planning investments in roads and logistics corridors.
