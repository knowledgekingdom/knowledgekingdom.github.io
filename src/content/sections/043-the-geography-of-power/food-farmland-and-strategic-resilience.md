The ability to feed a population depends on soils, growing seasons, water, technology, storage, transport and purchasing power. Fertile land is important, yet cities can be well supplied through trade while agricultural regions experience hunger if households lack income or access. Food security is not a simple equation between national farmland and mouths to feed.

Climate and geography influence harvest risks. Drought, flooding, frost, pests and conflict can disrupt production. Governments may diversify imports, maintain reserves, improve irrigation or build connections between producing and consuming regions. Such responses also create trade-offs: a dam can help irrigation while reducing downstream flow, and a strategic reserve costs money to store and rotate.

In wartime, grain routes, fertilizer, fuel and ports may be strategically important because modern agriculture depends on industrial inputs and transport. But 'self-sufficiency' is not always the safest outcome. Complete dependence on domestic production may expose a country to a single regional crop failure. Resilience often comes from diversified suppliers, functioning markets, emergency planning and stable relations rather than from isolation.
