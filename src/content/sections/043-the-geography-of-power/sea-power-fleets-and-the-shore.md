A navy can protect shipping, escort commerce, threaten an adversary's supply routes, support allies and project force ashore. Yet ships are only one part of sea power. Harbours, dry docks, sailors, intelligence, logistics, merchant fleets, industrial production and access agreements make distant naval operations possible. A carrier or submarine far from reliable maintenance and resupply can be impressive but strategically constrained.

Alfred Thayer Mahan, writing in the late nineteenth century, argued that maritime commerce, naval strength and bases helped explain the power of major states. His work remains influential, though it described particular historical conditions and should not be used as an eternal law. Air power, missiles, submarines, satellites and containerization changed the relationship between coastal fortifications, fleets and the global economy.

Sea control itself is relative. A military may be able to operate in an area for a period without denying all use to an opponent. A weaker actor may make passage costly through mines, missiles or drones even while lacking a large navy. The geography of nearby islands, coastlines and airfields can shape such contests. Today's maritime strategies require understanding both commercial traffic and the ways technology changes what it means to command a route.
