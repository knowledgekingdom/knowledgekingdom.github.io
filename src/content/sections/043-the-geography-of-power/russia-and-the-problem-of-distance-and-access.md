Russia offers a powerful example of geographic diversity: extensive plains, mountain frontiers, forests, northern seas and settlements separated by enormous distances. Historians and political analysts have long considered how its geography shaped transport costs, strategic depth, port development and relations with neighboring states. But such factors do not explain Russian policy without reference to rulers, institutions, identities and historical choices.

An ocean or a large land area does not guarantee secure access. Ice conditions, infrastructure capacity, maritime passage rules, foreign ports and the cost of supplying distant forces matter. Railways helped integrate parts of the empire and later the Soviet Union, while networks and settlement patterns left many regions reliant on long connections. Territorial extent created both strategic possibilities and administrative burdens.

The crucial caution is ethical as well as analytical: leaders may invoke geography to explain why they feel insecure, but insecurity does not establish a right to dominate neighboring countries. National borders and political autonomy are legal and political facts, not variables that disappear because a major power seeks strategic depth. A complete explanation connects perceived vulnerability with identifiable decisions and competing security interests.
