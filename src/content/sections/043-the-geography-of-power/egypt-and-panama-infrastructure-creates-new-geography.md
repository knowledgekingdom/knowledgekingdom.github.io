The Suez and Panama canals are striking examples of constructed geography. Engineers cut routes through land, dramatically altering distances for some voyages. Once opened, each canal became part of a global network of ports, shipping schedules, warehouses, insurance and trade. A canal can turn a narrow strip of land into an important commercial node, but it remains expensive to maintain and vulnerable to accidents, political disputes and environmental conditions.

A blockage at Suez may force ships between Europe and Asia to divert around southern Africa. Restrictions at Panama can alter patterns linking the Atlantic and Pacific, depending on vessel size and destination. The disruption is not identical for all commodities or shippers: some can switch routes, change delivery schedules or draw down inventories, while others cannot easily do so.

These cases show that strategic geography is dynamic. Canals did not change the locations of continents; they changed the cost structure of movement between them. Their importance may rise or fall with ship design, demand, alternative routes and climate. A map of shortest distances must therefore be paired with a map of usable capacity and with an understanding of who can manage the infrastructure.
