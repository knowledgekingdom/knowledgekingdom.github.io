In the early seventh century, the Byzantine and Sasanian states had endured a destructive series of wars. The conflict of 602–628 drew armies across Syria, Egypt, Mesopotamia and beyond. Both empires continued to possess formidable institutions, but prolonged fighting, political instability and the burden of taxation strained parts of their societies. A further problem was that many frontier populations had their own grievances and affiliations. Imperial religion, local church traditions, ethnic categories and political loyalty did not always line up neatly.

That background matters for the later Muslim conquests, but it must not become an all-purpose explanation. Exhaustion did not make conquest inevitable. Arab-Muslim armies still had to organize, fight, hold territory, negotiate surrender terms and create administrative relationships. Nor should we assume that Byzantine or Sasanian subjects everywhere greeted the conquerors as liberators. Reactions differed sharply by region and period. Some local elites cooperated, others resisted, and many families adjusted pragmatically to a new sovereign while maintaining older languages and religions.

A central lesson in historical causation is that <strong>an opportunity is not the same as a sufficient cause</strong>. Imperial weakness created openings; religious motivation, military leadership, Arabian alliances and institutions helped determine what happened next. The fall of Sasanian rule in Iran and the loss of Byzantine provinces altered a world already connected by trade, scholarship and religious dispute. Early Islam inherited and reshaped that connected world rather than replacing it with a blank slate.
