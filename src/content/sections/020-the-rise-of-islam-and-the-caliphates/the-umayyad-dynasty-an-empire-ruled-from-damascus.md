With Muawiya's establishment of authority in <strong>661</strong>, the caliphate entered a dynastic phase conventionally called <strong>Umayyad rule (661–750)</strong>. Damascus became its major governing center, benefiting from Syria's established towns, agriculture, military organization and administrative connections. Muawiya's political achievements included building a stable Syrian power base after the civil war. His choice to advance his son Yazid as successor helped make inheritance a central controversy. Many Muslims saw the new arrangement as a departure from ideals associated with earlier consultation, while defenders emphasized the need to avoid renewed fragmentation.

The dynasty did not enjoy uninterrupted peace. The <strong>Second Fitna</strong> followed Muawiya's death in 680 and involved rival claimants and regional factions before <strong>Abd al-Malik</strong> consolidated rule in the late 680s and early 690s. The political center had shifted away from Medina, where the community first formed. The caliph now governed provinces stretching across different ecologies and languages, and his authority depended upon armies, money, patronage, delegated governors and public symbols of legitimacy.

This transition was not simply a story of religious decline. Dynastic government allowed continuity and more predictable provincial administration in some periods, while also creating rivalries over inheritance and resources. A historian can acknowledge the real achievements of the Umayyad state and the violence by which it sometimes asserted power. The term <strong>“Islamic empire”</strong> describes a state under Muslim rulers; it does not mean that every resident was Muslim or that every official acted according to one uncontested religious standard.
