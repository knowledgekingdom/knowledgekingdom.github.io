According to Islamic tradition, Muhammad was born in Mecca around 570 CE, was orphaned young, and later worked in commerce. He married Khadija, whose support is prominent in the traditional accounts. Around 610, he began receiving revelations, understood by Muslims as communication from God through the angel Gabriel. Those revelations came to constitute the Qur'an, whose Arabic name means “recitation.” The Qur'an's teaching centers on the oneness of God, moral accountability, worship, charity and the coming judgment. It insists that wealth and status cannot excuse injustice and places Muhammad in a line of prophetic figures that includes Abraham, Moses and Jesus.

The message challenged more than individual belief. It confronted existing religious practices and asked a community organized partly through kinship and prestige to measure itself against moral obligations extending beyond status. Qur'anic passages repeatedly speak about the poor, orphans, contracts, honesty and the difference between genuine piety and conspicuous devotion. That does not mean seventh-century Muslims instantly created an egalitarian society. Every movement develops within material and political constraints, and later Muslim societies displayed sharp inequalities. It does mean that religious and social teaching cannot be separated from the new movement's public significance.

Early believers included people connected to Muhammad's household and different social backgrounds. Opposition developed among some Meccan leaders, while the traditional narratives describe harassment, protection offered by kin and the migration of some followers to the Christian kingdom of Aksum across the Red Sea. Specific details of these years are known predominantly from later Muslim accounts and should be dated cautiously. Their broad historical significance is clear: the movement was not yet an empire. It was a vulnerable religious community whose future depended on finding a place to survive and organize.
