An account focused exclusively on battles and dynasties would miss what made Islam durable. Muslims practice obligations that organize daily, weekly and annual life. The <strong>five pillars</strong>, as classically expressed in Sunni teaching, include the profession of faith (<strong>shahada</strong>), the five daily prayers (<strong>salat</strong>), prescribed almsgiving (<strong>zakat</strong>), fasting during <strong>Ramadan</strong> (<strong>sawm</strong>) and pilgrimage to Mecca (<strong>hajj</strong>) for those able to undertake it. Shi'i traditions have related but differently organized doctrinal and practical frameworks; the religious life of Muslims has never been uniform in every legal detail.

The Qur'an's emphasis on belief, accountability and moral action helped form a portable religious community. The direction of prayer, sacred recitation, calendrical practices, legal deliberation and charitable obligations connected distant worshippers without requiring the same local language, ethnicity or political ruler. These practices spread and developed through teaching, family life, mosques, study circles, trade networks and juristic discussion. Their precise formulations evolved over time, and later legal manuals should not be casually projected backward onto every community of the 620s.

Faith and politics are intertwined here but not identical. The religious meaning of revelation for Muslims is not something a historian can settle through archaeology. Conversely, government policies concerning taxation, armies and succession are historical matters open to investigation. Keeping both levels visible prevents two bad simplifications: that Islam was only a political machine, or that all political conflicts involving Muslim rulers were straightforward expressions of religious doctrine.
