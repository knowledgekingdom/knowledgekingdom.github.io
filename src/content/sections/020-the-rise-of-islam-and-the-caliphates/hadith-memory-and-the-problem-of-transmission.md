<strong>Hadith</strong> are reports concerning Muhammad's sayings, actions or approvals. They became important evidence for questions not answered in detail by the Qur'an alone. The compilation and assessment of hadith involved networks of teachers and students across distant regions, and scholars developed classifications for transmission chains and report content. Well-known Sunni collections associated with <strong>al-Bukhari</strong> and <strong>Muslim</strong> date to the ninth century, while Shia traditions developed different authoritative collections and approaches. The fact that compilations were written generations after Muhammad does not prove that every report was invented then; neither does a later attribution automatically establish the precise wording of a seventh-century event.

Historical research investigates what can be recovered about transmission, early use, variant formulations and the interests of transmitters. Religious scholars evaluate reports according to traditions of authenticity and authority developed within their communities. Some questions can be approached through both methods, but the assumptions and goals differ. It is possible to explain this distinction respectfully while avoiding the misleading claim that “Muslims all use exactly the same hadith books.” In fact, debates over interpretation have remained central to Muslim intellectual life.

A useful analogy comes from Chapter 4. Suppose a report appears in several later books. Mere repetition does not give us several independent witnesses if those books relied on one earlier transmitter. To determine independence, a historian looks for transmission pathways and early attestations. Conversely, a report linked through reliable early channels may offer valuable evidence, even when its surviving written form is later. What matters is not whether a source is late <strong>in isolation</strong>, but how its provenance, alternatives, genre and corroboration fit the question being asked.
