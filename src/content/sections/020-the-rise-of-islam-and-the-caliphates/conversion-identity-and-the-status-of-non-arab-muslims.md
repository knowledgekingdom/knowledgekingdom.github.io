As the number of non-Arab Muslims grew, the empire faced a contradiction. Religious belonging proclaimed a community of believers, but political and fiscal privileges often reflected early military settlement and Arabian ancestry. <strong>Mawali</strong>, a term frequently used for non-Arab clients and converts associated with Arab groups, occupied different positions across regions. Some achieved high status; others encountered unequal treatment. The details varied over time and must not be summarized as a permanent and universal rule.

Conversion also interacted with taxation. A government whose revenues depended partly on levies assessed on non-Muslim subjects could face practical questions when people converted. Some later accounts describe officials resisting the loss of revenues; reforms and local practices differed. We should not assume everyone converted solely to avoid taxes or that everyone refused conversion for economic reasons. Belief, family relationships, legal opportunities, prestige, scholarly teaching and political circumstances all mattered. As Muslim populations expanded, the older distinction between an Arab ruling military group and a non-Arab subject majority became increasingly difficult to sustain.

This helped weaken Umayyad legitimacy. Opposition came from several directions: supporters of the Prophet's family, dissatisfied provincial groups, rival Arab factions and movements critical of unequal treatment. The successful Abbasid movement built support especially in the east, notably in <strong>Khurasan</strong>, by appealing to alternative claims of rightful leadership and wider constituencies. No one slogan explains the revolution, but a common pattern appears: an empire that expands faster than its political bargain can adapt eventually faces demands to redefine who belongs and who benefits.
