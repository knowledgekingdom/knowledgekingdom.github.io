<strong>Uthman ibn Affan (644–656)</strong> presided over further military activity and significant administrative controversy. The empire's growth brought wealth, opportunities, disputes over provincial posts, and questions about who should benefit from conquest. Critics accused Uthman of favoring relatives and powerful associates; historians must distinguish the existence of such accusations from independent proof of every allegation. Ultimately, unrest culminated in his assassination in 656. That event created an acute question: who possessed the right and practical ability to punish his killers while maintaining the community's unity?

Islamic tradition associates Uthman's reign with a major step in standardizing the <strong>written Qur'anic consonantal text</strong>, as recitation and manuscript practices circulated across newly connected provinces. The later account describes efforts to produce authoritative copies and limit disputes. Early manuscripts and inscriptions attest to the Qur'an's presence in the first Islamic century, but scholars distinguish <strong>the date of an animal skin</strong> from the date ink was put upon it, and both from a complete account of textual transmission. The Birmingham Qur'an leaves, for example, have parchment radiocarbon dated to a range around <strong>568–645 CE</strong> at 95.4% probability; that is evidence about the parchment's likely age, not a direct timestamp for every scribal act.

Textual standardization was not identical to making all oral readings mechanically uniform. Later canonical recitation traditions preserve recognized variations within the broader textual tradition, and the history of early writing, pronunciation and manuscript transmission requires specialist analysis. A fair general history explains the traditional Uthmanic account, the evidence of early manuscripts, and the questions historians continue to investigate. It does not claim that one fragment by itself settles every religious or textual controversy.
