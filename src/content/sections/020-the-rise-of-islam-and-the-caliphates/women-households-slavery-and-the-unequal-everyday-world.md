Grand histories of caliphs can make the majority of people invisible. Farming families maintained irrigation, artisans manufactured ordinary goods, merchants handled contracts, religious teachers instructed children, and women participated in household management, trade, charity and scholarship. The Qur'an and early Muslim legal discussions addressed matters including marriage, inheritance and responsibility, but actual experiences differed enormously according to wealth, region, legal status, kinship and local custom. The well-known early importance of <strong>Khadija</strong> and <strong>Aisha</strong> in Muslim tradition challenges any historical account that treats women as absent from Islam's formation.

At the same time, these societies were hierarchical. <strong>Slavery</strong> existed in the late antique world and continued under Muslim rulers, with captives, people born into slavery and others incorporated through different channels. Enslaved people worked in households, agriculture, military systems and other settings. Islamic teachings and legal discussions encouraged manumission in various circumstances and regulated aspects of enslavement, but they did not abolish the institution in the early caliphates. It would be misleading to claim that Muslim rule produced universal equality; it would be equally misleading to erase instances of legal agency, intellectual contribution and the social diversity of enslaved or freed persons.

Economic rank mattered at least as much as the title of a dynasty. Rural producers funded armies through taxes and deliveries, while privileged officials could access wealth and courts. Religious communities also experienced different restrictions and opportunities. Reconstructing these lives requires papyri, contracts, archaeological remains, biographies and legal texts read critically together. A surviving law tells us what some jurists thought ought to happen; it does not alone prove what every household did in practice. That distinction is indispensable to writing a history of ordinary people rather than only their governors.
