Between the early 600s and the Abbasid centuries, Islam transformed the religious and political map of Eurasia and North Africa. That transformation began with Muhammad's preaching and the formation of a community in Mecca and Medina. It continued through succession disputes, conquest, provincial government, religious teaching, material networks and intellectual exchange. The caliphs were important, but the caliphate did not create every aspect of the worlds it ruled. Its institutions inherited much from older empires, and its inhabitants created forms of life no single ruler could completely direct.

The most important distinctions are now in view. <strong>The spread of Muslim political rule is not the same as the spread of individual conversion.</strong> <strong>Arabization is not identical to Islamization.</strong> <strong>Religious authority and imperial authority sometimes cooperated but sometimes conflicted.</strong> <strong>Shared Islamic identity did not mean one uniform society or a permanently united state.</strong> And the sources of early Islam are neither transparent windows nor useless inventions; they demand the same calibrated analysis we have practiced throughout this book.

The next historical journey will cross medieval trade routes between China, India, Central Asia, the Middle East, Africa and Europe. Those routes carried merchants and soldiers, pilgrims and translators, commodities and pathogens, inventions and stories. The rise of Islam was one of the great reorganizations of that connected world. Its legacy cannot be measured simply by the extent of a caliph's map. It also lies in languages, cities, worship, law, arguments, architecture and institutions that endured long after the imperial capitals changed.
