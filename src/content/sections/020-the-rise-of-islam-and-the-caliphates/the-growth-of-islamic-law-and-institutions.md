How should believers act when the founding prophet is no longer present to decide a dispute directly? The Qur'an, reports about Muhammad's practice, communal customs, reasoned interpretation and new circumstances all entered legal discussion. The field of <strong>fiqh</strong>—human understanding of religious law—developed through debate over worship, family matters, property, contracts and public responsibilities. It is important to distinguish <strong>sharia</strong>, often understood broadly as God's path or divine normative guidance, from the diverse historical judgments produced by jurists. No single court or ruler had a monopoly on interpretation across every Muslim society.

By the early medieval period, influential Sunni legal traditions became associated with scholars later remembered as <strong>Abu Hanifa</strong>, <strong>Malik ibn Anas</strong>, <strong>al-Shafi'i</strong> and <strong>Ahmad ibn Hanbal</strong>. Their schools—Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali—developed through students, debate, institutions and generations of scholarship. Shia communities formed their own legal and theological traditions, including the later <strong>Ja'fari</strong> school of Twelver Shia jurisprudence. These schools were not all fully organized during the lifetime of their eponymous figures. Their historical development is one of the great examples of how institutions emerge gradually through teaching and disagreement.

The relationship between religious scholars and governments could be cooperative, competitive or openly hostile. A ruler might appoint judges and use law for administration, while independent scholars claimed authority to interpret religious obligations. Local markets, marital arrangements, charitable foundations and inheritance questions generated practical legal work. The history of Islamic law is therefore not only about punishment, and it is not a single frozen code. It is a long series of attempts to connect authoritative texts, moral reasoning and changing social conditions.
