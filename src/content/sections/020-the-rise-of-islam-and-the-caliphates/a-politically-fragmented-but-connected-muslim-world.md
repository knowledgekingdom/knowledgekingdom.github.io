By the ninth and tenth centuries, the Abbasid caliphs faced powerful regional rulers who controlled armies and fiscal resources. In some places, dynasties acknowledged Abbasid religious prestige while administering territories with considerable independence. In others, alternative caliphal claims emerged. The <strong>Umayyad emirate of Córdoba</strong>, founded in 756, became a caliphate under <strong>Abd al-Rahman III in 929</strong>. The <strong>Fatimid</strong> dynasty, founded in <strong>909</strong> in North Africa and later centered on Egypt, claimed an Ismaili Shia imamate and caliphate in opposition to Abbasid authority. These competing centers developed distinctive legal, artistic and political histories.

The fragmentation challenges a simplistic map caption reading “the Islamic Empire” as though one capital exercised direct rule from Iberia to Central Asia for centuries. A Sunni Muslim in Central Asia, an Ismaili Shia official in Cairo and a Christian merchant in Córdoba inhabited different political worlds. Yet people and ideas moved among them. Merchants traveled, scholars corresponded, pilgrims headed toward Mecca, and religious texts circulated. A shared faith did not guarantee political obedience, and political competition did not eliminate religious or commercial connections.

This is an enduring pattern in world history: <strong>civilizational connection is not identical to political unification</strong>. Chapter 21 will examine more of the commercial and cultural networks of medieval Eurasia; Chapter 23 will follow Byzantine and Ottoman developments; and later chapters will revisit the changing intellectual traditions of the Muslim world. Here it is enough to understand that the caliphate's early expansion laid the foundations for many societies whose histories could not be controlled or summarized by one dynasty.
