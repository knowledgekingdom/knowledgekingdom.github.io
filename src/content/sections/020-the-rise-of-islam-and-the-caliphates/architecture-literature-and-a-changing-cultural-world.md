Islamic art and architecture emerged through continuity and innovation across the old Byzantine, Sasanian and regional worlds. Early mosques organized communal worship in new monumental settings. Calligraphy became an important visual art linked to the prestige of Qur'anic writing; geometric, vegetal and other decorative forms developed in different contexts. It is inaccurate to say that Islamic art never portrayed living beings: figurative representation occurs in many secular objects and settings, while devotional norms differ by context and tradition. Architecture, like law, cannot be reduced to one rule that never changed.

The <strong>Dome of the Rock</strong>, the <strong>Great Mosque of Damascus</strong>, Abbasid <strong>Samarra</strong>, and later Iberian and North African structures offer evidence of political legitimacy, materials, artisanship and local adaptation. Their forms often reveal older artistic techniques repurposed for new audiences. A monumental mosque communicates not only belief but also the ability of a patron to mobilize workers, land, revenues and skilled craftsmen. It can make religious authority visible in a city, while its actual use depends upon the lives of worshippers over time.

Arabic developed as a major literary and scholarly language, but it did not erase Persian, Berber, Coptic, Greek, Syriac or other tongues. Poetry, religious commentary, historical writing and scientific argument circulated among readers and listeners. The result was neither a single homogeneous culture nor a set of isolated local cultures. It was a network of practices that could travel, adapt and be challenged. This is why historians speak of <strong>Islamic worlds</strong> or <strong>Muslim societies</strong> when they wish to emphasize diversity within broad connections.
