Relations with Mecca entered a period of conflict. Muslim tradition records the Battle of Badr in <strong>624</strong>, at which the Medinan Muslims defeated a larger Meccan force, followed by the setback at Uhud in <strong>625</strong>. In <strong>627</strong>, during the Battle of the Trench or Confederates, Medina resisted a coalition siege. These episodes have deep religious meaning within Islamic memory, but a historian must also ask how supply, alliances, local geography and the changing reputation of leaders influenced their outcomes. Battle stories are rarely only about military technique.

The <strong>Treaty of Hudaybiyya</strong> in <strong>628</strong>, traditionally remembered as an agreement with Mecca that included a truce, is equally revealing. It shows how negotiation could alter power without immediate conquest. In <strong>630</strong>, Muhammad and his followers entered Mecca; the traditional narratives describe the city's surrender and the rededication of the Kaaba to monotheistic worship. Over the remaining years, alliances and delegations linked an increasing number of Arabian groups with the Muslim community. Some entered willingly, others under varying political pressures, and incorporation was not uniform.

Muhammad died in Medina in <strong>632</strong>. The Muslim community was already a significant Arabian political force, but the question of how it should be led after its founding prophet remained unsettled. The story has therefore reached its first central turning point. The movement had created a powerful shared identity, institutions and alliances. It had <strong>not</strong> produced an uncontested rule of succession, and later disagreements would profoundly shape Islamic political and religious history.
