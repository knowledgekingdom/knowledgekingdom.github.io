The Greek War of Independence began in 1821 and involved regional revolts, rival governing centers, foreign volunteers, Ottoman and Egyptian forces, and major-power naval intervention. An independent Greek state gained international recognition in the early 1830s, but its borders excluded many Greek-speaking or Orthodox communities. Political life did not become uniform at independence. Greek identity had been shaped by Orthodox institutions, classical learning, regional commercial networks and political organizing, none of which by itself predicted one final territorial settlement. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]

Serbian uprisings earlier in the century produced increasing autonomy within the Ottoman framework, with formal independence recognized at the Congress of Berlin in 1878. Different Serbian leaders, dynasties and local elites disagreed about institutions and foreign alliances. Romanian state formation followed another route: Moldavia and Wallachia united in 1859, and internationally recognized Romanian independence followed the war of 1877–1878 and the Berlin settlement. These cases demonstrate the range of ways states emerged: revolt, diplomacy, institutional union, war and recognition. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]

The region's histories were entangled with empires and one another. Orthodox affiliation did not imply a single national identity, and political labels did not map neatly onto language. Muslims, Jews, Catholics, Orthodox Christians and communities with mixed or changing affiliations inhabited cities and rural districts. National movements could create schools and institutions while intensifying disputes over who properly represented a district. The next chapter explores these intertwined histories more fully.
