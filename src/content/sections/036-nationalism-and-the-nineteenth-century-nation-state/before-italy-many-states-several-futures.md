The peninsula later unified as Italy contained distinct political units after 1815: the Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont, the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, the Papal States, duchies and Austrian-controlled territories. Italians shared many literary and cultural references, but everyday speech differed widely, and loyalty to city, region or dynasty remained powerful. National unification was therefore a political program, not the inevitable fulfillment of a fully formed state waiting to appear. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]

Giuseppe Mazzini's republican nationalism emphasized popular political education and a united independent Italy. Other reformers hoped for confederation, a papal role, or monarch-led constitutional unification. Camillo di Cavour, prime minister of Piedmont-Sardinia, pursued diplomacy and state power in ways that differed from Mazzini's republican ideals. Giuseppe Garibaldi's volunteers embodied yet another source of energy: popular mobilization and military leadership. These figures did not agree on the state they wanted even when they helped weaken existing rulers.

The Risorgimento is best understood as intersecting cultural, military, diplomatic and social processes. Nationalist histories sometimes turn it into a sequence of heroic biographies. That loses the participation of local activists and women, the effect of peasant conditions, the resistance of loyalists, and the influence of France and Austria. A more useful question asks why some projects for Italian unity succeeded while competing projects were defeated.
