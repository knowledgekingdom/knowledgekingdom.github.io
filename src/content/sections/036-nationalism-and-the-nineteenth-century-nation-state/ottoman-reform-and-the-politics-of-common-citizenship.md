The nineteenth-century Ottoman Empire governed Muslims and non-Muslims, speakers of Turkish, Arabic, Greek, Bulgarian, Albanian, Armenian, Kurdish and many other languages, and regions with different provincial histories. Its rulers faced military rivalry, fiscal constraints, diplomatic pressure and growing demands from local communities. Ottoman reformers increasingly argued that rebuilding state capacity required more predictable law, education, taxation, military systems and a shared imperial political identity. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]

The Tanzimat reforms, conventionally associated with 1839–1876, included the Gülhane Edict of 1839 and the Reform Edict of 1856. These documents promised changes in state obligations and the status and security of subjects, though implementation was unequal. The Ottoman Nationality Law of 1869 expressed a legal understanding of common membership, while the constitution of 1876 introduced a further, short-lived experiment in representative government. Reform did not eliminate the empire's religious and social hierarchies, nor did every subject embrace an overarching Ottoman political identity. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]\[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-22" class="cite">22</a>\]

Ottomanism—an ideal of common loyalty across religious and linguistic divisions—competed with movements for local autonomy and independence. Historians should not assume it was merely a cynical disguise or that national movements were bound to defeat it. Governments were attempting to solve a genuine coordination problem: how could diverse people claim equal political membership while preserving communities, customs and existing authority? The question also challenged Habsburg and Russian policymakers and remains relevant to states today.
