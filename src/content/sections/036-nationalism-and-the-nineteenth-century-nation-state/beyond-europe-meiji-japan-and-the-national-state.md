Nationalism did not remain a uniquely European political vocabulary. Japan's Meiji Restoration of 1868 transformed relations among imperial authority, domains, military organization and society. The new leadership pursued centralization, national institutions, industrial and military modernization, schools and a conscript army. It appropriated technologies and administrative models from abroad while developing claims about the emperor and a distinct national polity. The process was not simply the copying of a Western nation-state. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]

Japan's government reduced the autonomy of former domains and sought a stronger capacity to negotiate with Western powers. Modernization created winners and losers, including former samurai who opposed aspects of reform. Economic growth and education advanced alongside restrictions on political participation and the use of imperial ideology. Japan's later expansion in East Asia illustrates how a state could pursue sovereignty against Western pressure while becoming an imperial power in its own right.

Elsewhere national ideas interacted with different conditions. In Latin America independence preceded much nineteenth-century nation-building; governments struggled to integrate regions with contrasting social hierarchies and memories of colonial rule. In British India, reformist and political organizations developed complex arguments for representation and national rights. In China, later nineteenth-century defeats and reforms generated debates about state survival and political community. There was no single global sequence in which nations appeared before states or vice versa.
