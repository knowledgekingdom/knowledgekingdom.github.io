Historians cannot reliably measure national consciousness by counting flags in paintings. They compare schoolbooks, private correspondence, newspapers, petitions, election returns, association records, laws and administrative documents. They also study local conflict, migration, marriage, religious institutions and the changing use of language in everyday life. A speech that claims 'the whole nation demands independence' documents a political claim; it does not prove every inhabitant agreed.

Different sources reveal different layers. A census describes an official classification. A memoir may show an individual's changing loyalties but exaggerate how widely they were shared. A newspaper can demonstrate how activists framed an issue without indicating its total circulation or influence. A treaty establishes what states formally agreed, but not how people near a new border felt. Comparing independent types of evidence is particularly important where later national histories reinterpret earlier events.

Three common mistakes deserve special attention. <strong>Teleology</strong> assumes today's nations and borders were destined to exist. <strong>Essentialism</strong> imagines unchanging national characteristics across centuries. <strong>Retrospective unanimity</strong> projects the convictions of prominent activists onto everyone speaking a similar language. Sound history examines political choices that remained uncertain, including federal alternatives, multilingual patriotism and the possibility of peaceful coexistence.
