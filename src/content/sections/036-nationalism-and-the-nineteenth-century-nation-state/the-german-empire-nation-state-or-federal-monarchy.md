The 1871 German Empire was a federal state in which Prussia dominated by size, population and military weight. It retained other kingdoms, grand duchies and principalities, while an elected Reichstag participated in legislation and the budget. The chancellor was responsible to the emperor rather than to a modern parliamentary majority. Elections gave many men a national political voice, but voting rights did not by themselves create a system of fully accountable parliamentary government. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]

Religious and regional divisions persisted. The Kulturkampf involved conflict between state authorities and the Catholic Church, while socialist organization provoked restrictive laws and later welfare legislation. Poles, Danes, French-speaking inhabitants and others had distinct claims and experiences within imperial borders. Germany also became an overseas colonial power. National unification did not remove contested minorities or turn national belonging into a neutral, universal category.

The German case offers an enduring lesson: a national state can be legally and administratively consolidated without being culturally homogeneous or institutionally liberal in every respect. It can mobilize citizens while concentrating power. To understand its later foreign policy, distinguish national identity from the political incentives of its rulers and the military-diplomatic structure of Europe.
