Romantic writers, artists and collectors found political significance in vernacular languages, folklore, music, historical monuments and legends. Johann Gottfried Herder's ideas about cultural individuality helped inspire later interest in distinct peoples, though reducing him to a simple prophet of ethnic nationalism distorts his philosophy. Brothers Grimm collected stories; writers produced national epics; composers worked with popular musical forms. Scholars catalogued dialects and monuments in ways that could make distant villages appear part of one imagined cultural landscape. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]

Such cultural projects could preserve valuable traditions, but they also selected, edited and standardized. A song collected from one valley might be presented as representative of an entire nation; a medieval battle might be celebrated as the origin of modern citizenship despite radically different political institutions at the time. The important mechanism was not fraud but cultural organization: activists created shared reference points from a varied historical inheritance.

Language did not dictate political destiny. German speakers could live in Switzerland, Austria or Prussia and hold different political loyalties. Some nineteenth-century Poles, Jews, Slavs, Hungarians and Germans were multilingual. An individual could choose a literary language for education without renouncing a local dialect at home. Only when language was tied to schooling, state administration and claims of territorial sovereignty did its political consequences become especially intense.
