In the early nineteenth century many German-speaking territories belonged to the German Confederation, while important German-speaking populations also lived outside the political arrangements eventually adopted in 1871. The customs union known as the Zollverein, led by Prussia and expanded over time, reduced many internal trade barriers. Railways, industrial centers and financial connections increased economic interdependence. But trade integration was not the same thing as political unification, and its benefits were uneven. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]\[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]

Prussia possessed substantial military and administrative resources and competed with the Austrian Empire for influence. German liberals wanted constitutions and more representative government; industrial and commercial interests favored some common regulation and markets; conservatives worried about destabilizing their monarchies. The eventual Prussian-centered empire emerged from these tensions. German identity provided a language of political possibility, but which state would dominate and what liberties it would allow remained open questions.

A useful counterfactual is to imagine a Germany unified under Austria, a federal liberal constitution, or no single state at all. These alternatives faced real obstacles, yet their existence helps prevent hindsight from turning 1871 into destiny. Successful outcomes can make defeated plans look naïve, but contemporaries had no access to the historian's finished timeline.
