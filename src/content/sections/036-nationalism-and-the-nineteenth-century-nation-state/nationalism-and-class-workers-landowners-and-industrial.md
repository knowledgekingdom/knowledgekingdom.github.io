Industrialization restructured the societies in which nationalist ideas circulated. Railways linked provincial markets to capitals, factories concentrated populations, and new urban newspapers spread political languages. But industrial workers did not automatically identify with the state that governed them. They could organize around wages, religious affiliations, regional cultures, national independence, socialism or combinations of these. The relationship between class and nation changed with institutions and events.

Business leaders might support a common customs area and currency as tools for growth. Landowners could fear central taxation or agrarian reform. Workers might favor national protection against foreign competition while participating in international socialist organizations. For the poor, emancipation from dynastic rule might mean little if land tenure and labor conditions remained harsh. A nationally unified market did not guarantee equality of opportunity.

The point is not to divide history into 'economic real causes' and 'mere nationalist emotion.' National identity could shape which economic grievances became politically mobilizing, just as economic shocks could strengthen or weaken national claims. Consider the German Zollverein: commercial integration made a larger political union more practical, but no customs treaty alone decided who should possess sovereign authority. Economic networks supplied opportunities that political actors interpreted differently. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]
