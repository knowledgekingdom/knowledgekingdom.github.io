Piedmont-Sardinia adopted a constitutional framework in 1848 and developed institutions that allowed it to become the center of a monarch-led unification strategy. Cavour cultivated European relationships, supported commercial and infrastructural change, and sought French assistance against Austrian predominance in northern Italy. The diplomatic partnership with Napoleon III was instrumental, though it reflected French strategic interests as well as sympathy for Italian ambitions. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]\[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]

In 1859 Piedmont and France fought Austria. After victories that included Magenta and Solferino, Napoleon III negotiated an armistice that frustrated some Italian expectations. Lombardy was transferred into the expanding Piedmontese kingdom, while wider central Italian changes developed through local upheaval, plebiscites and political maneuvering. The sequence illustrates how independence movements could depend on great-power bargaining they did not control.

Plebiscites used in the unification process should not automatically be read as modern, unrestricted expressions of universal popular will. They operated in a period with uneven participation, political mobilization and pressure. Nonetheless the votes could express significant support among participating groups. Historical judgment requires attention to electoral rules, alternatives offered, and the political situation—not either blind acceptance or wholesale dismissal of every result.
