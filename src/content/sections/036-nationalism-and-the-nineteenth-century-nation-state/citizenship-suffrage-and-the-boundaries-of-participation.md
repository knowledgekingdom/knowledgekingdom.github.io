National belonging did not automatically grant democratic rights. In nineteenth-century states voting qualifications varied by sex, property, taxation, class and local law. Some unifying movements were led by constitutional monarchs; others by republicans or militant democrats. The German Empire introduced broad male suffrage for Reichstag elections while retaining strong monarchical executive power. Italy's electorate remained limited for much of the post-unification century, expanding gradually. France adopted universal male suffrage in 1848, yet changed regimes and constitutional rules afterward. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]

Women were central to family economies, education, journalism, philanthropy and political campaigns but were denied equal political participation in most national states. Women petitioned, organized associations, supported schools, published writing and pressed both national and feminist demands. Their exclusion was not a minor anomaly: it demonstrated how universal-sounding national language could coexist with restricted political membership. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

Citizenship also affected military service, taxation, property, freedom of movement and access to schooling. A state could expand civic responsibilities faster than political voice. When activists called on citizens to sacrifice for the nation, workers or women might ask what rights justified those sacrifices. The resulting disputes helped shape later mass politics, socialist parties and campaigns for suffrage.
