Imagine a fictional valley in 1875 containing six market towns. Its residents speak two related languages and several dialects; local merchants use a third language for trade; churches are divided between jurisdictions. A proposed new national border would place the railway on one side and the main market on the other. A nationalist newspaper calls the entire valley 'ancestral land,' while another movement claims that the school registers prove its majority. Neither side has asked the seasonal migrants who spend half the year elsewhere. This is a teaching example, not a description of a real Balkan district.

What information would be needed for a fair decision? First, establish population records without assuming that schooling, worship or everyday speech defines a person's political preference. Second, identify property, transport and security interests and assess who would gain or lose. Third, ask whether language rights, local autonomy or federal arrangements could protect multiple communities. Finally, recognize that votes held under military occupation or intimidation may not show free consent.

No nineteenth-century government had an easy solution to every version of this dilemma. Yet acknowledging complexity does not require moral paralysis: equality before the law, freedom of association, access to education and protection against forced displacement are defensible principles even when national borders remain contested. The case illustrates why the nation-state ideal could be politically powerful and practically incomplete.
