Not every nationalist movement defined belonging through ancestry, but some increasingly did. Racial pseudoscience, theories of national biological character, and conspiratorial antisemitism gained influence in parts of nineteenth-century Europe. Jewish communities faced different legal and social conditions across states: emancipation and expanded citizenship in some places coexisted with discrimination and recurrent violence. Modern political antisemitism often portrayed Jews as unassimilable outsiders despite long-standing residence and citizenship. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]

National movements could also target Roma communities, religious minorities, migrants and speakers of non-dominant languages. The categories varied across regions, and not all inequalities were products of nationalism; empire, local prejudice and economic conflict had older histories. Nevertheless nationalist programs sometimes transformed familiar differences into claims that a group did not properly belong in the political community.

There is an important conceptual distinction between asserting collective cultural rights and claiming that some people lack equal humanity or civil protection. A nation may preserve a language or commemorative tradition without denying minority citizenship. Conversely, a government may invoke assimilation while refusing the very equality that assimilation supposedly promises. The moral and legal problems of inclusion recur in later chapters on ideology, rights and international law.
