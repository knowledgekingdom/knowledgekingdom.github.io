Ireland's relationship with Britain combined parliamentary union, religious inequality, landholding conflict and economic dependence in complex ways. The Act of Union took effect in 1801. Catholic emancipation in 1829 removed major formal political restrictions, but grievances over land, representation and the constitutional union remained. Daniel O'Connell pursued mass political campaigns, while Young Ireland activists and later organizations considered more radical alternatives. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-27" class="cite">27</a>\]

The Great Famine of 1845–1852, driven by potato blight and intensified by social and political conditions, caused catastrophic mortality and emigration. Debates about British governmental responsibility, landlord power and relief policy became deeply connected to Irish national memory. Ireland's population loss had long-lasting consequences. Nationalism did not arise solely from famine, but famine transformed the moral and political weight of arguments about rule from London.

Later movements for tenant rights, Home Rule and independence overlapped without being identical. Some advocated parliamentary autonomy within the United Kingdom; others demanded complete separation. Irish national identity itself was not uniform: Protestant unionists, especially in parts of Ulster, and other communities opposed different forms of national self-government. The Irish example exposes a broader lesson: a demand by one national majority can be experienced by another group as a threat to its political security.
