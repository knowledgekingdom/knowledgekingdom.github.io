Liberal nationalists frequently linked constitutional rights to unity. They argued that a representative parliament required a politically conscious public and that national independence could shield citizens from distant autocrats. Conservatives sometimes defended inherited states against revolutionary upheaval, but others learned to use national symbols to reinforce monarchy, military service and social order. Nationalism did not remain the property of one ideology.

Republican and democratic activists often demanded wider participation than constitutional monarchists. Socialists debated whether workers' solidarity should cross national frontiers or whether national self-government could be a step toward social emancipation. Religious authorities might view the nation as a vehicle for education and moral duty, or distrust movements threatening ecclesiastical power. For a farmer, the central issue could still be a land tax, conscription or the protection of customary rights.

Ask two distinct questions when reading a nationalist speech: what is its <em>national</em> demand, and what social or institutional arrangement does it propose? An activist demanding Italian unity might want a republic; another might want a constitutional monarchy with a powerful king. They could temporarily cooperate against Austria and later quarrel about the resulting state.
