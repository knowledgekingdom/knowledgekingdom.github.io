France had possessed a centralized kingdom long before the nineteenth century, yet the formation of mass national citizenship was a prolonged process. French political upheavals in 1789, 1830, 1848 and 1870–1871 changed constitutional regimes repeatedly. The Third Republic, established after the fall of Napoleon III, pursued public education and civic symbolism. National identity increasingly found expression in schools, service to the state, republican ceremonies and common language. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]\[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]

A map showing modern France as one French-speaking block would misrepresent everyday life in many rural communities of the era. Breton, Occitan varieties, Basque, Alsatian, Corsican and numerous regional forms coexisted with standard French. Expanding schooling, military service, urban migration and national administration reduced some of those differences while also creating tensions about local rights. Historian Eugen Weber's influential phrase 'peasants into Frenchmen' captures this institutional transformation, though scholars debate variations and the degree to which it was imposed or negotiated. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]

The French case also complicates an easy opposition between 'civic' and 'ethnic' nationalism. Republican citizenship offered a legal language of equality but could be accompanied by compulsory cultural assimilation, exclusions and colonial hierarchies. A state might celebrate universal principles while demanding conformity to an official national language. Political inclusion and cultural pressure could occur at the same time.
