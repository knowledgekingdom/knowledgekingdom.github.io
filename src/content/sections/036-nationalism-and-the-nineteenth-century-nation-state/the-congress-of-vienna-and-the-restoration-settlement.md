The defeat of Napoleon led to the Congress of Vienna in 1814–1815. European diplomats sought a workable balance among great powers and tried to contain another revolutionary or expansionist upheaval. They restored and rearranged dynastic governments but also made pragmatic changes that could not recreate the earlier Europe exactly. The new German Confederation connected dozens of German-speaking and mixed-language states without forming a single sovereign Germany. Many Italian territories remained divided among monarchies and under Austrian influence. Poland's territories continued to be ruled largely by Russia, Prussia and Austria, although the details of administration varied. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]

The Vienna settlement was not a complete refusal of reform. Constitutions existed or were later introduced in some states, governments expanded their bureaucracies, and police surveillance coexisted with expanding universities and commerce. The problem for national activists was that diplomatic legitimacy generally rested on recognized rulers and treaties, not on a principle that every self-described nation must govern itself.

Consider three groups observing the same settlement. A Habsburg official might praise security after devastating wars. An Italian student might object to foreign armies and political fragmentation. A Polish patriot might perceive the division of a historical political community as an injustice. International diplomacy could stabilize interstate relations while leaving national aspirations unsatisfied. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]
