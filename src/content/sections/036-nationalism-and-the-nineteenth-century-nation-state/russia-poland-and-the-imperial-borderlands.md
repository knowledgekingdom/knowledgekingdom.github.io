The Russian Empire was another multinational polity, extending across eastern Europe, the Caucasus, Siberia and Central Asia. Imperial structures combined autocratic authority with different regional laws, local elites and religious communities. After the Napoleonic Wars, the Congress Kingdom of Poland possessed a constitution and limited institutions under the Russian emperor, but conflict over the meaning and extent of these arrangements led to the November Uprising of 1830–1831. The January Uprising of 1863–1864 again challenged Russian domination and was suppressed. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

Polish nationalism was sustained by schools, churches, literature, clandestine networks and emigration, but Polish-speaking societies differed over class, religion and political strategy. Polish elites had historically governed regions inhabited also by Ukrainians, Lithuanians, Belarusians and Jews, whose interests did not automatically match Polish national programs. The partitions of Poland were a historical injustice to many Poles; this fact does not settle all questions about competing national claims in the former Polish–Lithuanian lands.

Russian rulers expanded administration and promoted Russian language or imperial loyalty in different ways and periods, often described under the broad term <em>Russification</em>. These policies varied across the empire and over time. Reform also occurred, especially the emancipation of serfs in 1861, but legal change did not resolve land poverty or nationalist grievances. The empire's national problem was therefore connected to agrarian inequality, central administration, military security and the control of education.
