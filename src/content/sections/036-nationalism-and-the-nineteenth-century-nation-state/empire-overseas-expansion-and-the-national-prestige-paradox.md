Nationalism could present itself as a movement against imperial subjection while also becoming a justification for domination elsewhere. Newly unified Germany and Italy eventually sought overseas colonies; older national states such as Britain and France expanded imperial control. Newspapers and politicians depicted colonies as evidence of national greatness, sources of economic advantage and symbols of strategic status. The same rhetoric that praised European self-government frequently denied that colonized peoples deserved equal sovereignty. \[<a href="/036-nationalism-and-the-nineteenth-century-nation-state/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]

There were important differences between national movements. Some condemned empire or supported the political rights of other peoples; others embraced racial hierarchies and expansionist ambitions. Imperial policies were not caused by nationalism alone: investors, military planners, missionaries, political leaders and local partners pursued diverse objectives. Still, national prestige could turn colonial rivalry into domestic political competition. Chapter 35 showed how these pressures contributed to Africa's partition.

The paradox is worth holding in view: why might a population celebrate independence from an old empire while supporting its own government's overseas expansion? One explanation is the selective application of rights, reinforced by racist claims that some peoples were not ready to govern themselves. Another is the belief that national security required colonies and strategic resources. An explanation is not a justification; it shows how political ideals could be narrowed to those recognized as members of the nation.
