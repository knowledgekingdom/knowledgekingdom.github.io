By the end of the nineteenth century national identities had become central to European politics and were gaining global influence. States used schools, militaries, censuses and public symbols to mobilize populations. Activists invoked national dignity to oppose domination and secure representative institutions. Unification in Italy and Germany changed the European balance of power. National claims also challenged the Habsburg, Ottoman and Russian empires without producing one universally acceptable map of peoples and territories.

The promises were significant: wider participation, political accountability, cultural survival and the claim that rulers should answer to the people they govern. The dangers were also substantial: exclusionary definitions of membership, forced assimilation, competing border claims and imperial expansion carried out in the name of national prestige. Nationalism has no single moral outcome independent of the institutions and rights attached to it.

Chapter 37 will examine the Balkans and the Eastern Question in greater detail, where national movements, imperial reform, religious institutions and great-power diplomacy intersected. Chapters 38–40 will reveal how unresolved territorial and alliance questions fed into devastating wars. Carry forward a disciplined question: <strong>who is included in a claim made in the name of the nation, who is excluded, and what institutions turn that claim into power?</strong>
