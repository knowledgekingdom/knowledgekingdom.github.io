After Stalingrad, Soviet forces continued their costly offensive recovery, while Germany attempted to stabilize its position. The Battle of Kursk in July 1943 became associated with exceptionally intense armored combat, though popular claims about one tidy 'largest tank battle' require careful specification of what is being counted. Soviet operational planning, defensive preparations, reserves and growing production helped blunt German attacks. The Red Army gradually gained the initiative, but liberation of occupied territory brought further destruction, displacement and difficult political questions about the future of eastern Europe.

In July 1943 Allied forces invaded Sicily. Mussolini was removed from power, and Italy signed an armistice in September, but Germany continued to occupy much of the country and established a fascist client regime in the north. The Italian campaign required prolonged fighting against prepared mountain defenses. Resistance developed alongside civil conflict and reprisals against civilians. Italy illustrates why a state's armistice does not necessarily end the war for its population and why changing alliances can create both military opportunity and political disorder. \[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]
