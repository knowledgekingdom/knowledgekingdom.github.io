Military occupation changed daily life through rationing, requisitioning, identity papers, forced labor, censorship and the threat of collective punishment. People adapted in different ways: working for survival, exchanging goods illegally, fleeing, resisting, assisting occupiers or protecting family members. These categories often overlapped under extreme pressure. In occupied Poland, the Soviet territories, Greece, China and elsewhere, hunger and disease claimed many lives far from formal battle lines. The great difficulty for historians is to recover ordinary choices without implying that civilians had complete freedom.

Forced labor supplied German industry, agriculture and military construction, including labor from occupied Europe and prisoners of war. Japanese occupation economies similarly relied on coercive mobilization. Labor camps and deportations scattered people across national boundaries. Families lost track of relatives and survivors often had nowhere to return after liberation. The economic story of the war thus includes not only innovation and mass production but dispossession, stolen property and coerced work.
