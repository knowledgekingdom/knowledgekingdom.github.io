As Allied forces advanced, Nazi officials intensified repression and forced many prisoners on death marches away from camps threatened by liberation. Soviet armies entered eastern Europe, while Western forces crossed into Germany. In April 1945 Soviet troops fought their way into Berlin. Hitler died by suicide on 30 April, and Germany's armed forces surrendered unconditionally in early May; 8 May is celebrated in many countries as Victory in Europe Day, while different time zones and commemorative traditions account for the observance of 9 May elsewhere.

Liberation had more than one meaning. For Holocaust survivors it could end direct Nazi captivity, yet also expose the destruction of families and communities. In eastern Europe the defeat of Nazi rule was followed by expanding Soviet political control, which many people experienced as a loss rather than recovery of independence. Allied troops encountered displaced persons, ruined infrastructure and evidence of mass crimes. In territories liberated from Japanese occupation in Asia, the political situation was likewise unsettled. Military defeat was the necessary condition for ending Axis aggression, not a guarantee of a peaceful or just aftermath. \[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-33" class="cite">33</a>\]
