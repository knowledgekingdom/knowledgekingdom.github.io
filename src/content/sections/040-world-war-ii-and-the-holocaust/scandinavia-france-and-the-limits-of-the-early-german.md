After months of limited Western Front fighting, Germany invaded Denmark and Norway in April 1940, partly to secure maritime routes and strategic access. In May its offensive through the Low Countries and the Ardennes broke Allied operational assumptions. German forces reached the Channel, encircling major Allied units. The evacuation at Dunkirk saved a large part of the British Expeditionary Force but required leaving substantial equipment behind; it was a retreat, not a conventional battlefield victory. France signed an armistice in June 1940, and Britain faced the prospect of invasion.

France's defeat did not settle the political fate of French people. Occupied territory, the Vichy government, Free French forces and resistance organizations represented different experiences and choices. Vichy authorities administered parts of France under German domination and participated in persecution. The speed of the 1940 campaign can tempt us to label Germany's army invincible; yet victory depended on particular conditions, decisions by both sides, logistics and luck. German commanders would later confront enemies and distances that made the same operational rhythm impossible to sustain. \[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-6" class="cite">6</a>\]
