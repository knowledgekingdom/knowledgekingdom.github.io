The Holocaust did not begin with the opening of a killing center. After taking power in 1933, the Nazi regime used propaganda, violence, administrative exclusion and discriminatory laws to drive Jews out of German public life. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped Jews of crucial civil rights; coordinated anti-Jewish violence in November 1938, known as Kristallnacht, showed how state-sponsored persecution could become openly lethal. Jews were not an army or political organization; they were people of different nationalities, languages, beliefs and political views marked for persecution by Nazi racial ideology.

War radically enlarged the reach of this persecution. The occupation of Poland placed millions more Jews under German rule, and ghettos concentrated communities in overcrowded conditions where hunger, disease and coercion caused widespread death. Officials confiscated property and exploited labor. The Nazi persecution of European Jews developed through decisions taken at several levels of government and occupation administration. Historians debate precisely how specific decisions were made and communicated, but not the central facts of Nazi responsibility or the genocide itself. The Holocaust—the systematic, state-sponsored murder of about six million European Jews—was a defining crime of the period. \[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]\[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]
