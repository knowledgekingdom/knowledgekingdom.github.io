The interwar breakdown discussed in Chapter 39 set the stage but did not make war inevitable. Nazi Germany's leaders rejected the settlement after 1918 and pursued territorial expansion, racial domination and destruction of supposed ideological enemies. Fascist Italy invaded Ethiopia and sought a Mediterranean empire. Japanese military leaders sought regional hegemony in China and elsewhere in East and Southeast Asia. These aims were not interchangeable: their ideological justifications, strategic constraints and local political circumstances differed. Still, each regime used organized violence to revise borders and subordinate populations.

Appeasement, rearmament and economic pressure are often treated as rival explanations of the road to war. In reality they operated together. Britain and France wished to avoid another continental slaughter and were buying time to improve military readiness, but concessions encouraged German confidence that further demands might succeed. The Munich Agreement of 1938 transferred the Sudetenland from Czechoslovakia without a democratic mandate from the affected state. Germany then occupied the Czech lands in March 1939, exposing the inadequacy of the argument that Nazi demands were confined to Germans living outside the Reich.
