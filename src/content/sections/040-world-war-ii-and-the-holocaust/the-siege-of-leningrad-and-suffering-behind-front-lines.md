Leningrad endured a prolonged siege beginning in 1941, during which starvation, shelling, cold and disease killed large numbers of civilians. Its survival depended on extraordinary endurance, dangerous supply routes, changing front lines and state organization. A military map showing an encircled city can obscure what siege meant in daily life: food cards, funerals, scavenging fuel, caring for children and the fear that another winter would be unsurvivable. Diaries and personal accounts show both solidarity and desperation.

Similar human consequences occurred elsewhere. Civilians in occupied Soviet villages faced forced labor, destruction of homes and massacres. Soviet authorities deported certain ethnic communities and harshly punished perceived disloyalty. The violence of Nazi occupation was deliberate and systematic, but it does not follow that every act by the Soviet wartime state was legitimate. A serious history of victory over Nazi Germany can recognize Soviet sacrifice while examining coercion, political repression and human rights violations under Stalin.
