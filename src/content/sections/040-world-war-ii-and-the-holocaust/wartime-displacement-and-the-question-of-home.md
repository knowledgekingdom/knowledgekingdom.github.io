Europe and Asia contained millions of displaced persons, prisoners, refugees and former forced laborers by 1945. Some were repatriated; others feared persecution if returned. Border transfers and population exchanges uprooted communities who had lived in the same places for generations. The forced flight and expulsion of ethnic Germans from parts of central and eastern Europe, Soviet population transfers and the aftermath of Japan's imperial collapse created new humanitarian problems. Jewish survivors often found homes occupied, families destroyed and antisemitism still present.

Displacement illustrates a difference between ending a war and establishing a just peace. Governments needed transport, shelter, food and legal procedures, but postwar administration also reflected security priorities and political prejudice. The United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration and other agencies provided assistance on an immense scale, yet resources were limited. Later refugee law, human rights instruments and international migration regimes developed partly in response to such crises.
