Soviet soldiers reached Auschwitz in January 1945, and Western armies liberated other concentration camps during the final months. Many victims had already been killed, forced onto death marches or left without adequate food and medical care. Photographs of emaciated survivors shocked viewers and became enduring images of Nazi crimes. Yet photographs alone cannot explain who designed deportations, how property was seized or why perpetrators selected particular groups. Historians reconstruct the process through orders, transports, camp records, eyewitness testimony, perpetrator statements and demographic evidence.

The scale of murder made documentation urgent. The destruction of records by retreating Nazi authorities complicates reconstruction, but there is abundant convergent evidence for the genocide. Holocaust denial exploits normal uncertainty about particular locations or totals to make false claims about the established whole. The response should not be rhetorical outrage alone; it should demonstrate independent documentary chains. Survivors' voices are crucial, but no survivor could witness the entire system. Responsible history combines personal testimony with archival, material and comparative evidence. \[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]\[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]
