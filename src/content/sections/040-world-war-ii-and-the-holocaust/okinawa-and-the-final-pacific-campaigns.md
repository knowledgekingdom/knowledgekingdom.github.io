By 1944 the United States and its allies had seized strategically important Pacific positions and could increasingly strike Japan's home islands. The Philippines campaign involved major land and naval battles, the destruction of Manila and terrible civilian losses. In 1945 American forces captured Iwo Jima and Okinawa at extraordinarily high cost. The Okinawa campaign involved soldiers, local civilians, forced mobilization and mass death; survivors' experiences show why an island should not be treated only as a military objective on a map. Japanese units faced severe shortages, while Allied plans assumed a potentially costly invasion of the home islands.

Naval blockade, submarine warfare, mining and air attack disrupted Japan's transport and fuel supplies. American incendiary bombing destroyed large urban districts, including in Tokyo in March 1945. These operations caused extensive civilian suffering, and their effects must be assessed separately from military battlefield losses. The Japanese government was divided about ending the war, and its leaders had not yet accepted Allied surrender terms. The end came through a series of decisions and shocks, not an automatic consequence of winning the last island battle. \[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]\[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-31" class="cite">31</a>\]
