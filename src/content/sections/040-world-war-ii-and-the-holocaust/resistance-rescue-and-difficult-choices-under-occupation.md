Resistance took many forms. Armed uprisings, including the Warsaw Ghetto uprising in 1943, were dramatic, but underground teaching, the documentation of crimes, forged papers, food smuggling and hiding children also mattered. Some Partisan groups fought occupying forces; others faced hostile local populations, shortages, antisemitism or internal conflict. The Soviet, Yugoslav, French, Polish, Italian and other resistance movements had different goals and later political trajectories. Their experiences should not be merged into a single heroic story without attention to evidence and context.

The words <em>rescue</em> and <em>collaboration</em> describe actions, not unchangeable national characters. Some individuals protected persecuted neighbors while others profited from their exclusion. Occupation authorities created extreme danger, but personal choices still existed within unequal and often terrifying conditions. Ethical evaluation should neither demand impossible levels of courage from every victim nor excuse officials who facilitated deportation by citing mere obedience. When discussing rescue, ask who was protected, who remained exposed, what state authorities did and which parts of the population later appeared in public commemoration. \[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]\[<a href="/040-world-war-ii-and-the-holocaust/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]
