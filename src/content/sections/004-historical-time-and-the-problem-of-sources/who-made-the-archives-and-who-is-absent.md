The archive seems like a neutral warehouse, but somebody decided what to record, what to keep, and which collection deserves funding. Governments may preserve tax collections, land disputes, census records, and criminal files because administration requires them. Families preserve letters and photographs because relatives matter to them. Religious institutions preserve texts because they shape worship, identity, and law. Colonial bureaucracies produced documents to govern territories and extract resources. These differences leave deep imprints on what later historians can study.

Consider a census. It may provide powerful demographic evidence, but its categories are not timeless natural facts. Officials define household, occupation, language, religion, ethnicity, and other classifications for administrative purposes. People may answer strategically, be misclassified, or be omitted. The result can be simultaneously essential evidence and a record of how the state imagined the population. When comparing two censuses, ask whether categories or methods changed. A sudden statistical rise in a community's apparent size might reflect changed classification rather than rapid population growth.

Court records are another revealing example. They offer vivid descriptions of everyday conflict, property, honor, family, labor, and violence, especially for people who left no personal diaries. Yet the surviving text is often filtered through clerks, legal formulas, interpreters, judges, and institutions with unequal power. A defendant's words may be paraphrased. A dispute documented in court may be unusual precisely because it became an official problem. The absence of complaints from a community does not prove the absence of injustice; it may mean that members lacked safe access to the legal system.

History has become richer by combining archives with archaeology, environmental records, material culture, and approaches that ask whose perspective the records exclude. That is not a license to invent experiences that are missing. A responsible reconstruction distinguishes documented facts, plausible but uncertain inferences, and things we simply cannot recover. Sometimes the most accurate sentence in an important book is: <strong>“The surviving evidence does not let us answer this question confidently.”</strong>
