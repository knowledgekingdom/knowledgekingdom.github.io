A <em>primary source</em> is evidence produced in the period or circumstances under study, or by a participant or observer relevant to the question. A <em>secondary source</em> analyzes and interprets primary evidence, often placing it within a wider scholarly debate. These definitions are useful, but the boundary depends on what you are investigating. An eighteenth-century history of ancient Rome is secondary if your question concerns Julius Caesar; it becomes a primary source if your question is how eighteenth-century Europeans imagined Rome.

Primary does not mean automatically more reliable. An eyewitness can misremember; a political speech can exaggerate; a document can deliberately deceive. A careful modern synthesis that compares numerous independent sources may be more reliable for a broad claim than a single contemporary account. Conversely, a modern author who repeats one sensational quotation without checking it can be less useful than the original account examined critically. The source's <strong>relationship to the question</strong> and the quality of its evidence matter more than the status label.

Think about researching a food shortage. A trader's letter complaining about prices tells us something about perception and trade; a series of market-price records may reveal trends, if collection methods are consistent; weather evidence might reveal a plausible environmental pressure; tax registers might show policy changes. The trader may be exaggerating to influence a recipient. The price records might omit informal exchange. Weather may affect harvests without determining policy. All four sources can be useful, but none should be allowed to answer questions beyond its reach.

A robust source analysis asks about <strong>origin</strong> (who made it, when, where?), <strong>purpose</strong> (what was it trying to accomplish?), <strong>audience</strong> (who was meant to see it?), <strong>content</strong> (what precisely does it say or show?), <strong>context</strong> (what else was happening?), <strong>limits</strong> (what cannot it establish?), and <strong>corroboration</strong> (what independent materials agree or conflict?). An untrustworthy narrator can provide excellent evidence of the narrator's priorities, prejudices, or propaganda methods. The historian's job is not merely to accept or reject a source, but to determine what it can support.

The Library of Congress recommends a practical sequence when examining materials: observe, reflect, and question before jumping to an explanation. That is valuable partly because it makes us separate what is directly visible from what we assume it means. A photograph showing a packed square might directly establish the presence of a crowd at an identifiable place and time, once authenticated; it does not by itself show the political opinions of everyone present. A letter that says “the people are satisfied” establishes that the writer reported satisfaction; it does not independently establish what the people actually felt.
