We naturally trust someone who says, “I was there.” Presence is relevant: a witness may know details an outsider could not. Yet people notice different things under pressure, remember selectively, and interpret events through their own position. The court official and the demonstrator standing in the same square may describe the same confrontation in sharply different ways. Differences do not automatically prove that one invented the event. They may reflect vantage points, motives, information available at the time, or later changes in memory.

Suppose three contemporaneous accounts describe a city fire. One was written by a merchant concerned about inventory; another by a municipal officer concerned about damage to public buildings; a third by a resident concerned about family members. Each is likely to notice different locations and different consequences. We gain an account of the disaster by combining them with fire maps, insurance claims, archaeological traces, and records of rebuilding. The witnesses' disagreement might even reveal how unevenly different neighborhoods were affected.

A historian weighs <strong>temporal proximity</strong>: was a statement written during the event, the next week, or decades later? Immediate records reduce some forms of memory distortion but can be confused or incomplete. Later memoirs may benefit from reflection and wider evidence, while also becoming vulnerable to hindsight, self-justification, and later political narratives. A statement is not credible merely because it is immediate or incredible merely because it is late. Each type has a different risk profile.

Testimony is especially important when exploring violence, persecution, displacement, or experiences underrepresented in official records. A survivor's account can capture human realities that administrative documents conceal. Respectful analysis does not mean treating the witness as a perfect recording device. It means listening carefully, considering the testimony's context and scope, and looking for corroboration where possible. Detailed recollection can be compelling without requiring that every date or sequence be flawless.

Oral histories are not simply substitutes for written archives. An interview might provide memories of a village's social relations, local speech, everyday routines, emotional meaning, and events that bureaucracies overlooked. The interview itself is also a new historical event shaped by the questions asked, the relationship between interviewer and speaker, and how the recording is edited or stored. A responsible historian records those conditions when possible. When we hear a voice from the past, we ask both what happened and how a memory has been formed, maintained, and shared.
