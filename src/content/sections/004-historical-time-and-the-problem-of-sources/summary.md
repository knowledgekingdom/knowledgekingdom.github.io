<strong>Ten principles worth keeping.</strong> (1) Chronology establishes sequence, not cause. (2) No year zero separates 1 BCE and 1 CE. (3) Different calendars and periods require careful translation. (4) A primary source is defined in relation to the question. (5) Authentic sources can be mistaken, partial, or deceptive. (6) Survivorship and institutional power shape the archive. (7) Archaeological finds gain meaning from context. (8) Dating an object is not always dating an event. (9) Historical explanations combine structures, triggers, and choices. (10) Genuine disagreements should be distinguished from denial of overwhelmingly documented events.

<strong>The conversation test.</strong> If someone asks whether history is just a collection of opinions, explain why the answer is no without claiming that historians always agree. Use one historical case from this chapter, identify at least two kinds of evidence, and name one genuine uncertainty. If you can do that, you have begun thinking historically rather than merely collecting facts.
