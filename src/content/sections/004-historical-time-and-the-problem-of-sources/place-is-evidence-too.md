Dates tell us when. Geography often helps us understand how. A river may permit transport and provide water; a mountain range can impede some movements while offering strategic passes; seasonal winds can shape trade connections. But geography does not dictate a single destiny. The same sea can be a barrier for one technology and a highway for another. Different communities may exploit the same landscape in radically different ways.

A historical map is itself a source, not a transparent photograph of the past. A nineteenth-century imperial map may use colors to imply firm control over regions where state authority was uneven. A map drawn after a war may present negotiated boundaries as if they had existed naturally. Political maps rarely show all the overlapping claims of pastoralists, merchants, religious authorities, and local communities. Even a map based on valid geographic measurements reflects a choice about what to emphasize.

Distance also changes over time in practical terms. Two settlements may be 200 kilometers apart, but travel between them could take days through mountainous terrain, while rivers or coastal vessels might make a much longer route easier. Supply lines influence military campaigns; proximity to ports influences commerce; climate affects agricultural possibilities. Historical reasoning therefore often requires reconstructing roads, river courses, climate patterns, transport technology, and political checkpoints rather than judging the past by modern highways and national borders.

Digital historical maps can be powerful, but their apparent precision may be deceptive. A bright colored line marking a kingdom's boundary in 900 CE may conceal enormous uncertainty. When a map uses a modern boundary to represent an ancient people, ask whether the equivalence is defensible. When a town's location is known only approximately, its marker should not suggest GPS accuracy. As you read the atlas chapters later in this book, treat map legends, dates, sources, and degrees of certainty as part of the argument, not optional decorations.
