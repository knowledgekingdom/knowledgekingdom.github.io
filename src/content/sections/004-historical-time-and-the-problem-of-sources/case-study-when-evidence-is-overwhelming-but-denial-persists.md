Some historical questions are genuinely open to competing interpretations. Others are supported by an enormous convergence of evidence. The <strong>Holocaust</strong> is an essential example of the latter: Nazi Germany and its collaborators persecuted and murdered approximately six million European Jews, alongside the persecution and murder of millions of other victims under Nazi policies. The historical record includes perpetrator documents, administrative records, transports and deportation evidence, photographs, surviving sites and artifacts, testimony, demographic studies, and records assembled during and after the war.

At the first Nuremberg trial, prosecutors used many documents produced by German agencies and officials themselves. Those records became part of a large body of evidence, not a single witness's claim. In historical research, this kind of <strong>convergence</strong> matters. Sources created for different purposes and by different actors can support the same basic conclusion. Even if one witness misremembers a detail or one document has a disputed interpretation, the broader conclusion does not collapse when independent evidence remains massive and consistent.

This is why the phrase “historians disagree” must be used carefully. Scholars can debate how policies developed, the roles of particular institutions, decisions made at certain stages, local participation, or the effects of earlier ideology without seriously disputing that the genocide occurred. Treating denial of an extensively documented atrocity as just another equally plausible “side” misrepresents the evidentiary situation. Intellectual openness means examining claims according to their evidence, not awarding all claims equal credibility merely because they are asserted.

This case also demonstrates the moral purpose of careful method. Recording testimonies responsibly, preserving documents, reconstructing administrative decisions, and clarifying the scale and chronology of crimes help prevent public memory from drifting into comforting myths or conspiracies. The distinction between <strong>established fact</strong>, <strong>open interpretation</strong>, and <strong>fabricated denial</strong> is essential to responsible historical conversation. Good history is capable of both uncertainty and warranted certainty.
