A person who lived in the year 1000 did not wake up in the “Middle Ages.” They woke up on a particular morning, under a particular government or customary authority, with family obligations, food to prepare, crops to tend, disputes to settle, and some idea of what tomorrow might bring. “Middle Ages” is a later historian's organizing label. To people living then, the future was unknown, just as it is for us. A powerful historical habit is therefore to resist turning the past into a series of inevitable stepping stones toward the present.

We can ask three separate questions about time. <strong>When</strong> did something happen? <strong>How long</strong> did it take? <strong>What changed—and what continued?</strong> An official declaration may take a day. The changes that make it possible may build for decades. A new invention can appear in one place centuries before it changes ordinary life widely. A dynasty may end at a date printed in a textbook while patterns of farming, language, and family life continue mostly unchanged. A single date often names an event; it rarely explains the process that produced it.

Historical scale alters how we perceive causes. On the scale of a week, an army might retreat after a failed assault. On the scale of a generation, the same retreat might be one moment in a larger war shaped by supply networks, tax revenues, alliances, morale, and political legitimacy. On the scale of centuries, that war may belong to the longer formation of states and institutions. All three scales can be legitimate. The danger appears when a short event is treated as the whole explanation for a long transformation, or a broad trend is used to erase the choices of people at a specific moment.

A timeline offers <strong>sequence</strong>, not by itself <strong>causation</strong>. If Event A happened before Event B, A might have contributed to B, but sequence alone proves nothing. The historian must explain the mechanism linking them and compare alternative explanations. One useful technique is to mark a timeline in three colors: events, longer processes, and later consequences. For the development of a state, declarations may be events; education, military reform, and nationalist organization may be processes; diplomatic recognition and revised institutions may be consequences. The categories can overlap, but forcing yourself to name them makes your thinking more precise.

Try a mental experiment. Suppose a monarch signs a constitution on Monday, while royal officials continue governing much as before. Did the country become constitutional on Monday? Legally, perhaps something decisive changed. Socially and politically, the answer requires further evidence. Effective limits on power may take years to develop—or might not develop at all. When you learn a new date in this book, ask which kind of change the date measures.
