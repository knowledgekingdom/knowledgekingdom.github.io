India and Pakistan emerged as independent states in 1947 amid the violence and political upheaval of partition. Their subsequent rivalry has involved territorial disagreements, competing national identities and changing relationships with external powers. India conducted a nuclear test in 1974, and both countries conducted tests in 1998. Their choices arose from different domestic and international circumstances, including India's relationship with China and Pakistan's perception of its security relationship with India.

The presence of nuclear weapons did not end their disputes. The Kargil conflict of 1999 and later crises have generated considerable scholarly debate about the extent to which nuclear possession shaped restraint and escalation. Some analysts argue that the fear of catastrophic consequences contributed to limiting conflict; others emphasize that the same environment may create dangerous assumptions about the willingness of opponents to respond. It is difficult to isolate these effects from domestic institutions, international diplomacy and conventional military circumstances.

Comparative analysis is most useful when it recognizes differences among cases. South Asia is not merely a smaller version of the Cold War. Its geographical proximity, historical disputes, political institutions and relationships with other powers create distinctive conditions. Nor should a case study assume that the interests of the people of Kashmir are fully captured by the security calculations of either national government.
