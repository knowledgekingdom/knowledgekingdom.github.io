Suppose an archive shows that officials considered a confrontation and decided against it after discussing the prospect of nuclear escalation. This is meaningful evidence that perceived nuclear danger affected the decision. It is not necessarily evidence that the weapons were the sole reason for restraint. The officials may also have considered economic costs, diplomatic isolation, conventional military consequences and domestic opposition. Their discussion provides a window into the decision, not a complete experiment with all other conditions held constant.

Cross-national studies face another challenge. Countries do not acquire nuclear weapons at random. Governments with intense rivalries, major resources or distinctive security concerns are more likely to seek them than many peaceful countries. Comparing conflicts among nuclear states with conflicts among non-nuclear states can therefore confuse the effects of possession with the conditions that led to possession. This is the selection problem we encountered in Chapter 6, applied to an unusually consequential subject.

A strong research design uses several kinds of evidence: historical documents, comparisons across cases, patterns over time and plausible rival explanations. It states the outcome being investigated and the scope of any conclusion. Evidence that nuclear fear contributed to restraint in a particular crisis does not establish that the overall global effect of nuclear weapons has been beneficial. Conversely, evidence of serious risks does not prove that deterrence has never influenced a decision.
