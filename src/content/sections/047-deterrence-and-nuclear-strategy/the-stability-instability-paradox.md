Some scholars have argued that fear of catastrophic escalation at the highest level may coexist with more frequent competition below that level. This is called the stability–instability paradox. It is a hypothesis about the relationship between different forms of conflict, not a universal rule that nuclear weapons make every smaller war more likely. The historical evidence is disputed and affected by selection problems: states that acquire nuclear weapons often already have intense security rivalries.

A simplified example helps identify the logic without making a policy recommendation. Imagine two rival countries that are unwilling to fight an all-out war but continue to disagree about economic sanctions, border administration and regional alliances. Their aversion to one kind of conflict does not resolve the others. Both can therefore experience a relationship that is relatively restrained in one dimension and highly unstable in another. The possibility that behavior in the smaller disputes may affect the larger relationship is precisely what makes the paradox worrying.

South Asia has frequently appeared in discussions of this hypothesis, because India and Pakistan have experienced recurring crises despite their nuclear status. Yet many variables matter there: the history of partition, disputes over Kashmir, domestic politics, conventional military institutions and third-party diplomacy. A case study should not treat nuclear possession as the sole independent variable. Nor should the theoretical observation be interpreted as a permission to exploit perceived restraint. The lesson for public understanding is that apparent protection against one category of danger does not eliminate the need for peaceful dispute resolution at every other level. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]\[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]
