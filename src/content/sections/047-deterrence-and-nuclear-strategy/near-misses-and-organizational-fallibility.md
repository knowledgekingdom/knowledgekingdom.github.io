Aviation investigators study incidents that did not lead to disasters because an uneventful ending can conceal serious weaknesses. Nuclear historians and organizational scholars have applied a similar insight to the history of international security. The survival of a complex system does not establish that all of its safeguards worked as intended. It may show that a person exercised judgment, that an erroneous assumption was corrected or that several independent factors prevented a disastrous outcome.

Publicly documented episodes of Cold War false alarms and misperception include incidents in the early 1980s. The exact significance of individual episodes is sometimes debated, partly because archival access is uneven and memoirs written afterward may simplify the situation. Popular stories tend to credit a single heroic individual with saving the world. That emphasis can obscure the institutions, communication channels, uncertainties and decisions that surrounded the event. The lesson is not that one can reliably reconstruct an unknowable alternative timeline.

Scott Sagan's work on organizational safety challenges confidence in the ability of large institutions to eliminate catastrophic risk. Bureaucracies can develop routines that are effective in ordinary circumstances while performing poorly under unusual conditions. Institutional memory, independent review, civilian accountability and the ability to question information matter because perfect foresight is unavailable. The absence of a nuclear disaster is an important fact, but the record of errors and crises also belongs in the evidence. A system should be judged by its resilience and the quality of its oversight, not only by its previous survival. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]\[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]
