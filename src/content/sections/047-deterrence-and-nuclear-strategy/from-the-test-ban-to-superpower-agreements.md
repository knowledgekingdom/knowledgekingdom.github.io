The Limited Test Ban Treaty of 1963 prohibited nuclear test explosions in the atmosphere, outer space and under water for its parties. It did not establish a comprehensive prohibition on all nuclear tests. The agreement marked an important instance of cooperation between major powers in the aftermath of a dangerous crisis, and it responded to wider public concern about radioactive fallout. It also revealed the importance of verification disputes and the difficulty of reaching agreement on more comprehensive restrictions.

During the 1970s, the United States and Soviet Union negotiated the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks, or SALT. The 1972 agreements included the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty and an interim agreement regulating certain strategic arms. The subsequent SALT II agreement was signed in 1979 but did not enter into force. Later agreements included the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty of 1987 and START I, signed in 1991. These were products of specific historical negotiations rather than inevitable consequences of technological progress.

The history is not a straight line of improvement. Agreements can be concluded, implemented, disputed, superseded or terminated. The ABM Treaty ended in 2002 following US withdrawal, and the INF Treaty ended in 2019 amid accusations of noncompliance and withdrawal. Political relationships and domestic decisions can undo arrangements that once appeared durable. An important lesson is that an international agreement is not a permanent object like a mountain.
