Maps of the nuclear age frequently color countries according to whether they possess nuclear weapons. Such maps provide useful information but can obscure the distribution of risk. A state may be affected by the nuclear policies of its allies or neighbors. People may experience the consequences of former testing programs long after the military activity has ended. A region with no nuclear-armed states may still have a strong interest in the diplomatic conduct of major powers.

The political geography of the Cold War further complicates a simple national map. Alliances, former colonies, military dependencies and international agreements connected geographically distant societies. Communities living in remote regions were sometimes treated by governments as peripheral to the priorities of metropolitan populations. In discussions about the global nuclear order, the places least visible to the decision-makers could experience significant consequences. Geography here is not simply a matter of distance; it is also a matter of unequal political influence.

The concept of a global externality from economics helps explain this relationship. An externality is an effect of an activity on people who are not fully represented in the decision or transaction that produced it. Nuclear danger can create externalities on an international scale, including environmental consequences and risks to global food systems. Even when a government regards its security policy as a sovereign matter, the costs of catastrophic failure need not respect sovereignty.
