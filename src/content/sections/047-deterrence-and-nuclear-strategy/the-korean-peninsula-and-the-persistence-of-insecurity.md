The political division of Korea after the Second World War and the Korean War of 1950–1953 left a peninsula divided between two states with sharply different political systems. The armistice of 1953 ended major hostilities but did not produce a comprehensive peace treaty. This historical background is indispensable to understanding subsequent security concerns, including North Korea's nuclear program and South Korea's alliance with the United States.

North Korea joined the NPT in 1985, announced its withdrawal in 2003 and conducted its first announced nuclear test in 2006. International attempts to address its program have included negotiations, sanctions, agreements and periods of renewed confrontation. Different governments have offered competing explanations for the program, including regime security, international bargaining and domestic political legitimacy. Those explanations can overlap; describing a government's claimed motives does not establish that every action it takes is justified.

A frequent problem in public discussions is to treat the security of a ruling government and the welfare of its population as identical. The interests may differ substantially. Sanctions also raise complex questions about their intended political effects and their consequences for ordinary people. The Korean case reminds us that a durable settlement must be considered in a broader political environment, including historical grievances, humanitarian concerns, alliance relationships and the prospect of credible commitments.
