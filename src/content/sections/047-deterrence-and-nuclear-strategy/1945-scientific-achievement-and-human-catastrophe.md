The nuclear age emerged out of the Second World War. Research into atomic physics, fears of rival wartime programs and the concentration of scientific, industrial and government resources culminated in the United States' Manhattan Project. The undertaking was an extraordinary example of state-led scientific organization, but the measure of its historical significance cannot be restricted to the achievement of its designers. In August 1945 the United States used nuclear weapons against Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The two cities became enduring symbols of the consequences of nuclear warfare.

Japan's surrender followed in the same month. Historians debate the relative weight of the bombings, Soviet entry into the war against Japan, prior military defeat, blockade, internal Japanese politics and concerns about the future of the imperial institution. One interpretation stresses the American leadership's desire to end the war quickly and avoid a costly invasion; others emphasize the changing international balance or argue that different diplomatic options were available. The archive is indispensable precisely because the explanations are not interchangeable. Knowing the outcome does not prove that the actors had only one possible decision.

Hiroshima and Nagasaki also remind us that a political event has several histories. A diplomatic historian may study cabinet discussions and surrender documents; a doctor may study injury and illness; a survivor may remember missing family members and the difficulty of returning to ordinary life. All of these perspectives reveal different dimensions of the same event. Human experience is evidence, not decorative background to strategic theory. Our understanding of deterrence must begin with the consequences of its failure rather than with the apparent elegance of its logic.
