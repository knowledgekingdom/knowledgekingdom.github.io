New START, a bilateral treaty signed in 2010 between the United States and Russia, entered into force in 2011. It established limitations on categories of strategic nuclear arms and a framework for verification and information exchange. The parties extended it in 2021 for the five years allowed by the treaty. In the years that followed, deteriorating relations and disagreements disrupted important aspects of its implementation. The treaty expired on 5 February 2026. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-41" class="cite">41</a>\]\[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-44" class="cite">44</a>\]

The significance of an agreement like New START is not limited to its numerical provisions. A predictable reporting relationship can make it easier for governments and outside analysts to understand changes in military policy and distinguish them from speculation. When institutional transparency deteriorates, governments may find it harder to judge developments accurately. That uncertainty does not necessarily produce a particular response, but it reduces the amount of shared information available for political decision-making.

It is necessary to distinguish a treaty's expiry from what the parties actually do after expiry. The end of a legal obligation does not prove that either country immediately changed every practice previously governed by it. Nor does an informal political statement necessarily recreate the legal or institutional features of a former agreement. When reading contemporary reporting, check the date of the claim and distinguish binding legal instruments from voluntary commitments, proposals and statements of intention. The 2026 expiry is an established historical fact; subsequent arrangements require separate, dated verification. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-40" class="cite">40</a>\]\[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-44" class="cite">44</a>\]
