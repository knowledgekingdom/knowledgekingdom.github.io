Suppose a government wants a neighboring state to respect an existing border. It also wants that state to release prisoners and to sign a new trade agreement. These are three different objectives. Discouraging a future border violation concerns deterrence; demanding an action or a reversal of existing behavior concerns compellence; seeking a mutually beneficial arrangement may involve ordinary negotiation. In practice the lines can blur, because actors disagree about whether a particular action maintains or alters the status quo.

The distinction matters for historical interpretation. An arsenal might be associated with an absence of direct attack on the country possessing it, while proving largely irrelevant to a dispute about fishing rights or a contested election. Having tremendous destructive power is not equivalent to having unlimited political influence. Other governments may resist demands that seem disproportionate to the issue at stake, seek support from third countries, appeal to law or simply refuse to cooperate. A state's ability to threaten harm cannot automatically make a trade arrangement productive or an occupation legitimate.

Robert Pape's scholarship on coercion and the wider literature on bargaining invite us to separate a political objective from the instrument used to pursue it. A student should therefore avoid drawing a universal lesson from an isolated success. If a threat is followed by a concession, it does not follow that the threat caused it, that it was lawful, or that repeating similar threats would achieve comparable results. Compellence can also make an opponent more determined to resist.
