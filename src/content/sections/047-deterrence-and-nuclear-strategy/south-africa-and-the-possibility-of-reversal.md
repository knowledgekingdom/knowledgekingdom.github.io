South Africa offers a historically important challenge to the assumption that acquisition is irreversible. During apartheid, the South African government developed a nuclear weapons program. It later dismantled its weapons before joining the NPT as a non-nuclear-weapon state in 1991. In 1993 President F. W. de Klerk publicly disclosed the former program, after which international verification addressed the declared history and materials associated with it.

Several changes formed the political background to the decision: the end of the Cold War, a changing regional security environment and South Africa's transition away from apartheid. Historians debate the relative weight of these considerations and the motives of particular decision-makers. It would be misleading to claim that one straightforward formula explains the reversal. The case is valuable because it demonstrates that policies which governments once regarded as essential can become reconsidered as institutions, political priorities and external conditions change.

There is a distinction between the physical dismantlement of weapons and the political decision to renounce a weapons program. Verification can help establish compliance with commitments, while domestic law and international relationships can support their durability. Scientific knowledge cannot simply be erased from society. The central issue for non-proliferation is the maintenance of credible peaceful obligations, not the disappearance of all scientific competence. South Africa is not a blueprint that every country can mechanically follow, but its history establishes that nuclear possession is a political condition rather than an inevitable and permanent identity. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-35" class="cite">35</a>\]\[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-36" class="cite">36</a>\]
