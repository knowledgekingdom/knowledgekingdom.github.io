The Cold War was not one confrontation with a single cause. It involved the rival political and economic orders led by the United States and Soviet Union, competition over the future of Europe, decolonization, technological rivalry and conflicts in Asia, Africa, Latin America and the Middle East. The nuclear dimension was embedded in all of these relationships. It cannot explain them in isolation.

Many accounts describe the Cold War as bipolar because two states possessed exceptional global military, economic and diplomatic influence. But the world contained numerous independent political actors. China developed its own course; India and other states pursued non-alignment; European allies disagreed with their patrons; newly independent states demanded that the international order acknowledge their interests. Bipolarity is a useful description of a broad distribution of power, not a complete map of international politics.

There was no direct full-scale war between the two superpowers, but the era was violent. Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan and many other conflicts show why the phrase 'long peace' can mislead if applied globally. For a family living through a war on the periphery, the absence of a direct Soviet-American war was no guarantee of safety. Nuclear deterrence might have influenced the avoidance of one particular kind of confrontation while failing to prevent others. A historically responsible assessment must specify which peace is being described, whose security is being measured and which kinds of violence are excluded from the analysis. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]\[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]
