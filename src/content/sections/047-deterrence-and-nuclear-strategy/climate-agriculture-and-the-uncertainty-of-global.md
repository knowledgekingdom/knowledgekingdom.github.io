The consequences of nuclear conflict could reach far beyond the countries directly involved. Researchers have investigated how smoke from extensive fires could affect the atmosphere, alter climate conditions and disrupt agriculture. This field is often discussed under the term nuclear winter, although published research considers a variety of scenarios rather than one inevitable outcome. The models depend on assumptions about the event, atmospheric processes, environmental responses and the adaptation of human societies.

A 2022 study by Lili Xia and colleagues in Nature Food examined possible effects on crops, marine fisheries and livestock production under different modeled conditions. Its central significance for this chapter is the connection between military decisions and food security across national borders. Even countries with no involvement in a conflict might experience serious consequences through changes in growing conditions, trade and the availability of food. A model's numerical outputs are conditional estimates, not direct observations of a future event. Different assumptions produce different results.

The uncertainty is not a reason to ignore the problem. A responsible decision about a potentially catastrophic hazard must consider both the evidence available and what remains unknown. Nor is it necessary to adopt the most extreme estimate as certain in order to recognize that the risks matter. The climate dimension of nuclear conflict illustrates the importance of combining environmental science, economics and international relations. A nation may be able to define its own defence policy, but it cannot compel the atmosphere, ocean or global agricultural system to remain inside national borders. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-53" class="cite">53</a>\]
