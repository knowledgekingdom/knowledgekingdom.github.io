Imagine three countries attending an international summit. Alder is a nuclear-armed state that describes its existing policy as essential to its security. Brin is a rival nuclear-armed state with a different interpretation of their shared history. Cedar is a non-nuclear country whose economy and food imports make it especially concerned about the consequences of major international disruption. None of the three governments agrees on the legitimacy of every past political decision made by the others.

Alder and Brin want to avoid catastrophic conflict but disagree over their political relationship and military commitments. Cedar argues that the two countries' policies expose people outside their territories to unacceptable risks. At the summit, the governments find that they can discuss improved diplomatic communication, greater transparency and assistance to affected communities without first reaching agreement on every territorial or ideological dispute. Cedar continues to advocate complete disarmament; Alder and Brin do not accept that goal on the same timetable. Some cooperation is possible, but it does not resolve the deeper disagreement.

The exercise is deliberately fictional and should not be treated as a model for managing a real military confrontation. Its purpose is to identify distinct forms of political judgment. What is the legal status of an agreed statement? Which people bear the risks of the existing relationship? What evidence would establish that a new institution improved transparency? Which disagreements remain after the parties reach a partial agreement? A thoughtful answer recognizes that cooperation on a shared danger does not require the participants to agree on the entire history or morality of their relationship.
