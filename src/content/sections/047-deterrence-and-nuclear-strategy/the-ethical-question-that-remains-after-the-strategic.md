The debate over nuclear deterrence often begins with an empirical proposition: whether the possession of nuclear weapons reduces the probability of certain wars. It cannot end there, because even a convincing empirical conclusion would leave important ethical questions unresolved. Who has authority to expose future generations to a danger they cannot consent to? How should the interests of neutral populations be represented? Can a policy be regarded as just because it reduces one category of risk while creating another?

A consequentialist argument evaluates policies partly by their expected effects. A rights-based argument may emphasize prohibitions against deliberately threatening or harming innocent people, independently of calculations about aggregate benefit. Other approaches place special weight on duties to future generations, the responsibilities of public institutions and the distribution of risks among societies. These perspectives need not lead every thoughtful person to the same policy conclusion, but they make clear why an argument about deterrence is not simply a technical disagreement.

The humane response to uncertainty is not to pretend that history has settled the question. Nuclear weapons have influenced international politics profoundly, and their absence from warfare since 1945 is a fact of enormous importance. What explains that fact, whether the same conditions will continue, and which arrangements would provide a more just and durable peace remain contested. The strongest conclusion we can draw is that national security, diplomacy, international law, scientific evidence and humanitarian responsibility cannot be considered in isolation.
