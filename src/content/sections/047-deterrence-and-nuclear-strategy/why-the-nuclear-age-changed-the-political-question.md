Conventional warfare had already shown an immense capacity for destruction before 1945. Industrialization, imperial rivalry and the world wars had made whole societies vulnerable. Nuclear weapons introduced a different order of concern: the possibility that a decision about military force could produce catastrophic harm over a very short period, with consequences extending long beyond the initial event. The political significance of this change is more important for our purposes than the engineering of the weapons themselves.

Nuclear strategy is the body of political and military thinking about the existence, role and implications of nuclear weapons. It includes claims about security, alliances, escalation, legal obligations, arms control and disarmament. It is not simply a classification of weapons. Its foundational problem is that an instrument defended as a means of preventing war is also capable of producing devastation that would defeat ordinary understandings of national protection.

A paradox follows. If an instrument is so destructive that political leaders regard its actual use as unacceptable, why does its possession matter to the decisions of other leaders? If they believe the danger is real, how can governments ensure that the danger never becomes reality? Different traditions of strategic thought offer competing answers, but none removes the dependence on fallible institutions and human judgment. The study of nuclear strategy is consequently also a study of diplomacy, ethics, law, psychology, organizational sociology and the history of science.
