Escalation is an increase in the intensity or scope of a conflict. It can involve new participants, a widening geographical area, more severe political demands or increasingly destructive violence. We sometimes imagine escalation as a staircase climbed voluntarily by a single actor, but that image leaves out the interaction among governments. One country's action can be interpreted by another in unexpected ways, and an incident that neither government originally sought can acquire political significance as each responds to the other's interpretation.

This is especially important when reading newspaper coverage of crises. A declaration intended for a domestic audience may be treated abroad as a statement of future policy. A statement that one government believes demonstrates firmness may be understood by another as evidence that negotiations are hopeless. Language acquires meaning in a particular historical relationship, and that relationship can change faster than diplomatic institutions adapt. A prediction based entirely on what one government says it intends to do is therefore incomplete.

De-escalation is not necessarily surrender. Governments can maintain their disagreements while reducing the danger of misunderstanding, protecting communication, accepting mediation or returning to ordinary diplomatic processes. In many disputes the immediate reduction of danger creates room for a later discussion of the underlying issue. This distinction helps us see why crisis diplomacy matters independently of the distribution of military power.
