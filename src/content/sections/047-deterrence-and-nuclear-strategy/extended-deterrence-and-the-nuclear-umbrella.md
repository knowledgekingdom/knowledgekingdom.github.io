A state may claim that its military capabilities contribute not only to its own security but also to the security of allies. The nuclear dimension of that assurance is often called extended deterrence or a nuclear umbrella. The expression is metaphorical. It does not describe physical protection from the effects of nuclear weapons, and it does not make an ally immune from political or military pressure. It refers to a set of expectations about a relationship among governments.

Extended deterrence introduces an additional question: what does an ally believe the protecting state would do in a crisis? The issue has troubled alliance politics throughout the nuclear era. The interests of a major power and a smaller partner are not identical, even if they agree that cooperation is valuable. Smaller allies may fear abandonment; larger states may fear being drawn into disputes that they did not choose. These concerns, introduced in Chapter 45, do not disappear when an alliance is formalized.

Alliances also create channels for consultation, political coordination and diplomacy. Their commitments can influence a member's decision not to pursue an independent nuclear arsenal, although no single mechanism explains every state's choices. It is important to distinguish a treaty obligation from a political assurance and from a prediction of future behavior. A country's public statement of solidarity is evidence of its position at a particular time. It is not a mathematical guarantee of what every future leader will decide. Examining the written agreement, the institutional relationship and the historical record is more useful than treating the word 'umbrella' as an answer to every question. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]\[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]
