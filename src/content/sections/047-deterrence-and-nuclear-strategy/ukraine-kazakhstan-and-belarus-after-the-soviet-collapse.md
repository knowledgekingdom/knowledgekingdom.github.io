When the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991, nuclear weapons formerly associated with Soviet military institutions remained on the territory of several newly independent states, including Ukraine, Kazakhstan and Belarus. Their situation illustrates why territorial location, legal ownership, political control and technical authority should not be treated as identical. Describing the weapons simply as national arsenals equivalent to those of established nuclear powers can obscure the complex institutional inheritance of the Soviet system.

The three countries ultimately joined the NPT as non-nuclear-weapon states, and the weapons on their territories were transferred to Russia. In Ukraine's case, the Budapest Memorandum of December 1994 recorded security assurances by Russia, the United States and United Kingdom in connection with Ukraine's accession to the treaty. Those assurances included respect for independence, sovereignty and existing borders, and commitments concerning the threat or use of force. They were not equivalent to NATO's collective-defence treaty obligations.

Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 and full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 violated Ukraine's sovereignty and profoundly damaged the credibility of those assurances. The lesson for non-proliferation is contested. Some see the events as evidence that relying on assurances is insufficient; others emphasize the practical and diplomatic difficulties that surrounded the original situation and the danger of treating an unobservable counterfactual as certainty. We cannot know with confidence how an alternative history would have developed. We can conclude that respect for international commitments matters beyond the immediate case because other countries observe how promises are honored or violated. \[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-37" class="cite">37</a>\]\[<a href="/047-deterrence-and-nuclear-strategy/sources/#source-38" class="cite">38</a>\]
