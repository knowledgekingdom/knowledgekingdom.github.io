A telephone connecting two governments is not itself a peace agreement. Its importance lies in allowing communication that might otherwise be delayed, distorted or interrupted. During a confrontation, official messages may be accompanied by public commentary, rumors and assumptions about what the other government knows. Reliable diplomatic contact can make it easier to clarify misunderstandings and distinguish a genuine disagreement from a mistaken report.

The 1963 communications agreement between the United States and Soviet Union followed the experience of the Cuban crisis. It represented a recognition that adversaries had a shared interest in reducing the danger of miscommunication. This is a striking departure from the view that international relations are entirely zero-sum. Two competitors can regard one another as dangerous and nevertheless cooperate on an institution that protects both from an outcome neither wants. The existence of such an institution does not prove that the relationship has become friendly.

Institutional cooperation can extend beyond immediate crisis management. Recurring diplomatic meetings, published agreements, consultation with allies, parliamentary debate and independent scientific research all create opportunities to identify misunderstandings. They can also fail. An institution that exists only on paper, lacks authority or loses the trust of participants may contribute little to security. The central concern is not the number of diplomatic meetings but the reliability of the relationship they sustain. International institutions are a form of political infrastructure, much as transport and communication networks were physical infrastructure in Chapter 43.
