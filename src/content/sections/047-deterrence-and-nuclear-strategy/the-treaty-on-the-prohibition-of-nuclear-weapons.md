In July 2017, a United Nations conference adopted the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, which entered into force on 22 January 2021. Its emergence followed sustained advocacy by non-nuclear states, humanitarian organizations, civil society and survivors of nuclear use and testing. It represents an approach to nuclear security in which the unacceptable consequences of weapons are the basis for prohibition, rather than a reason to continue relying on them as a deterrent.

For its parties, the treaty prohibits a range of nuclear-weapon-related activities, including possession, use and threat of use, as specified in its text. It also includes obligations concerning assistance to affected people and environmental remediation in relevant circumstances. This latter dimension is especially important: the nuclear question is not only about preventing future harm but about responding to the legacy of past harm. An agreement with humanitarian provisions may serve purposes that are overlooked in a purely military account of arms control.

The treaty does not bind non-parties merely because it has entered into force. Nuclear-armed states and many states relying on extended deterrence have opposed it or remained outside it, arguing among other things that abolition must take account of their security relationships. Proponents argue that continued reliance itself perpetuates unacceptable risk and inequality. The debate is therefore both legal and political. The reader should distinguish a prohibition binding treaty parties, broader customary-law arguments and political commitments to eventual disarmament.
