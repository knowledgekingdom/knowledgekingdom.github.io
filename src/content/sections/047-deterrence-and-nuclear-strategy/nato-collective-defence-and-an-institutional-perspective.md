The North Atlantic Treaty Organization was established in 1949 and subsequently developed a nuclear dimension to its deterrence and defence policy. Its members describe the alliance as a defensive organization; critics debate the effects of its policies on security perceptions, nuclear risk and disarmament. The organization's stated policy should be read as an official position, not as neutral proof that every claimed effect occurs.

Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty treats an armed attack on one or more members within the treaty's geographical scope as an attack against them all. Each ally assists through action it considers necessary, including possible armed force. This wording is important: the treaty does not specify an automatic nuclear response to every attack. Its obligations concern collective defence, while particular responses remain political and legal decisions. NATO's Nuclear Planning Group, established in 1966, is an institutional forum concerned with alliance nuclear policy; participation in such discussions is distinct from national possession of nuclear weapons.

The alliance argues that its arrangements are compatible with the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. Critics have contested aspects of these arrangements and their implications for the broader disarmament regime. A reader should distinguish the alliance's legal interpretation, the arguments of its critics and the text of the relevant treaties. The disagreement illustrates a broader problem of international institutions: cooperation within one group can have consequences beyond that group's membership.
