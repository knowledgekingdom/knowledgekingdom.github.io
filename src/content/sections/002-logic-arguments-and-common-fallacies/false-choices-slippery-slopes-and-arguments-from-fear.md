A <strong>false dilemma</strong> presents two possibilities as though they exhaust the options. “Either we fully privatize the service or the country will have no efficient healthcare” ignores combinations of public funding, private delivery, regulation and mixed systems. “Either we invest nothing in defense or we must spend whatever the military requests” likewise hides a range of budgets and strategies. To test a proposed dilemma, ask what alternatives exist and whether those alternatives are actually feasible.

Not every dilemma is false. Some choices really are mutually exclusive under a stated budget, law or deadline. If a small school has funding for only one bus this year, it may have to choose between two proposals. The question is whether the speaker has established the restriction rather than merely asserted it. The presence of trade-offs does not mean the available choices are limitless.

A <strong>slippery-slope argument</strong> claims that an initial action will lead through a chain to a disastrous result. “If we permit one temporary exception, soon no rule will be enforced.” That could be an empty scare tactic; it could also be a real institutional concern if a plausible precedent and incentive mechanism are shown. The key is to inspect the steps. Why would the first exception make the second likely? What safeguards exist? What evidence from comparable institutions supports the forecast?

An <strong>appeal to fear</strong> can distort reasoning by making a possible danger feel certain or by demanding an extreme response without proportional analysis. Still, fear itself does not make an argument invalid. Serious risks deserve attention. A responsible argument specifies the probability, magnitude, evidence and alternative responses. “This is frightening” is not equivalent to “this policy is justified.” Neither is “this frightens you” equivalent to “the danger is imaginary.”
