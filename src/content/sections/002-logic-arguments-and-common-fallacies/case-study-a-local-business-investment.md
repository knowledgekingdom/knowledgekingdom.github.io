A shop owner is considering installing rooftop solar panels. A salesperson says, “Your electricity bill is €400 per month, so a €6,000 solar system will pay for itself in fifteen months.” The arithmetic—€6,000 divided by €400—works only if the panels eliminate the entire bill immediately, produce at the same hours as consumption, cost nothing to maintain, and involve no financing expenses or unavoidable grid charges. The calculation may be valid under imaginary premises but unsound as an investment forecast.

A better argument starts with actual energy consumption by hour and season, the proposed system's generation estimates, tariffs, self-consumption, export compensation if available, maintenance, installation cost, taxes, loan terms and equipment lifetime. If the verified annual net savings were €1,200, the simple payback would be five years before more detailed adjustments. That hypothetical calculation is illustrative; it is not a price quote or a profitability forecast for a particular property.

Notice the three layers. The salesperson's conclusion is an investment recommendation, the savings estimate is a forecast, and the comparison of costs and benefits depends on the owner's financial goals and tolerance for risk. A good critic need not say “solar is a scam.” A good advocate need not promise unrealistic returns. Both can ask what assumptions must be true and how the decision changes when those assumptions vary. This is logic in the service of a real choice.
