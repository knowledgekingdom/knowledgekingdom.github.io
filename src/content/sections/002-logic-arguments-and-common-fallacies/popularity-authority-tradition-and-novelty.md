Millions of people can believe something false. Therefore, the statement “Everyone believes it” is not conclusive proof. This is the core problem in an <strong>appeal to popularity</strong>. A rumor repeated across thousands of accounts is not transformed into independent evidence by repetition. But popularity can matter for questions about shared customs, cultural meaning or what consumers actually prefer. The error is treating a measure of belief or preference as a proof of unrelated facts.

<strong>Appeals to authority</strong> need equal care. Imagine that a world-renowned physicist confidently comments on an obscure medieval manuscript. Their physics credentials do not automatically establish expertise in medieval paleography. By contrast, a specialist who has studied the manuscript's script, material, provenance and comparable documents may provide excellent testimony. The question is whether the source has relevant expertise, uses appropriate methods, and represents the evidence accurately.

Consensus among qualified researchers can deserve substantial weight because it often reflects work evaluated by multiple specialists across institutions and methods. Yet consensus is not infallibility, and its strength depends on the field and question. In Chapter 1, we learned to ask how knowledge is produced. Here the logical lesson is narrower: citing expertise is not an error in itself. Treating a name or title as a substitute for examining an argument is.

<strong>Appeal to tradition</strong> says that a practice must be right because it is old. Tradition can preserve valuable experience and meaningful commitments, but age alone does not establish effectiveness or justice. <strong>Appeal to novelty</strong> makes the opposite mistake: assuming something is better because it is new. A new teaching app may be excellent, ineffective or worse than established practice; its date of release tells us little about its learning outcomes. Ask for the mechanism and the comparison.

An <strong>appeal to nature</strong> claims that something is good because it is natural or bad because it is artificial. Some natural substances are dangerous; some manufactured technologies are lifesaving. That observation does not mean every natural practice is safe or every technology benign. It means that the relevant standards—harms, benefits, sustainability and values—must be argued for directly.
