A <strong>fallacy</strong> is a recognizable problem in reasoning. Some fallacies arise from an invalid logical structure; others arise from irrelevant evidence, unjustified assumptions, manipulation or insufficient support. Learning the names can help you notice patterns, but the aim is not to collect labels. Someone who shouts “straw man!” whenever criticized may be committing the very conversational error this chapter is trying to prevent: substituting a theatrical victory for an examination of reasons.

The first question when you suspect a fallacy is therefore not, “What is its name?” but, “Where does the argument fail?” Is a premise unsupported? Is a fact irrelevant to the conclusion? Does the conclusion overreach? Has a genuine opponent's position been replaced with a different one? Can we describe a possible counterexample? Once you can explain the problem in ordinary language, the label is optional.

Scholars of informal logic commonly evaluate everyday arguments by asking whether the premises are <strong>acceptable</strong>, <strong>relevant</strong>, and <strong>sufficient</strong>. Acceptability concerns whether we have reason to believe the premises. Relevance asks whether those reasons actually bear on the conclusion. Sufficiency asks whether the degree of support warrants the conclusion's strength. A real statistic can be acceptable and relevant, yet insufficient to prove a broad political claim. A terrible argument might contain an accurate fact that is simply beside the point. These tests will be more useful across your life than memorizing fifty named errors.

One caution deserves its own paragraph. An argument is not necessarily fallacious because someone uses emotion, quotes a specialist, tells a story or criticizes a public figure's integrity. Emotions can reveal what people care about. Experts can supply genuine evidence. A conflict of interest can be relevant to reliability. The error arises when the move is used in a way that does not support the claimed conclusion, or distracts from the better evidence. Context and purpose matter.
