People frequently confuse a condition that is <em>necessary</em> for something with one that is <em>sufficient</em> to guarantee it. A passport is normally necessary for a citizen to make many international journeys, but possessing a passport alone is not sufficient to cross every border: visas, entry rules and other conditions may matter. Conversely, winning a chess game by checkmate is sufficient to win that game, but it is not necessary: an opponent can resign.

The distinction can be summarized as follows. If <strong>A is sufficient for B</strong>, then whenever A occurs, B follows. If <strong>A is necessary for B</strong>, then B cannot occur without A. The direction matters. If being a square is sufficient for being a rectangle, then all squares are rectangles. But being a rectangle is not sufficient for being a square. Being a rectangle is necessary for being a square under the standard geometric definitions.

In public debates, confusion about these conditions can produce spectacularly confident arguments. “Successful cities have universities, therefore building a university will make our city successful.” Universities may be one contributing factor, but the presence of a university does not guarantee good transport, stable institutions, jobs or investment. Even if some characteristic is present in every successful case, it may be necessary without being sufficient; it might also be neither, if it merely happens to correlate with success in the cases inspected.

It also helps to distinguish <strong>causes</strong> from <strong>signs</strong>. Smoke can be evidence that a fire is burning; it is not ordinarily the cause of the fire. An excellent school may show high examination scores because it teaches well, attracts well-prepared students, receives strong family support, or combines these influences. A sign may be useful for prediction without identifying the mechanism producing the result.

Words such as <em>always</em>, <em>only</em>, <em>must</em>, <em>may</em> and <em>usually</em> alter logical force. “All voters in a district supported the proposal” differs profoundly from “Most voters supported it.” “This policy may reduce traffic” is much weaker than “This policy will reduce traffic.” Before declaring agreement or disagreement, identify the exact strength of the claim. Many arguments are lost or won by silently changing one of these little words.
