In <strong>circular reasoning</strong>, a conclusion is supported by premises that already assume that same conclusion. “This newspaper is trustworthy because it always tells the truth, and we know it tells the truth because it is trustworthy.” The speaker has provided no independent basis for the judgment. Sometimes the circle is hidden across several statements, making it harder to see. Ask where the chain of reasons connects to something we can evaluate without already accepting the disputed conclusion.

<strong>Begging the question</strong> is often used in philosophy to describe reasoning that assumes the disputed point; its technical use differs from the everyday meaning “raises the question.” An argument can beg the question even when its premises and conclusion are not identical sentences. For example, “The proposed regulation is an unjust restriction on freedom, so we must reject it” has already packed the contested moral evaluation into the premise.

<strong>Moving the goalposts</strong> occurs when someone demands new evidence after an agreed standard has been met, without a good reason for the change. Imagine that a participant says they will accept official records confirming a project was funded. The records are produced. They then insist that only private letters from every official would count. There may be legitimate reasons to reassess evidence standards when flaws emerge, but endlessly shifting them makes a claim impossible to test.

A related idea is the <strong>burden of proof</strong>. A person asserting that an extraordinary event occurred should provide reasons proportionate to the claim and context. This does not mean that everyone who doubts an assertion must prove its opposite. If an acquaintance claims that a particular policy doubled crime, you can reasonably request the source, measurement period and baseline without having to prove that crime was unchanged. But your own positive claim—“the policy actually halved crime”—also creates an evidential responsibility.

Beware of the <strong>argument from ignorance</strong>: “Nobody has proved X false, therefore X is true,” or “Nobody has proved X true, therefore X is false.” Absence of evidence can sometimes be evidence of absence when we would strongly expect evidence if a claim were true. An archaeologist's failure to find a particular kind of artifact may matter if careful excavations at a well-preserved site should have revealed many examples. But not finding something in a poorly surveyed area tells us much less. The quality and expected detectability of evidence change the inference.
