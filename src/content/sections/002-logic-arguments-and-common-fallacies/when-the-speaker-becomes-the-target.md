An <strong>ad hominem</strong> error rejects a claim because of an irrelevant attack on the person making it. “That economist is rude, therefore her forecast is wrong” does not test the forecast. “The historian has an annoying voice, therefore the archival quotation is fake” does not examine the archive. The speaker's personality may influence whether we enjoy the conversation, but it is not a substitute for checking the evidence.

Yet reputation and interests can sometimes matter. Suppose a consultant secretly receives a fee from a company whose product they recommend. That conflict may give us reason to scrutinize their testimony more carefully. It does not, by itself, prove the product defective. Likewise, a witness who previously fabricated relevant testimony may be less credible on an issue requiring trust in their account. <strong>Assessing reliability</strong> is different from using an insult to evade a claim.

The <strong>tu quoque</strong> (“you too”) maneuver is closely related. A public official urges citizens to save energy, and a critic responds that the official's residence uses excessive electricity. The criticism may expose hypocrisy worth examining, but it does not establish that saving energy is unnecessary or harmful. Whether a rule is sensible remains a distinct question from whether its advocate follows it.

The <strong>genetic fallacy</strong> judges a claim solely by its origin. A good idea does not become false merely because it was first proposed by a disliked group; a bad idea does not become true because a respected institution repeats it. Provenance is valuable when assessing credibility, as Chapter 1 showed, but it does not replace an examination of the claim's content and supporting evidence.
