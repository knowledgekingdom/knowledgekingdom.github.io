History provides a powerful lesson in reasoning. A textbook might say that an assassination “caused” the First World War. An assassination in Sarajevo in June 1914 was indeed the immediate crisis trigger, but a satisfying explanation also considers alliances, military planning, imperial competition, nationalist movements and leaders' decisions. Without those contexts, the same kind of violent act might have remained a regional event rather than becoming part of a world war.

The same logic applies to ordinary events. Imagine a bakery that loses money after flour prices rise. “Flour inflation caused the closure” may be partly true. But perhaps the shop already carried large debts, its ovens were inefficient, a competitor opened nearby, or the owner had no financial reserves. A triggering event and underlying vulnerability can both be causally important. The counterfactual question—what would likely have happened without that factor?—helps separate explanation from storytelling.

Think in layers: <strong>background conditions</strong>, <strong>trigger</strong>, <strong>mechanism</strong>, <strong>amplifiers</strong>, and <strong>decisions</strong>. Background conditions make an outcome possible or likely. A trigger starts a sequence. A mechanism explains the process. Amplifiers make consequences larger or faster. Decisions can interrupt or intensify the chain. These categories are flexible tools, not a universal scientific formula.

A useful historian asks not merely why an event happened, but why it happened <strong>then</strong>, <strong>there</strong>, and <strong>in that form</strong>. Why did an empire decline in one period rather than another? Why did similar economic shocks produce reform in one state and revolution in another? Why did some political movements attract mass support while others remained marginal? Each question forces us to compare circumstances, not merely find a memorable villain or hero.

In politics and economics, we must also distinguish what a policy is designed to do from what it actually achieves. A restriction may be designed to reduce danger and nevertheless create illegal markets. A subsidy may be intended to help poor households and unintentionally raise prices if supply cannot respond. Predicting behavior requires identifying incentives, constraints, institutions and how people adjust when rules change. The goal is not to assume that every policy fails; it is to investigate consequences rather than stopping at intentions.
