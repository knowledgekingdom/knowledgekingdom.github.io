The <strong>fallacy fallacy</strong> is the mistake of assuming that a conclusion must be false because a particular argument for it is poor. Imagine someone says, “Smoking must be harmful because I met an unhealthy smoker.” The anecdote is weak evidence for a population-level claim. But the conclusion may still be strongly supported by independent epidemiological and biological research. Criticizing the anecdote does not overturn that other evidence.

This error is common in politics. A person makes an exaggerated argument for a policy. Their opponent successfully exposes the exaggeration and then announces that the policy itself has been disproved. That final leap may be as unreasonable as the original one. A position can be right for bad reasons, wrong for good-seeming reasons, or partly right under conditions that were never discussed.

Another difficulty is that people often disagree over <strong>facts</strong>, <strong>causal interpretations</strong>, <strong>definitions</strong>, and <strong>values</strong> simultaneously. Suppose two residents argue about whether their town should host a major sporting event. One doubts the forecast number of visitors. Another accepts the visitor estimate but expects only limited local spending. A third thinks cultural prestige justifies a loss. These are different disputes. A fact-check cannot settle a value judgment; a passionate appeal to civic identity cannot repair a calculation.

To make progress, ask which premise each person would change if presented with strong contrary evidence. When a factual issue can be tested, define what would count. When the disagreement concerns values, state the priorities openly. If neither person can imagine any evidence that would affect their view, the exchange may be about loyalty or identity rather than investigation. That is useful to recognize before pouring more time into it.
