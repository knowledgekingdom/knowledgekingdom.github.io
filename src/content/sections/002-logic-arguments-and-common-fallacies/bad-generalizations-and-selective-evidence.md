A traveler has three unpleasant encounters in one city and concludes that everyone from that country is unfriendly. The problem is a <strong>hasty generalization</strong>. Even an honest observation about three encounters cannot support a conclusion about millions of people. The sample may be too small, unrepresentative or selected in ways that matter. A hundred encounters can still be misleading if they all occur at one crowded airport during a major disruption.

<strong>Cherry-picking</strong> selects favorable evidence while ignoring relevant counterevidence. Suppose an investor advertises five profitable trades but conceals fifteen losses. The displayed trades may be genuine. The conclusion “I am consistently successful” depends on the full record, not an attractive selection. Similarly, an argument about a government's economic record should not silently omit years or indicators that would materially change the comparison.

<strong>Survivorship bias</strong> focuses on the cases that remain visible and overlooks cases that disappeared. Famous entrepreneurs can offer valuable stories, but their habits do not, by themselves, tell us which practices distinguish success from failure. Many unsuccessful founders may have shown the same persistence, confidence or willingness to take risks. To investigate whether a trait predicts success, we must look beyond survivors to the wider comparison group.

There is also a difference between a <strong>counterexample</strong> and a complete statistical rebuttal. If someone says <em>every</em> successful artist has formal training, one clearly successful untrained artist refutes the universal claim. If someone says <em>most</em> successful artists receive training, one untrained celebrity does not disprove it. Universal and probabilistic claims demand different kinds of evidence. This is why precise wording matters so much.
