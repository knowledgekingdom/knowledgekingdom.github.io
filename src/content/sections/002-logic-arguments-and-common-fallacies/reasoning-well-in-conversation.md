An intelligent person is not someone who recognizes every fallacy and interrupts everybody. One of the least useful habits is to listen for a mistake while ignoring the thought being expressed. People often speak imprecisely, especially when they are emotional, tired or describing a personal experience. Your aim should be to identify the strongest relevant claim and help make it clearer.

Three ordinary sentences can transform a discussion. “<strong>What do you mean by that?</strong>” asks for a definition. “<strong>What makes you think so?</strong>” requests a reason. “<strong>What would change your mind?</strong>” explores whether the claim is open to testing. These questions may sound simple, but they move the conversation from personalities toward propositions. Ask them in a genuine tone, not as an interrogation technique.

Sometimes the best answer is a qualified one: “That seems plausible, but we have not separated correlation from causation.” Sometimes it is a firm one: “Those two premises cannot both be true under the definitions we are using.” Sometimes the correct response is to admit limits: “I do not know enough to judge this.” Intellectual humility is not passivity. You can demand better evidence while recognizing uncertainty; you can defend a moral conviction while admitting the practical consequences are not fully known.

When a conversation becomes heated, summarize the point of agreement before naming the disputed premise. “We both want young people to have opportunities. We disagree about whether this program will help enough to justify its cost.” That formulation does not erase legitimate conflict. It gives the disagreement a shape that can be investigated. The purpose of argument is to make claims more accountable to reasons, even when consensus is impossible.
