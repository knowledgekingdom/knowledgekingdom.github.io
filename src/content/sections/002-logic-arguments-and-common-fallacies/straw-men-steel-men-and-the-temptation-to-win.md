Imagine a discussion about whether a city should reduce the number of cars allowed in its historic center. One person says, “We should consider restricting through-traffic during peak hours and improving buses.” A critic replies, “So you want to ban everybody from driving and punish people who live outside the city.” The critic has moved the debate from a limited proposal to a much broader and harsher one. This is a <strong>straw man</strong>: attacking a distorted version of the position rather than the position actually advanced.

The charitable response is not to pretend the proposal has no downsides. It may indeed burden commuters, emergency access, deliveries or people with disabilities. A strong criticism could demand exemptions, reliable alternatives, evidence from comparable cities and cost estimates. Those objections are harder to dismiss precisely because they address the real proposal. Accuracy makes criticism stronger, not weaker.

A <strong>steel man</strong> is the converse discipline: reconstruct the most defensible version of an opposing argument. Suppose one person favors freer international trade because specialization can raise total output and lower some prices. Another worries about job losses in exposed industries. A serious steel man of the second position notes that even if society gains overall, the losses may be concentrated, painful and persistent for affected communities. A serious steel man of the first notes that protecting every existing industry can impose large costs on consumers and other producers. The positions still disagree; they now disagree about the real trade-offs.

Steelmanning can go wrong if you invent a stronger argument that the other person does not endorse and then claim to have represented them. The practical safeguard is simple: <strong>ask for confirmation</strong>. “Is this the strongest fair version of what you're saying?” If they correct your summary, revise it. You can be charitable without attributing invented views to your partner.

A useful exercise is to take a position you strongly oppose and write two paragraphs in defense of it before writing your criticism. You are not required to change your values. The goal is to prove that you understand what you are rejecting. If your reconstruction is so silly that no thoughtful person would defend it, you have probably not reached the strongest version yet.
