Some disagreements appear factual but are actually disagreements about definitions. One person says that a country is “rich” because its total economy is large. Another says it is “poor” because its average household has limited purchasing power. Both could be using genuine statistics, but different definitions of wealth: total output, output per person, median income, household assets or living standards.

Before debating a headline, classify what kind of statement is being made. A <strong>descriptive claim</strong> says what is or was the case. A <strong>causal claim</strong> says what produced an outcome. A <strong>forecast</strong> says what may happen. A <strong>definitional claim</strong> proposes the meaning or boundaries of a term. A <strong>normative claim</strong> says what ought to be done or valued. These categories interact, and a single sentence may mix several of them.

“Inflation rose to 6 percent, therefore the central bank must raise interest rates” contains a descriptive premise, an implied causal model about interest rates and inflation, a forecast of consequences, and a normative judgment about what policy should take priority. Even if the descriptive statistic is correct, the recommendation is not logically automatic. Possible costs include slower borrowing and employment growth; the decision also depends on expectations, credibility, the source of inflation, and institutional mandates.

A classic philosophical concern, often associated with David Hume, is the gap between statements about what <strong>is</strong> and statements about what <strong>ought</strong> to be. One cannot obtain a policy recommendation from descriptive premises alone without some further value, objective or normative premise. “The new bridge will cost €10 million” does not tell us whether it should be built. We need to know its benefits and alternatives, and how we value safety, access, environmental impacts and public funds.

That does not mean facts are optional in moral arguments. Accurate evidence can show whether a proposed means would actually serve a shared goal. Nor does it imply that moral reasons are unworthy of analysis. It simply makes the structure of disagreement clearer: are we disputing the figures, the expected effects, the definition of fairness or the weight of competing priorities?
