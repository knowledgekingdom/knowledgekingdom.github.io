<strong>Claim A: 'Great Zimbabwe was built by outsiders.'</strong> The historical question is not answered by asking which group a nineteenth-century explorer found most impressive. Archaeological dating, local material culture, building methods and regional settlement evidence overwhelmingly support construction by African communities ancestral to Shona-speaking peoples. Imported objects demonstrate trade, not the nationality of the builders. The older denial reflects a history of racial prejudice shaping historical interpretation.

<strong>Claim B: 'Mansa Musa was worth exactly a modern trillion dollars.'</strong> No reliable conversion of medieval royal command over land, labor, gold and taxation into an individual's present-day net worth is possible. Sources support exceptional royal wealth and a famous pilgrimage, but they do not establish a precise modern ranking. This claim fails the denominator-and-definition tests from the statistics chapters as well as the source tests from historical method.

<strong>Claim C: 'The Sahara prevented African contact with the world.'</strong> Desert conditions made travel costly, but caravans and institutions connected regions through the Sahara over many centuries. The claim also ignores Red Sea, Mediterranean and Indian Ocean routes. A defensible version would say that environments shaped the intensity, direction and costs of particular connections. Strong evidence rarely requires an absolute word such as <em>never</em>.
