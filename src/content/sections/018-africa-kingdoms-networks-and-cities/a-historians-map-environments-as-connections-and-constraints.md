The northern rim of Africa belongs to the Mediterranean world as well as to African history. Ancient Carthage flourished in the territory of modern Tunisia; Egypt linked the Nile valley with the eastern Mediterranean and the Red Sea; North African towns produced writers, soldiers, merchants and religious movements integral to Greek, Roman and later Islamic worlds. Yet concentrating exclusively on these familiar shores would erase histories that followed different routes through the continent. To study African history well, we must change the scale of the map repeatedly—from local landscapes to interregional exchange.

The Sahara has never been an unchanging blank. In parts of the early and middle Holocene it supported lakes, grasslands and human communities in places that later became far more arid. As its environment changed, patterns of settlement and movement changed too. In the historical periods examined here, camel transport, knowledge of oases and commercial agreements made some desert crossings possible. Such journeys remained costly: people needed water, animals, guides, security and buyers. Routes were not straight lines drawn through empty sand; they were chains of negotiated relationships.

South of the Sahara, the Sahel is a broad, variable transition zone rather than a sharp boundary. Its rainfall fluctuates across time and space. The Niger River bends through important West African agricultural and commercial regions, but its seasonally changing floodplain cannot be treated as a simple canal connecting every city. Farther east the Nile, Ethiopian highlands and Red Sea create another connected system. Inland southern Africa, grazing lands, gold-producing regions and pathways toward the Indian Ocean supported a distinct set of political centers. <strong>Geography shapes opportunities without making historical outcomes inevitable.</strong>
