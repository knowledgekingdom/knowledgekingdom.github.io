Great Zimbabwe's political prominence weakened in the fifteenth century, but the meaning of that change remains debated. Environmental pressures, access to trade, competition among elites and changes in regional settlement could all have mattered in different ways. It is too simple to identify one exhausted resource or one invading army as the complete explanation. Archaeology reveals movement and reorganization rather than the instant disappearance of a population.

In later centuries, powerful political formations including Mutapa and the Rozvi states developed in parts of southern Africa. They drew on regional economic systems, military power and relationships among rulers, cattle owners, cultivators and trading partners. Their political geography was not identical to that of Great Zimbabwe. Moreover, names used in European accounts may not match the ways all inhabitants understood authority and identity.

This historical arc illustrates a core concept from Chapter 7: centers of power may shift when routes, resources and alliances change. A reduction in one city's building activity can coincide with growth elsewhere. To explain the apparent 'decline' of a site, the historian must specify whether the evidence concerns population, architecture, royal authority, trade or cultural practice. Those variables need not all move together.
