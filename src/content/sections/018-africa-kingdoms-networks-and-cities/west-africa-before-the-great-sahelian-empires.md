West African history did not begin when North African merchants wrote about it. Local communities developed intensive agriculture, craft traditions and settlements long before the best-known empires appeared. In parts of present-day Nigeria, the Nok archaeological tradition is associated with striking terracotta sculpture and evidence of early ironworking, with much material dated broadly within the first millennium BCE and the early centuries CE. What political institutions produced these works remains uncertain. It would be careless to infer an empire from artistic sophistication alone.

Farther west and inland, settlements developed along ecological zones where cultivators, herders, fishers and traders could exchange goods. Production did not require a single centralized monarchy. Market towns, extended households, councils, ritual institutions and local authorities could all organize labor and exchange. The archaeology of the Inland Niger Delta, including the settlement complex of Jenné-jeno, demonstrates important urban development without forcing every urban center into the political model of a European capital or a Mesopotamian palace city.

The variety matters for a larger historical argument. Monumental royal architecture is one kind of evidence for political power, but it is not the universal measure of social complexity. Researchers can learn from household density, craft specialization, burial practices, changing settlement patterns and connections among communities. <strong>Not every city must have had a king, and not every kingdom ruled through a single city.</strong>
