A historical textbook can unintentionally create a ranking: mobile communities become farmers, farmers become kingdoms, kingdoms become cities, and cities become empires. Such a ladder assumes that societies all aim toward the same political form. Real history is not so linear. Mobile pastoralism can be an effective response to dry and variable environments. A fishing or trading community may prosper without creating an empire. A highly centralized government may build monuments but also impose severe extraction and warfare. Forms of organization are adaptations, not grades awarded by history.

It is equally mistaken to imagine Africa as an isolated continent awaiting connection by outsiders. Ancient North African cities traded across the Mediterranean; Aksum used Red Sea routes; Mali and Songhai were integrated into Saharan networks; the Swahili coast participated in Indian Ocean commerce; Great Zimbabwe was linked through regional networks to overseas exchange. Nor was every African community connected in the same degree. Geography, political choice, war, technology and environmental change made some routes powerful and others marginal.

A better comparison asks: what environment did this society confront, which institutions coordinated resources, what evidence survives, who benefited, who paid the costs, and how did circumstances change? Those questions make Kush, Mali, Benin, Kongo and Great Zimbabwe legible on their own terms while allowing comparisons with Rome, Persia, China and other societies we've already studied.
