In what is now southwestern Nigeria, the city of Ile-Ife became a major religious and artistic center associated with Yoruba-speaking histories. Its celebrated naturalistic heads and other sculptures, made in terracotta and metal, reflect highly accomplished techniques and complex traditions of representation. The art is not a simple photographic record of an individual ruler. Its use and significance need to be interpreted in relation to local religious ideas, political authority and the archaeological contexts from which works emerged.

Yoruba political history includes many cities and kingdoms, each with its own institutions and changing connections to neighbors. Some traditions recognize Ife as a place of origin or sacred authority, but these narratives cannot all be read as literal demographic histories. Urban centers such as Oyo later developed significant political and military systems, showing that the region's history neither began nor ended with one famous set of sculptures.

One lesson is that artistic excellence should not be treated merely as a clue that a 'proper' state must exist behind it. The works themselves are evidence of technical training, specialized labor, social meaning and aesthetic choice. Their value does not depend on whether researchers can reconstruct the exact tax system of every ruler who sponsored them.
