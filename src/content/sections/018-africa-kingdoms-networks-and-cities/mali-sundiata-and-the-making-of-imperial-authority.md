The Mali Empire grew during the thirteenth century around Mande-speaking political networks and controlled important territories and trade routes over subsequent generations. Traditions about Sundiata Keita recount struggles over kingship, alliances and the establishment of authority; the epic is one of the great achievements of West African oral literature. Its versions cannot be treated as identical contemporary court records. Historians combine them with Arabic-language accounts, archaeology and the wider regional setting to reconstruct a cautious political history.

An empire is more than a ruler's personal wealth. Mali's leaders had to negotiate relationships with communities that supplied food, soldiers, transport and commercial goods. Control could vary across its territory. Some rulers exercised more direct authority in key centers; other areas were connected through tribute, local leaders or military pressure. The political center was not necessarily a single immovable capital. Routes through the Niger region helped connect river transport, overland journeys and Saharan markets.

The story of Mali is sometimes compressed into the biography of Mansa Musa, but empire-building had a longer history, and society was not merely a collection of royal courtiers. Farming households, craftsmen, pastoralists, merchants, religious specialists and people compelled into labor sustained political and economic systems. The opportunities and burdens of imperial rule were unevenly shared. Like other empires studied in this book, Mali combined <strong>authority, negotiation, wealth and the possibility of violence</strong>.
