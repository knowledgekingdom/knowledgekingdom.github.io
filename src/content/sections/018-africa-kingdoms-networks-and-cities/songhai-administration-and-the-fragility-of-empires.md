As Mali's influence declined in some areas, Songhai rulers based around the eastern Niger River built an expanding state. Sonni Ali, who ruled in the later fifteenth century, extended military control over important urban and riverine territories. After him, Askia Muhammad developed a different pattern of alliances and administrative authority, with strong links to Islamic scholarship and trans-Saharan networks. The rulers' achievements and conduct appear differently in chronicles written from distinct political and religious perspectives.

Songhai's reach depended on military forces, river transport, provincial authority and access to commercial and agricultural resources. Yet these strengths did not remove structural vulnerabilities: far-flung territory required cooperation and communication, local rivalries persisted, and military technologies and alliances could change. In 1591 an army sent from Morocco defeated Songhai forces at Tondibi, contributing to the break-up of the empire's central political system. It would be inaccurate to say that one battlefield decision instantly erased all the societies, trade and scholarship that had existed within it.

The same distinction applied to Rome and Aksum: <strong>a dynasty's defeat is not a continent's decline</strong>. Political centers may weaken while local people continue farming, trading, teaching and reorganizing authority. More reliable explanations trace the specific institutions that changed rather than invoking a vague 'fall of civilization.'
