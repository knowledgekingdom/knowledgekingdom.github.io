American policymakers developed the idea of containment: restraining further expansion of Soviet influence rather than directly overthrowing the Soviet government. President Harry Truman's 1947 request for aid to Greece and Turkey linked regional instability to a broader struggle over political futures. The Marshall Plan, announced in 1947 and implemented from 1948, provided substantial resources for Western European recovery and encouraged economic coordination. Its humanitarian and commercial effects were real; so was its strategic aim to reinforce a non-communist Europe.

Western European governments had their own agency in the reconstruction process. Agricultural shortages, damaged housing, disrupted transport and limited access to dollars made recovery urgent. The Soviet leadership rejected participation by the Eastern European governments under its influence, reinforcing economic division. It is too simple to claim the Marshall Plan single-handedly rebuilt Europe: domestic reforms, labor, prior industrial capacity, and world trade also mattered. It is equally incomplete to describe it as charity without political consequences. Aid can alleviate hardship and also alter alliances, markets and power.
