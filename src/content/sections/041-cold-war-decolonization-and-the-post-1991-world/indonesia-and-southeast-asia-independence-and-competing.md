Indonesian nationalists proclaimed independence in August 1945 after Japan's defeat. Dutch authorities attempted to restore colonial power; diplomacy and armed struggle continued until Dutch recognition of Indonesian sovereignty in 1949. Sukarno led an enormous, diverse archipelago with hundreds of language communities and differing local political organizations. Indonesia's leaders had to build a state while managing ideological competition, regional rebellion and economic hardship. In 1965–1966 mass anti-communist killings occurred amid a political transition that elevated Suharto's authoritarian rule.

In French Indochina, Vietnamese independence movements confronted an attempt to restore French colonial rule. French defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954 led to agreements temporarily dividing Vietnam, not a stable final settlement. In Malaya, Britain combined security operations with constitutional negotiations before independence in 1957; the region's later political map developed through the formation and reorganization of Malaysia and Singapore. These examples show that the routes to sovereignty included negotiated transfers, protracted war and authoritarian consolidation. The word decolonization identifies a broad historical process, not a standard political formula.
