When Mikhail Gorbachev became Soviet leader in 1985, the country faced economic stagnation, costly external commitments and declining confidence in official institutions. Perestroika sought restructuring; glasnost permitted more public discussion and criticism. Reform opened space for activists, national movements and citizens to challenge corruption and historical repression. But partial economic liberalization, political contestation and shortages also destabilized arrangements that had previously been maintained through coercion and predictable bureaucratic authority.

Gorbachev reduced international confrontation, pursued arms-control agreements and declined to use overwhelming Soviet force to preserve every allied East European government. His decisions mattered, but so did pressure from workers, religious groups, dissident networks, national organizations and ordinary citizens. The ending of the Cold War was therefore partly negotiated among leaders and partly driven by social movements. Explanations that credit one U.S. president alone, or treat Soviet collapse as purely an economic inevitability, ignore this complex interaction.
