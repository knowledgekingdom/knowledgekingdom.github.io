After the Cuban crisis and the danger of the early 1960s, Washington and Moscow pursued some agreements to manage confrontation. The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty limited certain nuclear testing, while the 1972 SALT I agreements and Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty established constraints amid continued competition. The Helsinki Final Act of 1975 recognized principles of European security, sovereign borders and human rights, while remaining a political commitment rather than an immediately enforceable constitutional charter.

Détente did not signify the end of rivalry. The superpowers continued to support opposing forces in regional conflicts and accumulated large nuclear arsenals. Human-rights advocates in Eastern Europe nonetheless used Helsinki commitments as language against repression. This produced an unexpected feedback loop: a diplomatic agreement intended in part to stabilize relations gave dissidents a standard against which to evaluate governments. The lesson is that international institutions sometimes matter through changes in expectations and legitimacy, not only through sanctions or direct military action.
