Fidel Castro's revolutionary forces overthrew Cuba's Batista dictatorship in 1959, following a history of inequality, corruption and U.S. influence on the island. The new government nationalized property and increasingly aligned with the Soviet Union amid American hostility. A U.S.-backed invasion attempt at the Bay of Pigs failed in 1961. In 1962 U.S. reconnaissance revealed Soviet nuclear missiles under construction in Cuba, triggering an exceptionally dangerous confrontation.

The Kennedy administration announced a naval quarantine and demanded removal of the missiles. Soviet and American leaders negotiated a settlement: Soviet missiles were withdrawn from Cuba, the United States pledged not to invade the island, and U.S. Jupiter missiles in Turkey were later removed under a confidential understanding. Cuban leaders were not passive, although the decisive bilateral talks often excluded them. What prevented nuclear war was not an automatic law of deterrence but uncertain intelligence, restraint, communication and difficult choices. The crisis encouraged improved direct communication and later arms-control efforts without ending the rivalry.
