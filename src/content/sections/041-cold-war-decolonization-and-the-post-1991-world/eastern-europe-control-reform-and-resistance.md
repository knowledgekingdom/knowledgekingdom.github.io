In Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, Romania and other countries, Soviet influence and domestic communist parties helped build one-party regimes after the war. The pathways varied: coercion, political manipulation, purges and the absorption of rival parties played different roles in each country. Land reform, nationalization and expansion of education accompanied secret-police surveillance, censorship and restrictions on religious and civic institutions. Explaining social changes without the coercion distorts the experience; describing only coercion leaves the economic and educational transformation unexplained.

Moscow did not always control every initiative, but it intervened decisively when allied governments threatened to leave its sphere. Soviet troops suppressed the Hungarian Revolution in 1956; Warsaw Pact forces invaded Czechoslovakia in 1968 to halt the Prague Spring reforms. Poland's Solidarity movement later demonstrated the strength of autonomous labor organization despite the imposition of martial law in 1981. Yugoslavia, meanwhile, broke with Stalin in 1948 and charted a more independent socialist path. Eastern Europe was a region of varied societies and political choices, not merely a colored zone on a superpower map.
