China's civil war resumed after Japan's defeat. The Chinese Communist Party under Mao Zedong established the People's Republic of China in 1949, while the Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek retreated to Taiwan. The new division emerged from Chinese political history and warfare, not simply from Soviet direction. Land reform, campaigns against perceived opponents, centralized party authority and economic reconstruction remade mainland Chinese society. The Nationalist government retained control of Taiwan, where authoritarian rule later gave way to democratic reform.

Initially the People's Republic aligned with Moscow, but the relationship eventually fractured over ideology, security and national interests. The Sino-Soviet split from the late 1950s and 1960s demonstrated that communist governments could be rivals. The Korean peninsula, divided following Japan's defeat, became another point where domestic political struggles and external intervention intersected. East Asian history therefore cannot be reduced to a single East-West line. China, Korea, Japan and Taiwan each developed institutions that influenced the region's later economic and military balance.
