As the world became more economically connected, shared problems required cooperation beyond bilateral alliances. Climate change links fossil-fuel combustion, land use, historical industrialization and present-day development. Ozone-depleting chemicals were addressed through the 1987 Montreal Protocol, an example of international cooperation grounded in scientific monitoring and negotiated obligations. Global epidemics, including HIV/AIDS and COVID-19, tested public-health systems, international communication and unequal access to resources.

These problems complicate older maps of power. A government can possess a large army while remaining vulnerable to imported pathogens, energy disruptions, extreme weather or financial contagion. Similarly, progress on vaccination or communications may help societies that would otherwise lack strategic influence. International cooperation involves disputes over who pays, whose knowledge counts and how obligations are monitored. The central lesson is not that borders no longer matter, but that some consequences cross them regardless of political preference.
