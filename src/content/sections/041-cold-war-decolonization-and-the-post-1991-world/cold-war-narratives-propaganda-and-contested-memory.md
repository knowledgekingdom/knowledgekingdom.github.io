Western and Soviet governments invested heavily in public diplomacy, radio, television, schoolbooks, cultural exchanges and secret information operations. Propaganda could mix truth, selective presentation and outright fabrication. A U.S. film praising freedom might ignore U.S.-backed dictatorships; a Soviet poster about social equality might ignore prison camps and censorship. The appropriate response is not to assume every source is worthless but to ask who produced it, for whom, what it omits, and whether independent evidence supports its claims.

Memory after 1989 is equally contested. Some families recall social security and industrial opportunity; others recall informers, prison, forced name changes or travel restrictions. People can describe different parts of the same system without all memories being equally sufficient as evidence for national trends. Official archives, demographic data, photographs, personal testimony and foreign diplomatic records must be compared. The skills from Part I—source provenance, causation, probability and ethical distinction—are especially useful when historical questions remain politically sensitive.
