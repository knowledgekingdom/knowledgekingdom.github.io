Ghana gained independence in 1957 under leaders associated with Kwame Nkrumah, becoming an influential symbol of African self-government. In 1960 many former French and Belgian territories became sovereign states, a year sometimes called the Year of Africa. French rule ended in Algeria after a brutal war marked by repression, insurgency and suffering among civilians. In Kenya, colonial detention and violence accompanied the Mau Mau emergency and the path to independence. Portugal's African colonies gained independence after long conflicts and the 1974 Portuguese revolution.

African nationalists were not united on how to structure the resulting states. They argued over federalism, ethnic representation, one-party government, socialism, private enterprise, pan-Africanism and relations with former colonial powers. Colonial boundaries sometimes enclosed many communities without a shared political history. Nonetheless, retaining inherited borders often seemed less dangerous than reopening almost every frontier. Explaining later crises as unavoidable products of 'tribalism' ignores colonial institutions, external interventions, economic structures and post-independence political decisions. African people created states and political movements under exceptionally difficult conditions.
