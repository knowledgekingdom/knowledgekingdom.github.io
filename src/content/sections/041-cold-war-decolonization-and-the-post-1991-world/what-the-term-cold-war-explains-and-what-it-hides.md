The Cold War usually refers to the prolonged strategic and ideological confrontation between the United States and the Soviet Union, roughly from the late 1940s until 1989–1991. It was 'cold' chiefly in the sense that the two superpowers avoided a direct, full-scale war against one another. The same decades included devastating hot wars in Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan, Angola and elsewhere. The term must not conceal casualties simply because the main capitals did not exchange nuclear strikes.

The United States represented a market-oriented economy and multiparty constitutional traditions, although it struggled with racial discrimination and supported some repressive foreign partners. The Soviet Union officially pursued Marxist-Leninist socialism through one-party rule, state planning and a powerful security apparatus; it provided education and industrial growth while restricting dissent and political competition. Both systems changed over time, and neither controlled every government claiming ideological kinship. This history requires comparing actual institutions rather than slogans, and distinguishing how people experienced state power from what those states said about themselves.
