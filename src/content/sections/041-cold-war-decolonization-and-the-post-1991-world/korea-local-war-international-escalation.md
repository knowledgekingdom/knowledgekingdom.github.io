War began in June 1950 when North Korean forces attacked South Korea. A United Nations-authorized force led by the United States intervened on the South Korean side; China later entered on the North Korean side, while the Soviet Union provided substantial support without openly sending an equivalent conventional field army. Front lines swung dramatically before settling near the original dividing region. The 1953 armistice stopped large-scale fighting but did not create a comprehensive peace treaty.

Korea illustrates the problem of describing a conflict only as a superpower proxy war. Korean leaders and populations had their own goals, histories and responsibilities. At the same time, outside powers supplied weapons, personnel and strategic guarantees that dramatically changed the war's scale. Civilian losses were immense. The result institutionalized one of the world's most durable military divisions. In later decades South Korea experienced authoritarian government followed by democratization and rapid industrialization, while North Korea maintained hereditary one-party dictatorship and severe international isolation. The original Cold War boundary outlived the Cold War itself.
