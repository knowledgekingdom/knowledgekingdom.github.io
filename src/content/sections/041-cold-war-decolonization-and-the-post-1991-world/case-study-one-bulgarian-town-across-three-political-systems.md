Imagine a fictional Bulgarian town in 1945 with an agricultural market, a modest school and limited electrification. By 1985 it has apartment blocks, a state factory, trained technicians, a hospital and improved roads, but citizens cannot organize an independent political party and some minority families face forced assimilation. After 1989 the town gains free elections and commercial choice, while the factory loses markets, unemployment rises and younger people migrate to larger cities or abroad. By the 2000s new investment and EU-linked projects may arrive, but the advantages are distributed unevenly.

This composite example is not a statistical reconstruction of an actual settlement. Its purpose is to prevent a false comparison. If one person praises industrial employment and another praises political freedom, both may be pointing to real differences while answering different questions. A careful assessment compares living standards, freedoms, health, migration, environment and opportunities across generations, noting which groups benefited and who paid the costs. Historical judgment improves when we refuse to compress a complex century into a single verdict.
