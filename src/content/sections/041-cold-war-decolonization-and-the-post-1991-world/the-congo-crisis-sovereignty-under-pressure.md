The Belgian Congo became independent in June 1960 with limited preparation for autonomous administrative and military leadership. Almost immediately the army mutinied, the mineral-rich province of Katanga attempted secession, and Belgian forces intervened. Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba sought to defend national unity while the United States and Soviet Union interpreted the crisis through their rivalry. Lumumba was removed and murdered in 1961, amid involvement and responsibility extending beyond any simple local factional story.

The United Nations deployed peacekeeping forces, exposing the difficult distinction between preserving sovereignty and intervening in a state's internal political struggle. Joseph-Désiré Mobutu later consolidated an authoritarian regime sustained in part by external alliances. The Congo became a central example of how formal independence could coexist with vulnerability to foreign corporations, covert operations, regional power struggles and weak national institutions. Yet Congolese political agency, labor movements and local interests remained active throughout. The case warns against assuming that either colonial withdrawal or outside military assistance automatically creates stable statehood.
