Descriptions of Mongol conquest often oscillate between minimization and sensationalism. Some writers celebrate commercial exchange so enthusiastically that they pass quickly over destroyed towns. Others repeat spectacular casualty figures and turn a vast population into an undifferentiated story of massacre. Both distort the historical record. We know from Persian, Chinese, Arabic and other sources that mass killing, enslavement, forced relocation of skilled workers, seizure of food supplies and damage to irrigation systems took place. We also know many communities survived and later rebuilt under new dynasties.

A historian facing a claim that “a million people were killed in one city” should ask: Who wrote the number? When? Could the city plausibly have housed that population? Is the figure rhetorical, copied from an earlier author or supported by archaeology and independent records? Medieval writers sometimes expressed moral horror using rounded or immense figures. We can acknowledge that an atrocity took place without pretending to know a precise death toll.

There is a second trap: <strong>destruction is not the same as an irreversible disappearance of civilization</strong>. Local agriculture, languages, art, law and trade could survive, adapt or return, although recovery might take generations and some institutions never reappeared in their earlier form. The people who rebuilt were not necessarily the same as those who ruled before. The word <em>resilience</em> should not be used carelessly, as if survival made forced displacement acceptable. It describes choices made in difficult circumstances, not a moral credit transferred to conquerors.
