Return to our imagined camp from the opening. Its couriers, interpreters, soldiers, artisans and envoys belonged to a world in which an order issued in one place could affect a city thousands of kilometers away. That reach was extraordinary. But the people who maintained the roads and provided the grain did not all benefit equally, and their experiences are less visible in the documents of courts. The empire was a network held together through family claims, administration, resources and violence. Each could strengthen the others, and each could become a source of weakness.

Later Eurasian history did not simply continue as if the Mongols had never existed. Political institutions, trade relationships, imperial memories and cultural practices survived in altered forms. The <strong>Golden Horde's legacy</strong> shaped politics across the Volga and Black Sea regions; <strong>Yuan history</strong> influenced later Chinese memories of empire; <strong>Ilkhanid scholarship and art</strong> became part of the history of Iran and the Islamic world; Chagatai and other Central Asian political traditions developed along their own paths. These histories belong to their local societies as much as to a common Mongol dynasty.

The next chapter will return to medieval Europe, Byzantium and the Ottoman emergence. The steppe will remain relevant there: eastern European and Balkan politics were never sealed off from the events described here. But before moving forward, retain the chapter's central distinction. <strong>Connectivity is not the same as peace, and destruction is not the same as historical emptiness.</strong> The Mongol Empire demonstrates both with unusual force.
