The Mongol period left vivid travel narratives. <strong>John of Plano Carpini</strong> traveled as a papal envoy in the 1240s. <strong>William of Rubruck</strong>, a Franciscan who visited Mongol territories in the 1250s, described routes, religious encounters and the court. <strong>Marco Polo</strong> is associated with accounts of Yuan China and Asian travel later in the century. These texts help us imagine distances, administrative practices and cultural misunderstandings. They also require disciplined source criticism: travelers had sponsors, purposes, limited languages and varying degrees of direct observation.

A visitor may accurately report the clothing he saw at court but repeat hearsay about a remote kingdom. A text copied and edited after the journey may contain literary embellishments. In Polo's case, historians debate particular claims, his precise itinerary and the text's transmission, rather than dismissing the entire account or accepting every episode. The problem is not solved by simply calling the author “trustworthy” or “untrustworthy.” Reliability must be assessed <strong>claim by claim</strong>.

Imagine an envoy writing that “the khan has ordered all subjects to be treated fairly.” That establishes the existence of a claim about policy, not that every soldier or tax agent obeyed. To judge implementation, look for independent local records. The same method works for a Persian chronicle praising a ruler and a European report condemning him. <strong>Different perspectives are necessary evidence, not obstacles to be removed.</strong>
