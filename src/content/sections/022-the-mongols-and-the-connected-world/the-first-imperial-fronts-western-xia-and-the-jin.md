Conquest began near the homeland. The <strong>Tangut-led Western Xia</strong> state and the <strong>Jurchen-led Jin</strong> dynasty had long-standing relationships with frontier communities. Mongol attacks on Western Xia unfolded in phases, while operations against the Jin began in <strong>1211</strong> and developed over years of campaigning. The Jin state did not collapse in a single cavalry charge; the conquest of northern China required sieges, alliances, administrative learning and repeated strategic changes. The Jin dynasty ultimately fell in <strong>1234</strong>, after Chinggis Khan's death.

Chinese records provide a different view from Mongol victory narratives. They describe the crises confronting cities and courts, the experience of officials, and decisions made by defenders. They also reveal that the invaders depended on people who already knew Chinese bureaucratic, agricultural and technical systems. Skilled administrators and engineers were valuable because victory over a city did not immediately produce regular revenue or stable obedience.

Western Xia faced a devastating final campaign in <strong>1226–27</strong>. Chinggis Khan died in <strong>1227</strong>, traditionally while engaged in this war, although the precise circumstances are uncertain. Later legends provide dramatic versions of his death, but none should be promoted to fact without evidence. The early wars show a broader point: the Mongol Empire emerged through interaction with existing Asian states. <strong>It did not simply descend upon a passive continent from outside history.</strong>
