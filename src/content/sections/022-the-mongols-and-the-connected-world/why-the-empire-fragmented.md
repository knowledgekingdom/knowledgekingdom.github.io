A vast empire needs agreed rules of succession, sustainable taxation, communication and a means of resolving conflict among powerful families. The Mongol polity struggled with all four. After the mid-thirteenth century, branches of the ruling family competed over the <strong>great khanate</strong>, territory, tribute and trade routes. Kublai's victory in the civil war did not restore uncontested authority over every western ruler. Warfare between the Ilkhanate and the Golden Horde, and conflict across Central Asia, undermined the idea of an undivided Mongol Peace.

Regional institutions also created <strong>path dependence</strong>. A Yuan emperor relied on Chinese agrarian taxation and court politics; a Jochid khan ruled through different arrangements. Diverse populations, religious conversion, local elites and revenue systems made each successor polity harder to govern from one center. This was not a single moment when a unified empire neatly broke into four pieces. Fragmentation proceeded unevenly, while dynastic ties and communication endured.

By the fourteenth century, the Ilkhanate disintegrated as a united state after <strong>1335</strong>, the <strong>Yuan lost control of China to the Ming in 1368</strong>, and the Chagatai and Jochid political worlds underwent their own transformations. Mongol-descended rulers and political traditions continued long beyond those dates. <strong>The end of one dynasty is not the extinction of the people who lived under it</strong>, nor the disappearance of the institutions and cultural links that outlived it.
