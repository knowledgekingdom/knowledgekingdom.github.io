Suppose a fourteenth-century Iranian manuscript contains a painting with East Asian visual motifs. It is reasonable to ask whether imperial patronage, traveling artisans, the movement of finished objects or artistic imitation helped those motifs appear. The object is evidence of a <strong>cultural connection</strong>, but it is not automatically proof that one named Chinese artist visited the Iranian workshop. The same style can arrive through a second-hand drawing, a textile or the training of an apprentice.

To reconstruct its history, a museum curator examines pigments, paper, binding, provenance and comparable works, and checks written references to artists and patrons. A historian examines political contacts and routes without assuming that every plausible route was used. Even if a dynasty sponsored exchange, the actual sequence of transmission could be complex and partly invisible.

This lesson generalizes. A Buddhist image at a Mongol court, a Persian textile in China or a western coin in a steppe settlement offers a clue about circulation. <strong>Circulation, direct travel and political control are three different claims.</strong> This is why the most useful map is often a network of possible interactions with degrees of evidence, not a straight line from one capital to another.
