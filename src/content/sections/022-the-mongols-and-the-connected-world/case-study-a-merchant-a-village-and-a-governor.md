Imagine three <strong>hypothetical</strong> residents of a Mongol-ruled region around 1300. A long-distance merchant with imperial authorization receives help changing horses and avoids some tolls. A nearby village must provide grain or livestock for a relay station. A provincial governor tries to meet revenue demands while keeping agricultural production alive. The three people participate in one system but face very different incentives. A traveler might praise its safety while villagers complain that the cost has been transferred to them.

Now change one condition: a civil war interrupts the route. The merchant's privileges become less valuable; a governor demands new supplies; the village has fewer workers after animals are taken. Even without an invading army nearby, conflict between dynastic elites can reach household finances. Conversely, peace and predictable rules might help markets revive. The imagined case does not assert a specific documented set of accounts; it shows how historians can organize questions about uneven consequences.

What evidence would test the case? <strong>Permission tablets and court orders</strong> indicate formal privileges. <strong>Fiscal registers</strong>, where surviving, reveal obligations. <strong>Local petitions</strong> might document burdens and disputes. <strong>Archaeological evidence</strong> could show changes in settlement or goods, but it rarely identifies a single cause on its own. The strongest account would not choose the merchant's optimistic story over the peasant's hardship: it would seek to explain both.
