<strong>Biological evolution</strong> means changes in inherited characteristics of populations across generations. Variation arises through processes including mutation and recombination, and the frequencies of variants change through natural selection, genetic drift, migration, and other processes. Evolution does not require an animal to 'want' to change. A long-legged individual does not stretch its body into a new species by trying harder; rather, inherited differences can affect survival and reproduction across many generations in particular environments.

Imagine a population whose members vary in how efficiently they walk long distances. In some environmental conditions, certain anatomical traits might contribute to getting food or avoiding danger. If those traits are heritable and affect reproductive success, their frequency may change. But the story will not be uniform: some characteristics may spread by chance; others may be linked to traits that are favored for different reasons; and an environment can change faster than a population adapts. Natural selection is powerful, but it is not the only force.

A <strong>species</strong> is commonly thought of as a population or set of populations forming a distinct evolutionary lineage. In living organisms, scientists often consider whether groups can reproduce with each other; in fossils, reproduction cannot be observed. Researchers therefore compare anatomy, ages, locations, and, where possible, genetic evidence. There is no single operational definition that settles every ancient case. That is why scholars sometimes disagree about whether two fossil groups represent one species with variation or separate species.

For human origins, the most important mental correction is this: <strong>evolution is a tree of branching populations, not a ladder with modern humans on the top rung</strong>. You have probably seen the familiar cartoon in which a bent, ape-like figure gradually stands straighter until he becomes a modern human. That picture is memorable but misleading. Our closest living relatives, chimpanzees and bonobos, are not unsuccessful humans or our direct ancestors. They and we descend from older shared ancestors. Chimpanzees have been evolving along their own branches as long as humans have.

Human and chimpanzee lineages are generally understood to have separated from a common ancestral population millions of years ago, often discussed in the broad vicinity of <strong>six to eight million years</strong>. Such estimates depend on genetic models and a sparse fossil record. The point is not an exact birthday for humanity. It is that the separation occurred deep in time and was followed by many populations adapting to changing African environments. Early hominins—the human branch after divergence from the lineage leading to chimpanzees—were diverse. Most left no descendants alive today. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

Nor does evolution operate toward a predetermined goal of superior intelligence. It does not produce a universal ranking of creatures. A cheetah is remarkably specialized for speed; a bat for powered flight; a whale for aquatic life. Our lineage eventually developed unusual combinations of walking, manipulation, cooperation, language, and cumulative technology. These capabilities became consequential, but their emergence was contingent, extended, and incomplete at each stage.

## Why the 'survival of the fittest' slogan is easily misused

In evolutionary biology, <em>fitness</em> concerns reproductive success in a specific environment, not moral worth, strength, wealth, or social status. A trait advantageous in one environment can be neutral or disadvantageous in another. Human societies are also shaped by learning, institutions, ethics, and choices. Biological evolution provides no scientific justification for judging modern people as more or less deserving of rights. Historical efforts to turn evolutionary language into social hierarchies, racial doctrines, or eugenics confused scientific explanation with moral prescription and often relied on false assumptions. A shared evolutionary origin, if anything, makes it clear how closely related modern human populations are.
