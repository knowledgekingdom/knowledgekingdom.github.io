Imagine standing on a shoreline with no modern map. Beyond the visible water lie islands and, farther away, lands you may have heard about or glimpsed. To get there may require planning a crossing, knowing wind and currents, and maintaining a craft. The settlement of <strong>Sahul</strong>—the Pleistocene landmass incorporating Australia, New Guinea, and Tasmania at lower sea levels—has enormous significance because reaching it involved moving through island Southeast Asia, where water passages remained even during periods of low sea level.

Australia's early settlement is often summarized as 'around 50,000 years ago,' but that convenient number hides real debate. At <strong>Madjedbebe</strong> in northern Australia, a 2017 <em>Nature</em> paper argued from archaeological layers and dating for occupation about <strong>65,000 years ago</strong>. Other scholars have questioned aspects of the chronology and how it fits broader regional evidence. What is secure is that humans were established in Sahul by the late Pleistocene, long before the emergence of modern states and written histories. Whether the earliest occupation dates comfortably to 65,000 years ago or somewhat later changes models of movement out of Africa, not the profound antiquity of Indigenous Australian and Papuan histories. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-13" class="cite">13</a>\]

Sea level is central to the puzzle. Much water was locked in ice sheets during glacial periods, lowering global sea level. Places now divided by shallow seas could have been connected by land. Australia and New Guinea were joined on Sahul; many parts of western Indonesia formed the extensive landmass known as Sunda. However, <strong>Sunda and Sahul were never joined by a simple continuous dry road</strong> during these periods. At least some crossing of open water was necessary. This makes watercraft or other means of maritime crossing a reasonable inference, even when the original boats have not survived.

A familiar trap is to read a modern world map backward. If a present-day channel exists, you may assume it was equally wide and difficult 50,000 years ago; if two islands are now separate, you may overlook an old land bridge. A good prehistoric map must show <em>paleogeography</em>, meaning reconstructed earlier geography, not simply contemporary country borders. Even with a reconstructed coastline, one cannot draw migration routes with the certainty of a modern train schedule. Routes are hypotheses constrained by archaeological sites, sea-level history, ecology, and genetics.

The people who crossed and settled Sahul became ancestors of populations with deep, diverse, and continuing cultures. They encountered new plants, animals, seasons, and landscapes. Their ways of living changed over time. It would be inaccurate to imagine a single uniform 'Aboriginal culture' frozen for tens of millennia; Indigenous societies have their own distinct languages, traditions, histories, and adaptations. Likewise, modern national frontiers between Australia, Papua New Guinea, and Indonesia should not be projected onto the ancient world.
