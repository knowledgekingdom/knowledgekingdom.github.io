Bones can show anatomy. Stones can show work. But how do we investigate ideas, imagination, or language in worlds without writing? The challenge is to look for <strong>behavioral traces</strong>. Pigments, engravings, personal ornaments, burial practices, spatial organization, and structured toolmaking can suggest symbolic meanings and social learning. Each type of evidence has alternative explanations. A perforated shell may have been worn as ornament; a pattern cut into a surface may reflect deliberate marking; neither provides a complete transcript of what its makers thought.

Some early evidence of intentional markings comes from African Middle Stone Age contexts. Ochre was used and processed in different ways, and engraved pieces reveal the capacity for repeated design. Such finds undermine an outdated narrative in which symbolic behavior appeared suddenly in Europe and then spread to a supposedly passive rest of the world. They also caution against assuming that modern cognition must have emerged simultaneously with one artwork or one toolkit.

A striking example comes from <strong>Sulawesi, Indonesia</strong>. Researchers studying paintings in limestone caves used uranium-series methods on deposits associated with the rock art. A 2024 study argued that a composition showing human-like figures interacting with a pig was at least <strong>51,200 years old</strong>. Its importance lies in the antiquity of representational scene-making outside Europe. The numerical result is a <strong>minimum age</strong>, because the mineral covering used for dating formed after the paint beneath it. If the dated coating is 51,200 years old, the painting is at least that old, possibly older. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]

The distinction between pigment and covering rock should now sound familiar. It is the dating lesson from section 5 applied to culture. Archaeology has a habit of joining subjects that seemed separate: a cave image can demand chemistry, geology, history, and anthropology all at once.

What about <strong>language</strong>? All surviving human societies use complex language; linguistic ability is central to cultural transmission. But spoken words leave almost no direct archaeological record. The anatomy of hearing and speech, genes associated with language-related functions, social learning, brain evolution, and material culture can constrain hypotheses. None allows a simple claim that one particular fossil individual spoke a modern language, or that speech began exactly when a species name first appears. The origins of language remain a major field of research.

The most consequential form of cultural learning may be <strong>cumulative culture</strong>: one generation inherits techniques, stories, norms, and ways of thinking, improves or modifies them, and passes them onward. A well-made tool can embody knowledge from many people, even if no individual understands every historical experiment that led to its final form. Knowledge compounds socially. An infant born into one society learns different techniques from an equally capable infant born elsewhere. Culture is not merely a decorative layer added after biological evolution; it changes which problems are solvable and can change the environments in which people live.
