Try a simple thought experiment. Place a hard stone in one hand and another stone beside it. By striking at the right angle, someone might detach a sharp flake from a core. The detached piece can cut meat, hide, plants, or other material, depending on its properties. It seems elementary beside a modern knife, but a controlled flake requires attention to material, force, and technique. The most important innovation may not have been the object itself. It was the capacity to recognize a useful fracture and repeat it.

For decades, stone-tool manufacture was treated as a hallmark of <em>Homo</em>. Excavations have complicated that idea. Artifacts at <strong>Lomekwi 3 in Kenya, dated to about 3.3 million years ago</strong>, indicate a form of stone working predating the oldest widely accepted <em>Homo</em> fossils. The Lomekwian implements are not simply the same as later Oldowan toolkits; they include techniques involving battering and breaking stones. We do not know which hominin population made them. Calling them 'human technology' in the broad ancestral sense is more defensible than assigning them to an inventor we cannot identify. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

Later, <strong>Oldowan</strong> assemblages show systematic production of cores and flakes. Evidence at Nyayanga in Kenya places Oldowan-style artifacts around three million years ago, older than earlier textbook dates. The find is important partly because it warns against assuming the makers must have been members of <em>Homo</em>: the associated fossils and tools do not provide such a simple identification. In archaeology, stone flakes can survive for ages while wooden implements, fiber cordage, and many forms of food preparation disappear. Our technological record is therefore heavily biased toward durable material. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]

By roughly <strong>1.76 million years ago</strong>, large cutting tools associated with the Acheulean tradition appear in the record. The distinctive handaxes were shaped by removing flakes from both sides. Yet technology did not advance uniformly from 'primitive' to 'advanced' at a fixed speed. Different toolkits coexisted; techniques spread unevenly; locally useful solutions persisted. An enduring tool type may reflect reliability, available stone, teaching traditions, or ecology—not necessarily a population incapable of inventing something new.

Tools also changed diets, and diets affected social behavior. A sharp stone could help access meat from a carcass, remove hides, process tough plant material, or work wood. Those capacities might broaden food options, but fossil cut marks do not tell us every detail of a meal or whether the makers hunted the animal rather than scavenged it. Archaeologists infer from patterns: marks made before or after animal tooth impressions, the distribution of bones, the age profile of prey, nearby stone artifacts, and the wider landscape.

The first tools made of wood, grass, or hide are mostly lost. That absence should make us humble. If a community used wooden digging sticks, containers, or carrying devices, those technologies could have altered its life without leaving a preserved trace. Human history starts not with complete information but with a sample filtered by geology, decay, excavation, and chance.
