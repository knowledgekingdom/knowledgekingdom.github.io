Science does not make all historical claims equally certain. The broad <strong>African origin of human evolution</strong> and the deep age of the hominin family are supported by extensive converging evidence. <strong>Bipedalism long before Homo sapiens</strong> is strongly supported by anatomy and footprints. <strong>Stone-tool use before modern humans</strong>, the early dispersal of hominins into Eurasia, and interbreeding among some later human lineages also have robust support. These conclusions do not depend on one specimen or fashionable theory.

Other questions remain more unsettled. Exactly how many hominin species should be recognized? Which early species made particular toolkits? How did language emerge, and when? Which groups contributed to the most ancient migrations beyond Africa? Precisely when was Sahul first occupied? What combination of routes and movements populated the Americas? Which causes best explain the disappearance of Neanderthals in different regions? These questions are investigable, but honest answers require ranges, models, and the possibility of revision.

An intelligent reader can hold both kinds of thought simultaneously: <strong>there is enough evidence to know a great deal; there is not enough evidence to know everything</strong>. That balance is more useful than false certainty or the cynical claim that scientists are 'always changing their minds' and therefore cannot be trusted. In the best cases, changing a conclusion is what intellectual seriousness looks like when better evidence arrives.

For the purposes of Knowledge Kingdom, the central idea is not an exact migration date. It is a new understanding of what humanity is: a single surviving species from a branching evolutionary past, shaped by biological change, environmental pressures, creative behavior, social learning, and multiple waves of movement. Our modern differences are real and historically meaningful; our deep ancestry is intertwined.
