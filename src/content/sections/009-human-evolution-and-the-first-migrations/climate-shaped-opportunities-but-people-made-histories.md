The Pleistocene epoch included repeated glacial and warmer intervals, major changes in sea level, and shifting ecosystems. These were not a single event called 'the Ice Age'; they were a succession of changing conditions. The <strong>Last Glacial Maximum</strong>, approximately 26,500–19,000 years ago, was a period when continental ice sheets reached great extent. In some regions cold and aridity made life harder; elsewhere changing coastlines and ecosystems opened routes. How a given population responded depended on the technologies, networks, resources, and traditions available to it.

Climate influenced humans and humans increasingly altered environments. Hunting, landscape burning, collection, movement of species, and changing settlement patterns all affected local ecology at different scales. The possible role of people in late Pleistocene extinctions of large animals is widely debated and varies by region. In some places hunting pressure may have contributed; climate and habitat changes were also significant. Saying 'humans killed all the megafauna' is as careless as saying human activity played no role anywhere.

One of the broadest historical transitions began toward the end of the last glacial period, when some communities intensified management and, eventually, domestication of plants and animals. Agriculture was not a switch that suddenly turned on everywhere at 10,000 BCE. Different regions took different paths; foragers and farmers coexisted; some communities combined methods for long periods. We will study this in Chapter 10. It matters here because the people who later built villages and cities were already heirs to a vast history of learning and ecological adaptation.
