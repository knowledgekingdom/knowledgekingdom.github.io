A remarkable feature of human history is that modern humans were not the first hominins to leave Africa. Fossils associated with early members of <em>Homo</em>, especially the broader <em>Homo erectus</em> lineage, appear outside Africa by around <strong>1.8 million years ago</strong>. The site of Dmanisi, in present-day Georgia, is especially important because it shows early humans living far beyond the African environments where their ancestors evolved. The individuals were not modern humans. They belonged to a much earlier chapter of our family. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>, <a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]

<em>Homo erectus</em> itself is one of the most consequential and long-lived names in paleoanthropology. Its fossils and associated populations occur across parts of Africa and Asia over a very long span. Researchers debate whether African fossils sometimes called <em>Homo ergaster</em> should be grouped within the same broadly defined species. Compared with many earlier hominins, these humans generally had longer legs and body proportions more suitable for efficient travel. But there was variation among populations; a taxonomic label should never make us imagine identical bodies spread across half the globe. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]

What made long-distance movement possible? There is no single proven answer. Changes in body form may have helped with sustained walking. Flexibility in diet, use of cutting tools, cooperation, and familiarity with diverse habitats may all have mattered. Climate fluctuations sometimes created routes between landscapes; at other times they imposed barriers. A successful movement need not involve an organized expedition with a destination. A population can expand its range over generations as families occupy adjacent territories, then move farther still.

Consider the arithmetic of slow movement. Suppose a population's outer range shifts just <strong>one kilometer every twenty years</strong>. That sounds almost motionless. Over 20,000 years, however, it would amount to 1,000 kilometers. This is only a hypothetical illustration, not a measured rate for <em>Homo erectus</em>. It shows why we should not picture all prehistoric migrations as deliberate journeys by an individual walking from one modern country to another. Geographic change at the scale of a population can be immense even when an average person's experience remains local.

Researchers once frequently linked the spread of <em>Homo erectus</em> to fire, assuming that cooking and warmth opened new habitats. Controlled fire almost certainly transformed hominin life, but its origins remain difficult to pin down. Charcoal at a site does not automatically prove people lit a fire; lightning can burn vegetation, and later deposits can mix with older ones. At South Africa's <strong>Wonderwerk Cave</strong>, microscopic study of sediments identified burned bone and ashed plant remains in layers about <strong>one million years old</strong>, offering strong evidence of burning in an archaeological context. Whether every such fire was repeatedly controlled, and precisely how cooking changed bodies and societies, remains a separate question. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-6" class="cite">6</a>\]

Fire changes the options available to a community: it can extend useful hours after dark, provide warmth, transform food, deter some animals, and create a center for social activity. Its effects also depend on reliable fuel and skill. We should not turn it into a magic invention that immediately made humans 'modern.' Like stone technologies, control of fire had a long and uneven history.

## Living alongside other humans

For much of prehistory, <strong>multiple kinds of humans existed at the same time</strong>. That fact is easy to miss when museums display a tidy sequence of skulls. During certain periods, different populations of <em>Homo</em> occupied Africa, Europe, and Asia. Some lived far apart; others' ranges approached or overlapped. No map of a single date captures the entire shifting picture. Human exclusivity is a relatively recent condition. The world in which our ancestors evolved was not one where <em>Homo sapiens</em> stood alone as the only possible human form.
