A map labelled <strong>1:100,000</strong> means one unit on the map represents 100,000 of the same units on the ground, at the projection's designated scale. One centimetre therefore represents one kilometre. On a <strong>1:250,000</strong> map, 4 cm represents 1,000,000 cm = 10 km. That is a straight-line or map-measured distance under the assumed scale—not the length of a winding road. If you forget to convert centimetres into kilometres, even a simple problem can produce a wildly wrong answer.

The language of cartographers can sound backwards: a <strong>large-scale map</strong> might show one city block at 1:5,000, while a <strong>small-scale map</strong> shows an entire continent at 1:25,000,000. The first is called large-scale because the fraction 1/5,000 is larger than 1/25,000,000 and it captures more local detail. You should not equate large-scale with a large geographic area.

Every map also <strong>generalizes</strong>. An atlas cannot show every stream, pathway, house, or bend in the coastline. At smaller scales, some features disappear, curved boundaries simplify, and symbols exaggerate the size of important roads or cities so they remain legible. A line on a world map may represent a river tens or hundreds of metres wide yet take up a stroke equivalent to many kilometres at that scale. It is a symbol, not a scaled photograph.

Distance has multiple meanings. <strong>Euclidean distance</strong> is the straight-line distance between positions in a plane; a <strong>geodesic</strong> follows the surface; <strong>network distance</strong> follows actual roads, paths, rails or shipping lanes. <strong>Travel time</strong> adds speed, service frequency, congestion and delays. <strong>Economic distance</strong> adds cost and institutions. A paved tunnel may shrink a practical journey without changing its geometric length. A border crossing may make a neighbouring town feel further away than a more distant town reached by motorway. A useful distinction is between <em>proximity</em> (nearby on a map) and <em>accessibility</em> (easy to reach or use).

Imagine two villages separated by a ridge. They are 12 km apart in a straight line but the road runs around the mountain for 48 km. At an average speed of 40 km/h, the trip takes roughly 72 minutes; a new tunnel shortening the road to 18 km at an average 60 km/h would reduce that to about 18 minutes. The straight-line locations remain unchanged. Yet the practical geography of hospital access, commuting, and commerce has been transformed.

The lesson extends beyond transport. An internet connection, a regional language, a trade agreement, or remote work can reduce the felt distance between places. Geography is the study of where possibilities occur, not a claim that kilometres alone determine them.
