Human population is highly uneven. Some regions contain dense settlements across productive plains and connected urban corridors; other regions have very low population densities because of harsh climates, difficult terrain, limited infrastructure, or different land-use histories. Yet no single environmental factor explains the pattern. Some of the world's dense cities occupy seismic zones, floodplains, or coastal areas exposed to storms. People accept hazards when employment, trade, family networks, institutions, and infrastructure make a location attractive or difficult to leave.

<strong>Population density</strong> is the number of people divided by land area, often expressed as people per square kilometre. A fictional province with 750,000 residents over 15,000 square kilometres has an average density of 50 people per square kilometre. The number tells us little about whether 600,000 residents are concentrated in one city while the remaining area is sparsely inhabited. For interpretation, distinguish density, spatial distribution, age structure and actual accessibility. An average is a starting point, not a complete population map.

<strong>Urbanization</strong> is the growing share of a population living in towns and cities. Urban growth can arise from migration, natural population increase, administrative reclassification, or a mixture of these. Cities support <strong>agglomeration economies</strong>: firms and workers benefit from sharing infrastructure, suppliers, knowledge, labour markets, and customers. A large city also faces congestion, expensive housing, pollution, inequalities and infrastructural stress. The pattern is not “cities are good” or “cities are bad.” Concentration creates benefits and costs, and local policy helps decide who receives them.

The place where a city began may differ from the reasons it thrives now. River crossings, defensible hills and natural harbours often attracted early settlement. A modern financial centre may continue growing because of institutions, education, communication networks and cumulative investment long after its original military or trading advantage changed. This is <strong>path dependence</strong>: historical advantages can generate infrastructure and institutions that make a location important even when its initial advantage is no longer decisive.

<strong>Migration</strong> reshapes the human map through journeys motivated by employment, family, conflict, education, environmental pressures and other circumstances. Migration is rarely explained accurately by a single “push” or “pull.” Laws, language, savings, travel networks, discrimination, remittances and personal expectations change who can move and where they settle. The borders on a political map are therefore not simply walls between cultures; they intersect with languages, kinship, businesses, diasporas and histories that often cross them.

Urban growth also modifies local climate. Asphalt, buildings and limited vegetation can absorb and retain heat differently from parks or nearby countryside, contributing to an <strong>urban heat island</strong>. The intensity depends on weather, urban form, materials, vegetation and local geography. Planning for shade, ventilation, access to water and public transport is a geographical intervention as well as an engineering or political one.
