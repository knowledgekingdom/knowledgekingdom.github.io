Southeast Asia includes both mainland landscapes and many thousands of islands. Its physical geography varies dramatically: mountain ranges, major rivers such as the Mekong, monsoon-influenced lowlands, tropical forests, volcanic arcs, and busy coastal seas. Mainland river valleys have supported farming, towns and transport networks; island and coastal communities have long participated in maritime trade. The region is not one uniform landscape and cannot be understood through one national example.

The Strait of Malacca became a particularly important passage because it sits among routes connecting the Indian Ocean and maritime East Asia. But a narrow strait alone did not create commercial prosperity. Ports developed because traders, rulers, sailors, financiers and communities supplied the institutions, labour, ships and services needed for exchange. Commerce and political power reshaped one another. Geography concentrates potential traffic; social systems determine how commercial centres function.

Parts of island Southeast Asia lie within active tectonic regions, producing volcanic islands, earthquake hazards and fertile volcanic soils in some locations. High population densities can coincide with both productive agricultural land and serious hazards. Wet tropical environments do not eliminate dry-season water problems, and a long coastline does not automatically create equal port access. The same regional map reveals opportunities, exposure and inequality at once.

This case also shows why national borders are a limited guide to connectedness. Shipping routes and family networks can link communities separated by water, while roads may poorly connect neighbouring valleys on the mainland. Political geography and network geography overlap but never completely coincide.
