A country's natural resources are not simply a list of minerals. <strong>Soils</strong> are living, chemically active systems formed through interactions among rock, climate, organisms, topography and time. Their texture, nutrients, drainage, depth and organic matter affect farming. River floodplains may be productive where water and land management support cultivation; some tropical rainforest soils can be highly weathered and nutrient-poor despite lush vegetation, with much of the ecosystem's nutrients circulating in living biomass. A green landscape is not necessarily a naturally fertile field.

<strong>Biomes</strong> are broad ecological zones such as tropical rainforest, savanna, temperate grassland, desert, boreal forest and tundra. Climate plays a major role in their distribution, but fire, soils, topography, species interactions, and human land use also matter. The boundary of a biome is often a gradient rather than a line. Agricultural expansion, mining, roads and urbanization can fragment habitats and alter water cycles, while conservation and restoration can modify outcomes.

The ecological significance of a landscape may not match its immediate market value. A wetland can filter water, store floodwater, sustain fish and provide habitat. A forest can influence erosion, carbon storage, local rainfall processes and biodiversity. These contributions are sometimes called <strong>ecosystem services</strong>. The phrase is useful for economic discussion but does not imply that every ecological value is easily converted to money.

To understand farming, look for the combination of soils, growing season, rainfall or irrigation, land tenure, labour, markets and knowledge. Wheat production in a temperate plain, rice cultivation in a monsoon landscape, and pastoralism in a dry grassland are different strategies shaped by environment and history. No single crop or landscape produces the same society everywhere. Technology—from irrigation and fertiliser to cold storage and improved roads—can transform what is feasible, though it can also require energy, water, capital and careful management.

Finally, beware of a simplistic <strong>resource curse</strong> story. Rich oil or mineral deposits may create revenues and opportunities, but political outcomes depend on ownership structures, institutions, economic diversification, public accountability, external markets, and many other variables. Geography may help explain where a deposit is found. It does not tell us whether its benefits will be broadly shared.
