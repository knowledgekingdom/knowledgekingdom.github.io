The period usually called the Reign of Terror, especially in 1793–1794, involved emergency institutions, surveillance, trials, executions, and coercive measures meant by their proponents to defend the republic against enemies. The Committee of Public Safety became central to wartime government, while political factions accused one another of betrayal. The revolutionary state could also mobilize armies and reform administration, making its history more complex than a simple descent into chaos.

To explain the Terror, historians consider foreign war, internal rebellion, political culture, fear, social conflict, and decisions by leaders. No single cause accounts for every episode of violence. The fact that a government faces genuine threats does not by itself establish that every coercive action was necessary or proportionate. Historical explanation is not moral permission. We can investigate the mechanisms that made severe repression possible while also documenting those harmed by it.

The fall of Robespierre in July 1794, known as 9 Thermidor in the revolutionary calendar, marked a shift, but did not end violence or settle the question of government. The Directory that followed in 1795 sought stability amid continuing war and political struggle. In 1799 Napoleon Bonaparte's coup of 18 Brumaire inaugurated a new phase. The revolution altered institutions, law, society, and warfare, but it did not produce a permanently peaceful representative system.
