A large-scale uprising began in northern Saint-Domingue in August 1791. Plantations burned, fighting spread, and insurgents challenged the institution upon which colonial wealth depended. The timing and organization of the uprising are subjects of historical research; narratives of a single secret meeting should be assessed critically rather than treated as a fully verified explanation. What is clear is that the insurgency involved organized military action, local leadership, and a rapid transformation of imperial politics.

As different factions competed, France, Britain, and Spain sought influence in the colony. Insurgent leaders negotiated, switched alliances, and made tactical choices within a wider war. Their aims were neither identical nor frozen in time. For some participants, ending slavery was inseparable from self-government; for others, immediate security and favorable military alliances came first. Historians need to reconstruct these changing choices instead of assuming that eventual independence had been everyone's single objective from the beginning.

The revolt forced a practical reckoning with universal rights. A French lawmaker could now confront the possibility that maintaining colonial slavery required large-scale military repression and the rejection of principles celebrated at home. Enslaved combatants, rather than metropolitan thinkers alone, made emancipation an urgent political question.
