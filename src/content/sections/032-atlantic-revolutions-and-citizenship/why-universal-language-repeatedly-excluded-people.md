Universal language can serve several purposes. It can articulate a principle genuinely wider than existing law; it can mobilize supporters against a specific opponent; and it can confer legitimacy on a new ruling group. These purposes may coexist. Revolutionaries were often sincere in defending some form of liberty and equally sincere in resisting changes that threatened their property, household authority, or social status. The contradictions were not always overlooked; contemporaries challenged them vigorously.

Gender, race, property, religion, and legal status shaped political inclusion. A woman might participate in street politics but be denied a vote. A formerly enslaved person might gain legal freedom but face compulsory labor or racial discrimination. An Indigenous nation might be treated as foreign in treaty negotiations yet deprived of territory by a republic proclaiming consent. The boundaries of membership were contested through petitions, legal cases, warfare, organizing, and new written declarations.

This leads to a broad insight: rights are not only abstract sentences. They are sustained by definitions, institutions, enforcement, resources, and political power. An attractive constitution can coexist with unequal courts or economic coercion. Conversely, groups denied formal office may still force changes in law through organized pressure. The history of citizenship is partly the history of people demanding to be included in principles originally applied only to some.
