The First Continental Congress met in 1774 to coordinate colonial resistance. The first open clashes between British troops and colonial militias occurred at Lexington and Concord in April 1775. The conflict that followed involved militia networks, the Continental Army under George Washington, regional campaigns, and fierce disagreements about the legitimacy of rebellion. The Second Continental Congress became the coordinating political authority of the revolutionary coalition, though it initially lacked the resources of a fully consolidated state.

Revolutionaries needed to make independence thinkable. Thomas Paine's <em>Common Sense</em>, published in early 1776, attacked monarchy and argued for separation in accessible prose. Yet his pamphlet did not single-handedly cause independence. Military fighting, British decisions, local activism, existing debates, and events beyond any writer's control all mattered. Printed persuasion could accelerate a movement because it provided shared language for grievances already becoming politically organized.

War itself changed priorities. Moderates who once sought reconciliation faced pressure to choose sides. Communities supplied troops and food; governments borrowed money and issued paper currency; civilians endured confiscation and displacement. The American Revolution was therefore a struggle to establish new authority, not merely an argument about a government across the ocean. People had to decide which institutions they would obey and what sacrifices they would make.
