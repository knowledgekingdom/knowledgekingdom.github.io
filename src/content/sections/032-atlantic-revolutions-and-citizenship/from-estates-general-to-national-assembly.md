At Versailles, deputies disputed whether each estate should vote separately or whether representatives should vote by head. Voting by estate could allow the first two orders to outvote the much larger Third Estate. In June 1789, deputies of the Third Estate declared themselves a National Assembly; the Tennis Court Oath expressed their commitment to establish a constitutional order. Clergy and nobles also joined, and the monarchy's ability to control events weakened.

This was a revolution in the meaning of political representation. Representatives who had been summoned within an older structure asserted that they embodied the nation. But who had authorized them to speak for every inhabitant? What should happen when the king disagreed, or when Parisian crowds demanded more rapid change than deputies preferred? The practical answers emerged through confrontation rather than calm legal interpretation.

The National Assembly's claim also depended on events beyond its chamber. Food shortages, rumor, unrest in Paris, and concerns about troops around the capital made the crisis volatile. Social conflict was not secondary to philosophical thought; it altered which choices politicians believed were possible. Those who defended reform had to confront the prospect of popular mobilization, royal repression, or both.
