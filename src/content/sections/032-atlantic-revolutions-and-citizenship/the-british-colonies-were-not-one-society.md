The thirteen mainland colonies varied in religion, population, economy, and political institutions. New England ports depended on commerce, crafts, and maritime work; the Chesapeake and southern plantation regions depended heavily on enslaved labor; the middle colonies included diverse commercial and farming communities. Colonial assemblies already had traditions of local taxation and political bargaining, though suffrage and officeholding remained restricted in different ways. Colonists could understand themselves as loyal British subjects while resisting particular imperial policies.

Many inhabitants had no voice in the imperial dispute as it was framed by elite pamphleteers. Enslaved Africans and African Americans, Indigenous nations, women, indentured workers, and poor men confronted separate constraints. Some Black people pursued freedom by serving British or Patriot forces, and some enslaved people fled to British lines in response to wartime proclamations and opportunities. Native nations chose alliances or neutrality according to their own interests, often with few satisfactory options.

Because the colonies were diverse, independence was not unanimously desired. Loyalists remained attached to the Crown for reasons ranging from conviction and social ties to fear of disorder or revolutionary persecution. Patriots also disagreed about how radical a new society should become. Describing the conflict as merely 'Americans versus Britain' hides disputes among people living in the same towns, families, and regions.
