On 26 August 1789, the National Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. It proclaimed liberty, legal equality, security, resistance to oppression, and the principle that sovereignty resides in the nation. It also addressed law, public accountability, property, and the freedoms of expression and opinion under specified limits. The Declaration did not create a full operating constitution; it supplied principles by which institutions could be judged.

Its achievement and limits must be examined together. Legal equality undermined privilege by birth, but property remained protected and economic inequality remained extensive. The political nation was not automatically coextensive with all adults. Many women, servants, the poor, and colonial populations were excluded from effective participation. Moreover, revolutionary lawmakers disagreed about the relationship between a general declaration of rights and emergency measures said to defend the revolution.

Comparing the French and American declarations reveals a useful difference in emphasis. The American document primarily justified separation from Britain; the French declaration attempted to state norms for remaking the law and public authority within a state. Both used broad language, and both left fundamental contradictions unresolved. Their subsequent impact came partly from people not originally included who invoked those principles to press new claims.
