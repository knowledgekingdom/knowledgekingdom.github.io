Suppose you read a declaration promising legal equality. A good analysis begins by asking who authored it, when, under what pressure, and which audience it hoped to persuade. Then compare the text with actual laws concerning suffrage, slavery, religious minorities, women, and colonial subjects. Finally examine responses from groups whose interests the document neglected. A text becomes historically meaningful not because we endorse it, but because we can place its claims in the struggles that made and used it.

Try this comparison with three sources: the American Declaration of Independence (1776), the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789), and Olympe de Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen (1791). Ask which clauses define legitimate authority, which assign rights, and which assume a particular community of decision-makers. Compare a protester's interpretation with that of a lawmaker, and distinguish a contemporary document from later commentary.

A final test concerns absence. Why are the voices of enslaved workers, poor rural women, or Indigenous communities less visible in some formal archives? This may reflect unequal access to print, institutions, preservation, and the ability to leave written records. Historians can also use petitions, court records, military documents, oral traditions, demographic evidence, and material history. Missing a quotation from someone is not proof that they were politically inactive.
