The Atlantic revolutionary period did not end in 1804. Napoleonic intervention in Spain in 1808 created a crisis of monarchy and legitimacy across the Spanish empire. Local juntas and later independence movements debated whether authority reverted to the people when the monarch could not rule, and whether local communities could speak on behalf of entire territories. In the following decades, wars and political transformations produced new states across much of Spanish America. These developments will receive dedicated attention in Chapter 33.

There were no simple copies of Philadelphia or Paris. The population of Spanish America included Indigenous communities, enslaved and free African-descended people, American-born elites, imperial officials, and many regional societies with different institutions and interests. Some independence movements promised legal changes or abolition; others preserved hierarchies. Wars could create new claims for rights while worsening displacement and economic destruction. The language of citizenship became a field of struggle rather than an uncontested inheritance.

Elsewhere in the Atlantic world, Irish political movements, Britain's abolition campaigns, Caribbean resistance, and debates over colonial reform responded to the same era of war and political experiment. Their inclusion helps explain why 'Atlantic Revolutions' is a useful perspective, though it must not flatten differences or pretend that all participants were in direct contact with each other.
