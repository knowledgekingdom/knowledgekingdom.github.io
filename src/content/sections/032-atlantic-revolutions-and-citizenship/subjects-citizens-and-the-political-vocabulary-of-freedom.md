Before analyzing particular revolutions, distinguish several terms that political actors often blended. A <em>subject</em> owed allegiance to a monarch or other ruler. A <em>citizen</em> could be a member of a political community with specified legal protections, obligations, and sometimes participatory rights. <em>Sovereignty</em> means the ultimate authority to make binding political decisions. <em>Representation</em> concerns who speaks or decides for a wider group. <em>Equality before the law</em> differs from equal property, equal political influence, or freedom from enslavement.

The language of <em>natural rights</em> held that some human claims should not depend solely on a ruler's generosity. But which rights, for whom, and enforced by what institutions? Those questions were fiercely disputed. In North America, many colonists defended inherited English liberties while also using Enlightenment ideas about consent and resistance. In France, the argument expanded to rebuilding legal and social status. In Saint-Domingue, emancipation required abolishing ownership of human beings, not merely replacing a distant king with a representative assembly.

Citizenship also contains a practical problem. A revolutionary state needs taxes, armies, public trust, and methods for deciding disagreements. If it proclaims that the people are sovereign, what prevents a temporary majority or a leader who claims to embody the people from suppressing opponents? Revolutionary movements could generate constitutional checks, new participation, and abolition, but also exclusionary rules, emergency coercion, and political violence. There was no single inevitable direction.
