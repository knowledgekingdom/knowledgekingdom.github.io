Here is the chronological spine. After the Seven Years' War ended in 1763, disputes over taxation and imperial authority escalated in British North America. War began in 1775; the United States declared independence in 1776 and secured recognition in 1783. France entered a fiscal and constitutional crisis in 1789; monarchy gave way to republic, war and the Terror, and Napoleon's coup in 1799. In Saint-Domingue, a massive rebellion began in 1791, slavery was abolished in the colony and then by France, and Haiti declared independence in 1804.

These dates are anchors, not explanations. The American Revolution depended on colonial institutions, imperial policy, war, international aid, and social conflict. France's revolution grew from fiscal paralysis, unequal privilege, food insecurity, political representation, and war. Haiti's revolution arose within a brutal plantation system and was driven decisively by the organized struggle of enslaved people. Connections matter, but so do differences in who acted and what they demanded.

Leave this chapter with five questions you can ask of any revolution: What authority was challenged? Who mobilized, and how? Which rights were promised? Which groups were excluded or coerced? What changed in law, in daily life, and over generations? If you can answer those questions, you can recognize why 1776, 1789, and 1804 belong in one connected history without pretending they meant the same thing to everyone.
