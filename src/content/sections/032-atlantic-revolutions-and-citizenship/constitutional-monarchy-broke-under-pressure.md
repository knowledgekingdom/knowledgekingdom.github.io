By 1791 France had a constitutional monarchy, but trust had deteriorated. Louis XVI's attempted flight in June 1791 damaged confidence in the king's commitment to the revolution. The constitution distinguished active citizens with voting rights, linked partly to tax qualifications, from others excluded from direct political participation. Even within revolutionary institutions, sovereignty of the people did not mean identical political standing for all residents.

International war accelerated conflict. In April 1792 France declared war on Austria; the fighting soon spread and drew in other powers. Many revolutionaries believed war could defend or spread the new order, while opponents feared it would destroy the monarchy or open the way to foreign intervention. Military defeats, suspicions of treason, rising prices, and popular mobilization radicalized Parisian politics. In August 1792, an uprising overturned the monarchy in practice.

War did not make later radicalism inevitable. Leaders faced choices, and factions offered rival plans. Yet war created emergency conditions that intensified political polarization and made it easier to describe opponents as threats to the nation. The French case shows how an effort to secure liberty by limiting monarchy could become an argument about whether the monarchy should exist at all.
