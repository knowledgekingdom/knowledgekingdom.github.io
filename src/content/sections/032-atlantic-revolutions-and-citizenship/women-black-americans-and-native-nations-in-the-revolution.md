Women supported households and war efforts, organized boycotts, wrote political arguments, and sometimes acted directly in military or civic contexts. Abigail Adams urged her husband to 'remember the ladies' during the revolutionary constitutional moment, a pointed reminder that male political leaders could proclaim liberty while assuming male control over family law and government. Her words did not produce equal suffrage, but they illuminate debates already present within the revolutionary society.

Free and enslaved Black people pursued their own strategies. Some interpreted Patriot declarations as reasons to challenge slavery; others sought the British side because it could offer routes out of enslavement. Northern gradual abolition laws emerged in parts of the new United States, while slavery remained central in other regions. The process was uneven, and freedom in law did not ensure land, wages, or protection from discrimination. A purely white elite account misses people who deliberately used the war's upheaval to change their condition.

For Indigenous nations, the choice between British and American power often involved land and survival rather than abstract allegiance. The new republic claimed western territory whose inhabitants had not necessarily consented to join it. Treaties, settlement, coercion, and warfare reshaped Native political autonomy. Revolutionary ideals and settler expansion thus advanced together in ways that were deeply contradictory.
