The French colony of Saint-Domingue, occupying the western part of Hispaniola, was among the world's richest plantation zones in the late eighteenth century. Sugar and coffee exports depended on the forced labor of a large enslaved African population under extraordinarily violent conditions. Plantation wealth linked French merchants, European consumers, colonial officials, shipowners, and financiers. This economy connected the rhetoric of metropolitan liberty directly to the property claims of colonial enslavers.

Saint-Domingue also had complex internal social divisions. White planters disagreed with smaller white proprietors and officials over trade, local representation, and metropolitan control. Free people of color included property owners who demanded equal civil rights, sometimes while themselves owning enslaved people. The enslaved majority came from different African societies and developed networks, languages, religious practices, and forms of resistance in an intensely coercive environment. No one group can stand for the entire colonial population.

This matters for causation. The Haitian Revolution did not occur because all inhabitants suddenly accepted an idea imported from Paris. Colonial inequality, repeated resistance, African political and cultural experience, disputes among slaveholders and free people of color, and the instability of French imperial authority interacted. The French Revolution created opportunities and contradictions, but the people who took revolutionary action in Saint-Domingue shaped its most far-reaching outcome.
