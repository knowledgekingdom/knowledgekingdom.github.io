The revolutionary cause faced an enormous military imbalance. Britain possessed powerful naval resources, professional forces, and global reach. Colonial forces could use local knowledge and militia networks but struggled with financing, discipline, supply, and continuity. A victory at Saratoga in 1777 helped convince France that supporting the revolution might weaken Britain. France entered an alliance in 1778, supplying military, naval, and financial assistance. Spain entered the wider war against Britain in 1779, and other powers influenced the diplomatic setting.

The 1781 campaign culminating in the British surrender at Yorktown depended on coordination among American and French land forces and French naval strength. The Treaty of Paris of 1783 formally recognized American independence. The outcome was not therefore produced only by colonial virtue or British incompetence. It emerged from military endurance, alliance politics, Atlantic shipping, European calculations, and difficult decisions made by civilians and soldiers.

The resulting peace did not secure peace for everyone. Indigenous allies and communities faced heightened pressure from an expanding United States, and Loyalists experienced dispossession or migration. For Black people who sought freedom amid wartime upheaval, outcomes varied by place, status, and which army controlled a region. Diplomatic independence solved one fundamental conflict while leaving the country's internal structure of freedom deeply unsettled.
