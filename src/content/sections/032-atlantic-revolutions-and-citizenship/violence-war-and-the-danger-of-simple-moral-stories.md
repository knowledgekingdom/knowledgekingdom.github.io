The Atlantic Revolutions involved violence by governments, revolutionary armies, crowds, colonial enslavers, and insurgents. Some violence was directed against armed opponents; some deliberately targeted civilians or prisoners. Different conflicts followed different patterns, and casualty estimates vary in quality. The work of historians is to identify actors, choices, causes, and victims, rather than to turn the entire era into an image of either heroic liberation or murderous fanaticism.

Two mistakes are common. The first is <em>teleology</em>: reading the eventual outcome as though it had been certain from the start. The second is <em>moral flattening</em>: treating all violence as indistinguishable, or assuming that describing a cause excuses an action. We can recognize the emancipation struggle of enslaved Haitians as legitimate without ignoring the killing of noncombatants. We can study security fears during the French Terror without portraying emergency tribunals as inevitable or unaccountable.

Comparative study requires proportional evidence. A dramatic painting of a crowd, for example, reveals how an event was represented; it does not measure how many people participated or prove what every participant believed. A parliamentary declaration tells us what a government publicly claimed, not automatically what its soldiers practiced. Political violence must be analyzed through records, local histories, laws, and the social groups affected.
