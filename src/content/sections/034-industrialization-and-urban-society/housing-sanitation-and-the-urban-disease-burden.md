Early industrial cities often struggled with contaminated water, inadequate sewers and overcrowding. Human and animal waste could enter wells, rivers and streets. Cholera and typhoid epidemics exposed the danger of dense populations without dependable sanitation, while tuberculosis and other diseases were affected by living and working conditions. Contemporary people did not initially possess modern germ theory. Some physicians and reformers emphasized miasma, or harmful air, and sanitation campaigns could sometimes improve health even when their stated explanation for disease was incomplete.

John Snow investigated London cholera outbreaks and in 1854 connected a Soho outbreak to a particular water source. His work is important, but the familiar story that one doctor immediately solved public health is misleading. Evidence was disputed, water supplies were controlled by different organizations, and large sewerage systems required engineering, finance and political pressure. Edwin Chadwick and other reformers advocated changes, sometimes on different theoretical grounds. The sanitary movement developed through argument rather than unanimous scientific enlightenment.

Urban disease makes a strong lesson in collective action. A household can boil water, but if a whole city's supply is polluted, private precautions may be insufficient. Building sewers or preventing industrial dumping may benefit many residents who cannot individually pay for the system. As a result, urbanization pushed governments and private providers to invent new institutions for public health, and later improvements were among the conditions that made modern cities more livable.
