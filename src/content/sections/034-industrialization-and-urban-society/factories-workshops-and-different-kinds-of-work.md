The stereotype of everyone abandoning the countryside for a giant factory exaggerates the speed and uniformity of industrial change. Small workshops, home sewing, domestic service, construction, farming, mining and street commerce remained essential. Mechanized mills employed important populations, yet many goods still required human dexterity and small-scale specialization. Industrialization reshaped relationships between sectors rather than replacing all earlier occupations at once.

Factory work could produce regular wages, a community of coworkers and opportunities to gain technical skills. It could also entail repetitive labor, dangerous machinery, fines, insecure employment, crowded environments and strict supervision. Experience differed by occupation, age, sex, skill, region and economic conditions. A mechanic operating and repairing expensive equipment might possess bargaining power that an easily replaceable casual laborer lacked. Workers were neither uniformly powerless nor automatically liberated by wage employment.

The same machinery could reorganize household life. When adults, adolescents or children earned wages outside the home, decisions about schooling, marriage, childcare, rent and family budgets changed. Employers often recruited entire families or workers of particular ages and sexes according to assumptions about wages, dexterity and obedience. These arrangements were socially constructed and disputed, not natural consequences of machine design.
