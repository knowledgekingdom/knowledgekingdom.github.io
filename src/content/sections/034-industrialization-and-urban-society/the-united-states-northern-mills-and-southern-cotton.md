Industrial development in the United States combined northern manufacturing, expanding transport, western settlement and the slave-based plantation economy of the South. Early textile firms adapted British methods, sometimes through the knowledge of emigrant workers. Mills in New England grew alongside canals and trade networks. Lowell, Massachusetts, became a prominent planned textile city where corporations initially recruited many young women from rural households and regulated aspects of their housing and social lives. Their letters and organized protests complicate the stereotype of passive factory labor.

Elsewhere, workshops, small mills and mechanized production developed through different business systems. Tariffs, financial policy, canals, railroads and political conflict shaped industrial choices. The country's vast internal market offered opportunities, but geographic expansion was bound to dispossession of Indigenous peoples and conflict over land. The northern factory and the southern plantation were not economically separate: cotton cultivated under slavery supplied much of the raw material for textile manufacture and international export.

After the Civil War, rail networks, large corporations, steel and oil helped accelerate American industrialization. Yet this later development should not be compressed into the earlier Lowell period. The American case illustrates how industrial growth could coexist with incompatible labor systems, regional politics and struggles over citizenship. Mechanization did not itself decide the fate of slavery or equality; those outcomes were fought over through political institutions, social movements and war.
