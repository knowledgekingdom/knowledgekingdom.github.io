The long-term significance of industrialization lies in sustained improvements in how much can be produced with available labor and resources. Mechanized weaving, machine tools, improved transport and cheaper energy expanded output in many industries. Economists call changes in output per unit of input <em>productivity growth</em>. But production per worker is influenced by capital and energy inputs, not just the worker's individual effort or ability. A factory worker with a power loom is not morally more deserving than a skilled handloom weaver; the machinery and supporting institutions help explain the difference in recorded output.

The gains from productivity may reach people in several ways. Owners may receive profits, workers may earn higher wages, consumers may pay lower prices, and governments may collect more taxes. The division depends on competition, labor bargaining, investment, legal rights and public policy. A company with an effective monopoly might hold prices high. Employers with abundant labor and little competition may pay low wages even as production increases. Political reforms and collective organization can alter the eventual distribution.

A useful mental distinction is <em>possibility versus outcome</em>. Higher productivity makes a higher material standard of living possible across society; it does not guarantee that every group experiences it promptly or equally. The Industrial Revolution increased humanity's productive possibilities while making the struggle over their distribution a defining question of modern politics.
