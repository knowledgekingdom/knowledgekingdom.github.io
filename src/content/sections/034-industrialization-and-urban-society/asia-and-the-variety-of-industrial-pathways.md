Parts of Asia possessed large commercial and craft economies long before nineteenth-century European industrialization. The arrival of mechanized imports did not produce uniform outcomes. In British-ruled India, the production and sale of cotton textiles changed through world prices, colonial policy and local adaptation. Handloom weaving survived in many areas, sometimes through specialization and technological adjustments. Historians debate the timing and scale of employment decline in particular textile sectors, and it is wrong to treat all Indian industry as disappearing at once.

In Japan, the Meiji Restoration of 1868 was followed by state initiatives, educational change, imported knowledge and private enterprise that promoted industrial and military modernization. Silk reeling, textiles, shipbuilding and other sectors developed in overlapping phases. Japanese officials studied European and American models, but Japanese industrialization was neither a simple copy nor an isolated creation. Its success also involved rural household labor, export markets, state finance and growing imperial ambitions.

China's nineteenth-century experience was shaped by existing commercial networks, internal conflicts, imperial pressures and selective industrial initiatives. Modern arsenals, shipyards, mines and textile firms appeared without transforming the whole Qing economy on a British timetable. These comparisons show that industrialization is not one universal package: labor abundance, resource prices, state capacity, political sovereignty and world-market position could favor different choices.
