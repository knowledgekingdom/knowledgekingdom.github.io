Industrialization intensified urban growth, but cities were already expanding through commerce, administration and migration. Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, Liverpool and other British towns developed different industrial specializations; London remained an enormous commercial and political center. Migrants were drawn by wage opportunities, while changing rural livelihoods and population growth also contributed. Not all newcomers entered mills: transport, retail, domestic service, building, entertainment, food supply and government employed substantial urban populations.

Urban housing supply often struggled to keep pace with arrivals. Investors might construct crowded courts, terraces and cellar dwellings where workers could afford rent, while wealthier families occupied larger houses and districts with better services. New housing was not uniformly miserable, but rapid expansion could outstrip drainage, paving, clean water and waste removal. High rents and insecure jobs left some households vulnerable to eviction and overcrowding. Cities combined opportunities for anonymous individual advancement with spatial concentrations of poverty.

Industrial urbanization produced political questions about responsibilities once spread among households, local parishes and landowners. Who should pay for clean water, street lighting, sewers and schools? Should private companies provide these services or elected councils? Could residents who lacked property vote on local taxes? Urban government increasingly required collective infrastructure because an infectious disease outbreak or fire could threaten households beyond the poorest street. The city was not simply a by-product of the factory; it became a new arena of public policy.
