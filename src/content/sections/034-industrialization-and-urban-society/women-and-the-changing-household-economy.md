Industrialization did not mark the first time women worked. Women had always contributed heavily to agriculture, commerce, textiles, domestic production and family enterprises, although contemporary records frequently undervalued or hid that labor. The shift toward factories and urban wage work made some earnings more visible and gave some women new opportunities to live outside parental households, manage wages and participate in organized labor. It also exposed them to new forms of exploitation and surveillance.

Textile mills often employed many women and girls, while other industries remained heavily male. Employers sometimes justified paying women less by assuming that men were the primary family breadwinners; these assumptions could disadvantage female workers even when their wages supported entire households. Married women's legal rights to property, earnings and contracts differed across countries and changed during the nineteenth century. A young unmarried mill worker and a widow supporting children faced quite different choices.

Domestic work remained an enormous labor sector, particularly in expanding cities. Moreover, middle-class ideals of a separate 'home sphere' and 'work sphere' did not reflect the lives of all women. The history of industrialization requires examining unpaid cooking, cleaning, child-rearing and care, because factories depended on the daily reproduction of workers' ability to work. Counting paid factory wages without household labor gives an incomplete picture of production and welfare.
