The British experience provided models and machines, but industrialization elsewhere followed distinct pathways. Belgium drew on coal, metalworking and textile traditions. Parts of France mixed small workshops with modern factories and railway investment. In the German states, coalfields, technical education, commercial integration and eventually the Zollverein customs union supported growth. Railways, banks and state policies had varying importance, and political unification was not always a prerequisite for industrial change.

Industrial progress remained regionally uneven. A modern steel district could exist beside predominantly agricultural provinces. The Russian Empire expanded railways and some heavy industry in the nineteenth century while retaining major agrarian structures and, until 1861, serfdom. Later Russian industrialization involved substantial state and foreign investment. Southern and southeastern Europe generally industrialized later and differently, influenced by markets, state capacity, infrastructure and international trade rather than a simple absence of talent.

Maps showing nations with one color per level of development can conceal this internal variation. A country's capital city, mining zone and rural frontier may have had very different opportunities. Comparative history therefore asks which industry, which date, whose workers and which region, instead of ranking entire peoples on a single ladder of 'progress'.
