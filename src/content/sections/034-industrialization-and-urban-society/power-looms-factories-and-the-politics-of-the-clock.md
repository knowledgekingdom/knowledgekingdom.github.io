Edmund Cartwright patented an early power loom in the 1780s, but profitable, dependable mechanized weaving required later refinement. For decades, handloom weaving and power-loom factories coexisted, with different outcomes for different fabrics and districts. As the technology spread, handloom workers could face severe wage pressure even when aggregate textile production grew. This distinction between expanding output and falling earnings for a specific occupation is one of industrialization's recurring social dilemmas.

Factories were not merely buildings containing machines. Concentrating expensive equipment allowed owners to supervise quality, synchronize operations and keep power systems running. It also increased control over workers' schedules. A machine driven by a central waterwheel or steam engine created pressure for employees to begin together, remain at fixed stations and follow rules about breaks, noise and discipline. Employers sometimes imposed fines, and workers could resist through absenteeism, slowdowns, collective action or leaving for other jobs if alternatives existed.

The notion that industrialization created all time discipline is too simple; farms, ships, armies and religious institutions had long imposed demanding schedules. What changed was the combination of coordinated mechanical production, urban wage dependence and growing markets for precisely timed transport and deliveries. The clock became one of the factory's most consequential instruments, even though it produced no cotton by itself.
