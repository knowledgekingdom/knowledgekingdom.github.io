The nineteenth century brought new combinations of scientific knowledge, formal engineering and applied invention. Railways, telegraphs, chemicals, precision tools and later electrical systems depended on organized skills and institutions. Research was important, but the relationship between science and industry varied. Some early textile improvements came from practical mechanics and craftspeople rather than academic scientists; many later chemical and electrical industries drew more directly on laboratories and technical education.

From roughly the later nineteenth century, the expansion of steelmaking, chemicals, electric power and petroleum-based technologies changed the industrial landscape. Historians sometimes describe this as a 'Second Industrial Revolution', although the periodization is a teaching convenience rather than a sharp break. Industries overlapped: coal and steam continued to matter while electrical networks grew. Technologies spread only where investors, skilled labor, raw materials and public infrastructure made them practical.

A useful example is the electrical grid. An electric motor is valuable by itself, but its widespread effect requires generators, wiring, standards, regulators, maintenance and enough customers. The broader history therefore concerns technological <em>systems</em> rather than isolated gadgets. This is one reason industrialization became so transformative: later innovations could use the capital, cities, engineering skills and institutions established by earlier ones.
