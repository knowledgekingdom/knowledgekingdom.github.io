Cotton is a revealing starting point because it was never solely a British story. For centuries, Indian producers excelled in spinning, weaving, dyeing and finishing cotton textiles. European consumers bought Indian calicoes and other fabrics through merchants and chartered companies; British producers tried to imitate and compete with them. Consumers wanted textiles that were washable, comfortable, colorful and increasingly affordable. Merchants had incentives to reduce the costs of each stage of production.

Cotton processing posed a technical problem. Preparing fibers, spinning yarn and weaving cloth were connected operations. Improving one task could create a bottleneck in another. If looms began weaving faster, spinners had to produce more yarn; if spinning machines dramatically expanded thread supply, weaving capacity had to catch up. Industrial growth therefore happened through clusters of improvements rather than one isolated clever device.

The raw material came from different regions over time. By the nineteenth century, cotton harvested by enslaved labor in the United States became central to British textile manufacturing, though India, Egypt and other suppliers also mattered. This connection is indispensable: cheaper cotton cloth and profitable mills were linked to forced labor, violence and land dispossession across the Atlantic. The factory worker and the enslaved field laborer belonged to one global commercial system, despite living under profoundly different legal and labor regimes.
