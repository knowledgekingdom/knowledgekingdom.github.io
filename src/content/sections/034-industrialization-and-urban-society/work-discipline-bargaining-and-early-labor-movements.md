Many workers resisted aspects of industrial change. Skilled textile artisans sometimes feared machinery that threatened their bargaining power, and the Luddite disturbances in parts of Britain during 1811–1816 involved attacks on machines in the context of insecure livelihoods and contested labor practices. It is misleading to treat every participant as irrationally hostile to technology. Some demanded fair work, a livelihood or regulation; the historical state treated machine-breaking harshly, especially during wartime.

Workers used petitions, mutual aid, strikes, demonstrations and associations to press claims about wages, hours and conditions. Governments restricted collective organization in some periods and allowed it under different legal arrangements later. British Combination Acts at the turn of the nineteenth century were repealed in 1824, followed by further restrictions in 1825. Trade unions did not arise fully formed; they developed from local craft bodies, friendly societies, political movements and industrial associations.

The struggle over work also became a struggle over representation. Chartists in Britain demanded political reforms, including expanded male suffrage and parliamentary changes, partly because working people faced decisions imposed by institutions they could not easily influence. Labor politics therefore connects factory discipline to citizenship and constitutional development. Economic bargaining and democratic reform were related but not identical projects, and workers disagreed among themselves about tactics, priorities and political visions.
