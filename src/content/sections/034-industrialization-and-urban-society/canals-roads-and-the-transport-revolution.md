Before railways became powerful, improving transport was already important to industrial change. Britain built and expanded turnpike roads and canals, allowing heavy goods such as coal, building materials and raw cotton to travel more cheaply between mines, towns, ports and factories. The Bridgewater Canal, opened in the 1760s, is a frequently cited example of a commercial waterway serving the Manchester coal trade. Waterways had limitations: they required construction capital, maintenance, water supplies and suitable routes, and could be disrupted by freezing or low water.

Transport costs shape the economic geography of industry. A coalfield may contain abundant energy, but if moving fuel to a distant town costs more than the fuel itself, its usefulness is limited. A canal can transform the profitability of a mine without improving the mineral deposit. Better roads can enlarge a farmer's market and make distant urban customers worth serving. Transport improvement is therefore not simply a consequence of industrialization; it can be one of its enabling conditions.

Historians should distinguish infrastructure built for different purposes. An estate road, a military highway, a toll canal and a public railway have different financing, user charges and political effects. The most useful question is not which form was 'modern' but who paid to build it, which goods and people benefited, what alternative routes existed, and whether the system made production more competitive.
