Coal-fired industry caused severe local environmental damage. Smoke, soot and sulfur compounds affected buildings and lungs. Mines altered landscapes and waterways, while chemical production and metalworking generated contaminated waste. River pollution came from industrial discharge as well as insufficient urban sanitation. The benefits of industry and its environmental costs rarely fell equally on the same people. Wealthier families could sometimes move away from the most polluted districts; workers often lived close to the factories employing them.

Industrialization also expanded the human use of fossil fuels, creating a long-term source of carbon dioxide that contributes to global warming. Early industrialists did not understand the modern climate system in the way contemporary science does, but present-day historians can identify the physical consequences without attributing modern knowledge to eighteenth-century actors. The historical question is how energy choices became locked into infrastructure, finance and consumer habits.

It is tempting to compare a factory's profits with only its immediate costs. A fuller social accounting includes damage to health, water and air—costs often borne by people who never agreed to pay them. Economists later described such uncompensated consequences as <em>externalities</em>. The concept clarifies why private incentives and collective welfare can diverge, and why technological progress does not automatically solve the problems created by its previous stages.
