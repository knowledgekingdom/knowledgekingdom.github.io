Industrializing societies had reasons to expand literacy and numeracy, but the relationship between factories and education was complicated. Some machinery reduced the need for particular artisanal skills while increasing demand for mechanics, managers, engineers and clerks. Urban employers needed invoices, accounts and schedules. Newspapers, pamphlets, scientific clubs, religious movements and labor organizations created audiences for written information. Education was also a moral and political project pursued by churches, reform associations and governments.

Not all early factories wanted school attendance if it reduced children's hours at work. Legislation gradually incorporated schooling requirements into restrictions on child employment, and national systems of mass education developed at different speeds. Britain's Elementary Education Act of 1870 expanded the institutional basis for publicly supported schooling in England and Wales; later legislation affected attendance. Continental European states pursued different paths. More formal schooling did not instantly eliminate child work or guarantee equality of opportunity.

Literacy also changed political participation. The cheapening of printed material helped people share grievances and organize movements across cities. Yet publications could spread propaganda, stereotypes and misinformation as readily as useful knowledge. Industrial technology therefore amplified the scale of both public enlightenment and manipulation, connecting material economic change with the intellectual transformations studied in earlier chapters.
