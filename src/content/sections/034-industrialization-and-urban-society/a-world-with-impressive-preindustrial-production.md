Before large mechanized mills spread, European and Asian societies already possessed sophisticated industries. Indian cotton producers made fabrics prized across the Indian Ocean and Atlantic worlds. Chinese manufacturers produced silk, ceramics, metal goods and paper on great scales. European households and workshops spun, wove, smelted iron and built ships. Water-powered mills, mine pumps and elaborate clocks demonstrate that machines did not begin in Britain around 1760. Nor did capitalism, wage labor, banks, long-distance trade or urban poverty suddenly emerge with steam power.

A merchant commissioning cloth might distribute cotton or wool to households that spun and wove it at home. Historians often call this a <em>putting-out system</em> or <em>proto-industrial</em> production. It gave some rural families an income beyond farming and connected distant suppliers, merchants and consumers. Yet household production could be hard to coordinate when every stage depended on dispersed labor, seasonal obligations and varying tools. New machinery and centralized power could eventually lower certain costs, though large factories were not always cheaper in every location or period.

Seeing what existed before industrialization avoids a familiar error: contrasting a supposedly static, technologically ignorant past with an inventive modern West. The more meaningful distinction is the scale and persistence of change. Earlier societies made advances, but eighteenth- and nineteenth-century industrializers increasingly combined innovations with investment and fossil energy in ways that made further mechanization profitable and reproducible.
