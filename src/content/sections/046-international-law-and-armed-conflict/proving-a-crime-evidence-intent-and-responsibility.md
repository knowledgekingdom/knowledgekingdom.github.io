An international criminal proceeding requires more than the assertion that a horrible event took place. Investigators must establish what happened, identify the persons involved and demonstrate the elements required by the alleged offense. Evidence may include documents, testimony, forensic findings, images and authenticated communications. Each has limitations. A witness may accurately describe an event without knowing the wider policy behind it; an official document may express an intention without establishing that it was carried out. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-43" class="cite">43</a>\]

The Rome Statute distinguishes modes of individual responsibility, mental elements and responsibility of commanders or other superiors. Command responsibility is not automatic guilt based on rank. It concerns specified relationships of effective control, knowledge standards and failures to take required measures in relation to crimes committed by subordinates. The applicable standards differ for military commanders and other superiors. Superior orders do not supply a blanket defense, and the Statute places stringent conditions on any such claim. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-43" class="cite">43</a>\]

At trial before the ICC, conviction requires proof beyond reasonable doubt. Earlier procedural decisions apply different standards. An arrest warrant, confirmation of charges and conviction are therefore not equivalent events. Nor does an acquittal necessarily prove that no crime occurred; it may mean that the prosecution failed to prove the accused's responsibility to the required standard. Fair procedures are especially important when public anger is intense. A legal system that sacrifices proof because it dislikes an accused person weakens the credibility of its judgments against anyone.
