Technological change does not itself create a legal vacuum. Digital operations during armed conflicts may fall within the existing humanitarian framework. A civilian hospital, for example, depends on software, communications and records as well as a building. The loss of digital services can cause serious humanitarian consequences. Important interpretive debates remain over when certain non-destructive cyber disruptions constitute attacks under humanitarian law and how civilian data should be protected. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-51" class="cite">51</a>\]

Autonomous systems raise related questions about accountability, predictable functioning and human control. The legal obligations are not transferred to a machine simply because software contributes to a decision. A system may produce an apparently precise output while relying on incomplete information or flawed assumptions. Chapter 6's warning about measurement and Chapter 7's discussion of feedback loops apply to this problem. Novel technology should be examined through existing legal rules, as well as through debates about whether additional rules are necessary.

Weapons and arms transfers can also be governed by distinct treaties. The 2013 Arms Trade Treaty establishes obligations for its parties concerning specified international transfers, including prohibitions and export-risk assessment. The legal status of a transfer depends on the applicable instrument, the relevant risk and other circumstances, not merely on whether a supplier is politically aligned with the recipient. Arms-control and disarmament regimes have different membership and scope. We shall return to nuclear deterrence and the legal debates surrounding nuclear weapons in Chapter 47. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-52" class="cite">52</a>\]
