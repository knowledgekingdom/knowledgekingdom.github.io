Sovereignty means that states possess their own political and legal authority and enjoy juridical equality in international relations. It does not mean that each government is entitled to do whatever it chooses. A smaller state's legal personality is not proportional to its population, military capacity or wealth. The principle of sovereign equality protects independence precisely because international power is so uneven. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]

Consent plays a central role in making treaties. Under the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, agreements in force are to be performed in good faith. A government cannot normally escape an international treaty obligation simply by passing contradictory domestic legislation. Some rules are also regarded as peremptory norms of general international law, or jus cogens, from which derogation is not permitted. A treaty conflicting with such a norm is void. Identifying the precise scope of a claimed peremptory norm is a legal inquiry, not a way of labeling every important preference as absolute. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]

Imagine a new government elected on a promise to end an expensive cross-border water agreement. It may possess the democratic mandate to seek renegotiation. It may have an agreed right of withdrawal. But until the applicable procedures are satisfied, its internal mandate does not by itself end its international obligations. Political independence and the capacity to undertake durable commitments are complementary. The same principle matters when governments change during or after a conflict.
