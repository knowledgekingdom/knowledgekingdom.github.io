It is tempting to imagine two simple worlds. In one, international law determines how states act; in the other, military and economic power determine everything. Neither picture describes the actual relationship very well. A government may comply because officials believe a rule is legitimate, because domestic courts enforce it, because other states demand compliance, or because repeated cooperation is valuable. These mechanisms can reinforce one another. A rule can influence an institution without being its only motive. The ICRC’s empirical Roots of Restraint study examined the social processes through which norms become embedded in armed organizations. Its implication for general readers is that written law and institutional culture should not be treated as rival explanations: both can matter to whether people adhere to a rule under pressure. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-59" class="cite">59</a>\]

Power can also obstruct accountability. Vetoes, selective investigations, diplomatic pressure, weak domestic institutions and lack of cooperation can leave grave wrongdoing without an effective remedy. International tribunals depend on evidence and institutional support. Humanitarian organizations depend on the willingness of parties to provide access. The existence of law does not guarantee that the people most in need of protection will receive it. Conversely, selective enforcement is a reason to demand fairer implementation, not a sound reason to declare that no one is bound.

When reading a judgment or official report, ask what it establishes and what remains uncertain. When comparing two conflicts, apply the same legal categories while recognizing factual differences. When assessing an allegation, separate moral urgency from proof of the particular legal elements. Most importantly, remember the hospital with which we began: the injured person's claim to humane treatment does not depend on a verdict about the political cause. The law may be imperfect, but its insistence that certain protections survive hostility is one of the most important achievements of the international legal order.
