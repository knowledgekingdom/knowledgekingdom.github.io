A war changes the relationship between food, water, energy and public health. Interruptions to ordinary trade can raise prices dramatically. Families may lose their income at the same time that the things they need become scarce. Malnutrition makes diseases more dangerous, while damaged or overstretched sanitation systems increase the risk of infection. These connections were already visible in Chapter 44's discussion of logistics. They become legal questions when the civilian population's basic survival is threatened.

Humanitarian law prohibits using starvation of civilians as a method of warfare. It also protects objects indispensable to civilian survival under the applicable rules. The legal protection extends beyond the people visibly injured by fighting. The fact that a person dies from a preventable disease rather than from direct violence does not make the collapse of essential services irrelevant. Restrictions on access to necessities must be examined under the relevant obligations, including those applicable to occupied territories. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-31" class="cite">31</a>\]

A reader should distinguish a description of a humanitarian disaster from a particular allegation of criminal intent. Reports of extreme scarcity can be true and urgently important even when investigators have not yet established the elements of a charged crime. At the same time, an absence of final judicial findings does not relieve parties of their present duties to protect civilians. Evidence about access, administrative decisions, the availability of supplies and the changing circumstances of communities helps explain the crisis; no single statistic captures the whole legal or human picture.
