In 1984 Nicaragua brought a case against the United States before the ICJ concerning military and paramilitary activities. The United States disputed jurisdiction and subsequently declined to participate in the merits phase. The Court nonetheless proceeded under its Statute and in June 1986 found breaches of obligations including non-intervention and the prohibition on the use of force. It rejected the justification of collective self-defence advanced by the United States. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

The case is particularly valuable because it demonstrates how treaty and customary rules can coexist. Jurisdictional limitations affecting particular multilateral treaty claims did not prevent the Court from applying independently existing customary rules. Legal sources are therefore more than a hierarchy of written documents. The existence of an obligation, the source of the obligation and the jurisdiction of a particular court must be considered separately.

The Court also held that the United States owed reparation, but the case illustrates the obstacles to enforcing judgments against a major power. Nicaragua later discontinued the reparations proceedings. Do not infer either that the judgment was legally meaningless or that it achieved everything judicial accountability was meant to achieve. Its doctrinal influence survived the political limits of enforcement. The case also offers an important corrective to a selective view of law: principles concerning the use of force apply to powerful Western states just as they apply to other governments. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]
