War can disrupt education, nutrition, family life and the development of children even when they are far from fighting. Children are entitled to special respect and protection under multiple international instruments. Rules restrict their recruitment and use in hostilities; the applicable age thresholds differ across instruments and contexts. Under the Rome Statute, enlisting or conscripting children under fifteen or using them to participate actively in hostilities may constitute a war crime, as demonstrated by the ICC's 2012 Lubanga judgment. Other instruments establish higher standards in certain circumstances. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-34" class="cite">34</a>\]

Consider a child associated with an armed group. A community may view the child solely as a threat, while a humanitarian worker sees a person who has experienced coercion, deprivation or abuse. The questions of responsibility, age, rehabilitation and protection must be examined carefully rather than reduced to a permanent identity. The protection of children is not merely protection from death: it also concerns the possibility of recovering education, social relations and an ordinary future.

War also separates families and leaves people missing. Parties have duties under applicable rules to search for and account for missing people, handle remains with respect, and provide information to relatives. Such work may continue decades after hostilities cease. A name in a register can become evidence of a legal claim, a family's first reliable knowledge of what happened, or a necessary condition for inheritance and civil status. The bureaucratic labor of humanitarian protection is easy to overlook precisely because its successes are often quiet. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-35" class="cite">35</a>\]
