Military necessity is a concept within humanitarian law, not a general permission to set humanitarian rules aside. The law attempts to accommodate the realities of conflict while imposing boundaries grounded in humanity. Some protections are categorical: torture, for example, cannot be justified by invoking a military emergency. Other rules incorporate circumstances, feasibility or specific exceptions into their own wording. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]

Consider a fictional detainee whom officials believe possesses information that could prevent future violence. A commentator argues that the urgency of the situation makes torture acceptable. The argument confuses a claim about usefulness with a legal exception that does not exist. The international prohibition of torture remains applicable in emergencies. Similar reasoning explains why punishment of families for the alleged conduct of a relative is incompatible with the prohibition of collective punishment.

A second mistake is to suppose that humane conduct is an optional reward for the enemy's compliance. Humanitarian law is not a sporting contest in which one team's violation suspends the rules for the other. The inability to prevent every breach is not a reason to weaken the standard. Institutions that normalize exceptional cruelty risk extending it into ordinary policing and detention. The costs therefore reach beyond the immediate victims, reshaping the relationship between state power and human dignity long after the fighting ends.
