International human rights law protects individuals in relation to public authority, while humanitarian law specifically regulates armed conflict. Both may apply during the same period. Their relationship can involve questions of territorial or extraterritorial jurisdiction, which rules are more specific to the circumstances, and the effect of treaty-specific limitations or derogations. Courts and scholars have disagreed about aspects of this relationship. It is not accurately described as an automatic replacement of human rights law by the law of war. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-39" class="cite">39</a>\]

The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights permits certain limited derogations during an officially proclaimed public emergency threatening the life of the nation. The measures must satisfy stringent requirements and some rights cannot be derogated from. Among them is the prohibition of torture. A government cannot declare an emergency and thereby escape all constraints. Moreover, derogation under one treaty does not alter independent obligations under other applicable law. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-40" class="cite">40</a>\]

Imagine a person detained far from active hostilities during a period of war. The authority may invoke national security. A lawyer must ask about the legal basis for detention, applicable procedural protections, treatment, the relationship to the conflict and the state's jurisdiction. It is not enough to assert that the country is at war. The legal regimes protect overlapping but not identical interests. Humanitarian law's application can help clarify a wartime situation, while human rights law may provide additional protections and routes to accountability.
