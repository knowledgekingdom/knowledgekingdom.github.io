After the Second World War, the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals prosecuted individuals for crimes connected with wartime conduct and aggression under their respective charters. The Nuremberg principles affirmed that individuals could bear responsibility under international law even when acting in official capacities. The tribunals were historically significant, but they also faced criticisms concerning selectivity, the structure of the proceedings and the relationship between previously existing law and new formulations of criminal responsibility. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-42" class="cite">42</a>\]

The point of individual criminal responsibility is to identify the person whose conduct and mental state satisfy an offense, not to attach collective guilt to a nation. A government official cannot simply claim that everything done on behalf of the state is immune from scrutiny. At the same time, an ordinary citizen cannot be declared guilty merely because the state committed a wrongful act. Responsibility must be established through applicable law and evidence.

The modern international criminal justice system includes national courts, historically specific international tribunals, hybrid courts and the permanent International Criminal Court. Each has a particular legal foundation and jurisdiction. The ICTY and ICTR, established in the 1990s, helped develop international criminal jurisprudence concerning the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda. The creation of institutions does not undo the harm already suffered, but it can recognize individual victims, establish an evidentiary record and challenge official attempts to deny crimes.
