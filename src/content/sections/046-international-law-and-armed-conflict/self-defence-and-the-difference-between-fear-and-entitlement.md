Article 51 preserves individual and collective self-defence in response to an armed attack. Measures taken in self-defence must be reported to the Security Council. Customary international law also requires necessity and proportionality: the defensive use of force must be justified by the need to respond to the attack and limited by that defensive purpose. This is a different proportionality test from the humanitarian-law rule discussed later. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

The International Court of Justice's 1986 Nicaragua judgment rejected the United States' asserted collective self-defence justification and examined the distinction between an armed attack and other unlawful conduct. It emphasized that collective self-defence is not simply a license for a third state to intervene whenever it declares that another state needs help. A request by the state claiming to be the victim of an armed attack matters. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

Disagreement remains over anticipatory self-defence before an attack occurs, and over the use of force against non-state armed groups operating in another state without its consent. Some governments and scholars accept limited grounds in particular circumstances; others reject or contest such theories. These debates must not be converted into a general right to wage preventive war against a speculative future danger. A government may sincerely fear a rival yet lack a lawful basis for using force. Chapter 45's security dilemma helps explain fear; it does not turn fear itself into a legal authorization.
