International law is implemented through national institutions as well as international courts. Article 5(4) of Bulgaria's Constitution provides that treaties ratified according to the constitutional procedure, promulgated and in force for Bulgaria form part of domestic law and take precedence over conflicting ordinary domestic legislation. Article 84 gives the National Assembly responsibilities including decisions on war and peace, while other constitutional provisions assign roles to the President and Council of Ministers. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-53" class="cite">53</a>\]

This is the institutional side of a topic sometimes discussed only in terms of diplomacy. Ratifying a treaty is not the end of implementation. Authorities may need legislation, trained investigators, judicial procedures, education, administrative coordination and resources. Military personnel and officials need to understand applicable humanitarian obligations before a crisis rather than learning them only after a violation. Domestic courts can be important sites of accountability, depending on national jurisdiction and the applicable legal framework. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-61" class="cite">61</a>\]

Bulgaria's participation in the UN, NATO and European Union does not merge these bodies into one legal institution. Their powers and commitments differ. An alliance decision cannot by itself erase the Charter or humanitarian-law obligations of the states involved. Nor does a disagreement with an international institution automatically end a treaty commitment. The constitutional example helps show why international law is not exclusively a matter for great powers or distant judges. It shapes the duties of legislators, public servants, border officials and courts in smaller countries too.
