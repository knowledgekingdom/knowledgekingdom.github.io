One of the most important achievements of contemporary humanitarian law is the requirement to distinguish civilians from combatants and civilian objects from military objectives in the conduct of hostilities. Civilians must not be directly attacked unless, and for such time as, they directly participate in hostilities. Membership and status questions in non-international armed conflict are more complex and contested, including the treatment of members of organized armed groups. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]

Political sympathy is not itself direct participation. A citizen who supports a government, pays taxes, reads its newspapers or has a relative in the armed forces does not thereby lose civilian protection. The fact that a population contributes to its society's economy cannot justify treating all of that society as a fighting force. Collective identities are especially dangerous shortcuts: nationality, religion, language and family membership do not substitute for the applicable legal categories.

There are legal rules concerning civilian objects and military objectives; in difficult cases their application is fact-specific. For the general reader, the important point is that neither an object's location in a conflict zone nor an official label alone establishes its legal status. A claim about the use of a civilian building must be supported by evidence and assessed under the law. This chapter is about understanding obligations and protecting people, not about selecting or evaluating real-world targets.
