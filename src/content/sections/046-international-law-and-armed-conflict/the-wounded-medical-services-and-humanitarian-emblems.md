Medical treatment is a particularly clear example of the distinction between military allegiance and humanitarian need. The wounded and sick who are no longer participating in hostilities must be respected and cared for. Medical personnel, facilities and transports receive special protection under the applicable rules. Caring for injured members of an adverse party is part of the medical function, not evidence that a hospital has abandoned its humanitarian role. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-27" class="cite">27</a>\]

The protections of medical facilities are legally defined rather than merely symbolic. Allegations that a facility has been used for acts harmful to the enemy outside its humanitarian function require careful factual assessment; special protection does not disappear merely because a belligerent makes an accusation. The applicable treaty safeguards, including warning requirements where relevant, and other humanitarian obligations remain important. The rules must not be confused with a general exemption from scrutiny or a justification for harming patients and staff. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-27" class="cite">27</a>\]

The protective emblems of the red cross, red crescent and red crystal communicate a humanitarian status under the relevant legal conditions. Their meaning is distinct from religious or national allegiance. Respect for medical care also requires attention to indirect consequences: interruption of ordinary medical services can threaten childbirth, chronic illness, vaccination and emergency care. A society's health system is an interconnected institution. Protecting only the physical structure while ignoring the people and services within it would miss the humanitarian purpose.
