War has never been wholly outside moral or legal reflection. Across different societies, writers, religious authorities and rulers discussed the treatment of prisoners, the protection of noncombatants, the keeping of promises and the circumstances under which war could be justified. These traditions were neither identical nor consistently respected. European just-war thought, for example, developed alongside practices of conquest and exclusion. Muslim jurists elaborated rules about armed conflict within different schools of Islamic law; interpretations varied across periods and circumstances. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]

It would be misleading to identify a single civilization as the inventor of compassion. It would be equally misleading to present older customs as if they were identical to the Geneva Conventions. Some historical protections were reserved for people of particular status, faith or allegiance. A modern treaty, by contrast, creates obligations expressed in negotiated legal categories. The ICRC's comparative work with Islamic legal scholars illustrates how different traditions can support common humanitarian purposes without being interchangeable.

The nineteenth-century development of international humanitarian treaties took place amid imperialism and industrialization. In the twentieth century, newly independent states participated in their further elaboration. The history matters because it counters the idea that humanitarian restraint is merely a favor granted by a powerful society to less powerful societies. It is a shared legal undertaking, repeatedly developed through political negotiations and the experience of people affected by violence.
