War threatens more than the bodies of people currently alive. Libraries, religious buildings, archaeological sites, art and archives connect generations. In 1954 states adopted the Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict. The framework requires safeguarding and respect for defined cultural property, with specific rules and carefully limited exceptions. UNESCO's work illustrates why cultural loss is not adequately represented by the replacement cost of a structure. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-36" class="cite">36</a>\]

In 2016 the International Criminal Court convicted Ahmad Al Faqi Al Mahdi for a war crime concerning protected historic and religious monuments in Timbuktu, Mali. The case showed that deliberate damage to a community's heritage can be the subject of individual criminal responsibility. It also showed the importance of the precise charge: the court addressed specific conduct within its jurisdiction rather than passing a verdict on every historical injustice suffered by the community. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-37" class="cite">37</a>\]

The natural environment similarly sustains civilian life. Damaged soils, contaminated water and destroyed ecosystems can impair livelihoods long after a conflict ends. The ICRC's 2020 environmental guidelines collect applicable rules and recommendations, including general humanitarian protections and particular environmental provisions. Not all environmental damage is automatically a war crime, and the legal thresholds differ. But the delayed and dispersed nature of damage does not make it legally or morally irrelevant. Chapter 85 will examine the scientific side of these ecological relationships. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-38" class="cite">38</a>\]
