In 1998 the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda convicted Jean-Paul Akayesu of genocide and crimes against humanity. The judgment was a landmark in the application of the Genocide Convention's criminal concept by an international court, including recognition of the circumstances in which sexual violence can constitute genocidal conduct. It concerned the accused's individual responsibility, not the collective guilt of a population. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-46" class="cite">46</a>\]

In 2007 the ICJ decided Bosnia and Herzegovina's case against Serbia under the Genocide Convention. It found that genocide had occurred at Srebrenica, but did not find Serbia responsible for committing genocide or complicit in it under the legal standards applied. It did find breaches of Serbia's obligations to prevent genocide and punish perpetrators, including its duty of cooperation with the ICTY. This is a distinction between attribution of the underlying acts and responsibility for a state's independent obligations. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-47" class="cite">47</a>\]

The two proceedings illustrate why a legal judgment cannot be accurately summarized by saying merely that a court found someone guilty of genocide. Which court? Which person or state? Which conduct? Which duty? Which evidentiary standard? The duty to prevent genocide is especially important because international law would offer inadequate protection if it only addressed crimes after completion. Prevention, however, requires institutions willing and able to act on credible evidence of serious danger. A judicial ruling cannot retrospectively supply the protection that was absent when people needed it.
