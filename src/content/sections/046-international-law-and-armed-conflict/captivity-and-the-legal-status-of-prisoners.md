The Third Geneva Convention provides a detailed framework for prisoners of war in international armed conflict, including conditions of detention, humane treatment, communication, medical care and repatriation. POW status is a legal category defined by the Convention, not a medal for moral virtue. Where applicable, doubt about entitlement requires the procedure provided by the relevant rules. Detention can prevent a combatant from returning to hostilities; it is not itself a license to punish someone for lawful participation in war. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

In non-international conflicts, fighters do not automatically receive the same POW status or combatant immunity under international humanitarian law. They can face domestic prosecution for participation in hostilities, depending on applicable law. Nonetheless, Common Article 3 and other relevant rules prohibit torture, cruel treatment and sentences without fundamental judicial safeguards. No one becomes legally unprotected because officials attach the term terrorist, rebel or unlawful combatant to them. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

Consider the family of a prisoner who has received no information for months. Their distress is independent of whether the prisoner was a soldier or a civilian. Rules on registration, family contacts and the accounting for missing people are therefore more than administrative technicalities. They respond to one of war's deepest social injuries: uncertainty over whether a loved one is alive. Humanitarian work must pay attention to those indirect victims as well as people physically in custody.
