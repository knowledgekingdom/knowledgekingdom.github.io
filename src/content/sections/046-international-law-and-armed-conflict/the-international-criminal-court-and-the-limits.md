The International Criminal Court (ICC), created by the 1998 Rome Statute and functioning since 2002, prosecutes natural persons rather than states. Its subject matter includes genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and, under a special jurisdictional regime, the crime of aggression. It cannot prosecute every serious violation in every country at every point in history. Its competence is limited by the Statute's rules on time, territory, nationality, referral and other conditions. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-45" class="cite">45</a>\]

For the first three core crimes, jurisdiction can arise through relevant territorial or nationality links to a state party, an eligible non-party state's acceptance of jurisdiction, or a Security Council referral, subject to the applicable requirements. Non-membership of an accused person's state of nationality therefore does not necessarily mean the court has no jurisdiction. The aggression amendments impose distinct constraints that must be examined separately. A jurisdictional conclusion is not a judgment on guilt.

Complementarity is the principle that the court supplements rather than generally displaces national criminal jurisdictions. A case may be inadmissible if a competent state is genuinely investigating or prosecuting it, subject to the Statute's specific conditions. Gravity and other admissibility requirements also matter. The ICC does not possess a global police force. Arrests, access to evidence and enforcement of sentences depend heavily on cooperation. A warrant can be legally significant while remaining unexecuted. That gap is one of the clearest examples of the difference between judicial authority and practical enforcement capacity.
