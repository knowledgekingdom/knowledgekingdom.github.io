The question whether armed force may be used to protect populations from mass atrocities has generated enduring controversy. The moral case for stopping atrocities can be compelling. The separate legal issue concerns who may authorize coercive military action and under what conditions. A unilateral humanitarian exception to the Charter prohibition is not generally accepted as an established rule. Some scholars and governments defend narrower doctrines; many others regard them as dangerous departures from the Charter. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

In 2005 UN members endorsed the responsibility to protect populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity. The agreed World Summit language begins with the state's own responsibility, emphasizes international assistance and peaceful means, and envisages collective action through the Security Council in accordance with the Charter when peaceful means are inadequate and national authorities manifestly fail to protect populations. It is not an unlimited permission for unilateral war. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

The difficulty is real. A strict system of collective authorization may be obstructed by vetoes when civilians urgently require protection. An expansive unilateral doctrine may provide a pretext for domination. Neither problem disappears merely by invoking noble motives. A serious evaluation should distinguish the humanitarian purpose, the legal authority, the likely effects on civilians and the safeguards against abuse. Diplomacy, prevention, protection, refugee assistance and criminal accountability also form part of the responsibility to protect; the concept is much broader than military intervention.
