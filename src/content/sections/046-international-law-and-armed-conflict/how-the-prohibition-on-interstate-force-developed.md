For much of the history covered in this book, rulers could treat war as a recognized instrument of policy. The League of Nations Covenant imposed procedural restrictions on resort to war but did not eliminate it. The 1928 Kellogg-Briand Pact renounced war as an instrument of national policy among its parties, yet enforcement remained weak and its language left interpretive difficulties. These experiments helped prepare the more comprehensive post-1945 framework. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\] Legal historians Oona Hathaway and Scott Shapiro argue that the 1928 Pact was a turning point in the international legal order despite its immediate failures. This is an interpretation worth comparing with accounts that emphasize the Pact’s weak enforcement. The historical question is whether changing the legitimacy of conquest and the legal consequences of war can have effects that emerge only over decades. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-58" class="cite">58</a>\]

Article 2(4) of the UN Charter requires members to refrain from threats or uses of force incompatible with the territorial integrity or political independence of states or the purposes of the United Nations. This prohibition is central to the contemporary order. Its customary counterpart matters even when particular treaty jurisdiction is unavailable. The Charter provides for self-defence and collective action under the Security Council; valid territorial-state consent can also affect the legal analysis of a foreign presence. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

Do not confuse an unfriendly act with armed force. Trade restrictions, diplomatic expulsions, harsh political rhetoric, espionage and coercive interference can raise other legal issues, but they are not automatically uses of force in the Charter sense. Modern disputes about digital activities make classification particularly difficult. Legal vocabulary creates thresholds because attaching a particular consequence to every form of hostility would make the system dangerously unstable. The prohibition does not promise that force will never occur; it changes the legal baseline from which claims must be justified.
