Johan Galtung distinguished negative peace, understood as the absence of direct violence, from a more substantive conception of positive peace concerned with the conditions that allow people to live without structural injustice. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-51" class="cite">51</a>\] The distinction has generated extensive debate, partly because peace can be defined so broadly that it becomes difficult to measure independently of other desirable social outcomes. Nevertheless, it highlights a vital question: what kind of social order is being restored when fighting stops?

A country can experience low levels of organized armed conflict while suppressing opposition, excluding minorities or denying citizens access to justice. Another country may contain intense political disagreement but manage it through peaceful institutions. Visible disagreement is not a reliable measure of fragility. Indeed, the ability to criticize government without fear can be evidence that institutions are functioning. A healthy political order does not require every citizen to agree about history, policy or identity.

The central argument of this chapter is that civil wars are not inevitable consequences of diversity, poverty or historical memory. They emerge from particular combinations of political disputes, institutional failures, economic conditions, decisions and international relationships. Their consequences also depend on how people and institutions respond. The enduring task of peacebuilding is not to make disagreement disappear. It is to create a political community in which losing an election, criticizing a law, belonging to a minority or living far from the capital does not mean losing the protection of the state. That is a demanding ambition, but it is a more useful understanding of peace than silence alone.
