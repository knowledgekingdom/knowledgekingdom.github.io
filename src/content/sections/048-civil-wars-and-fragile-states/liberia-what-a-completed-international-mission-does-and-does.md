Liberia experienced two interconnected periods of civil war between 1989 and 2003. The 2003 Comprehensive Peace Agreement provided a framework for transition, and the UN mission UNMIL supported the subsequent peace process. The mission completed its mandate in March 2018, after nearly fifteen years. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-48" class="cite">48</a>\]

The end of the mission is a meaningful institutional milestone. It indicates that the international operation was no longer required in its previous form. It does not imply that poverty, inequality, corruption or every consequence of war had disappeared. National institutions and communities continued to face the work of sustaining peace. The difference matters because international missions have defined mandates and finite resources; a country's development and political history do not end when those mandates expire.

Liberia also demonstrates the significance of civic participation in peacebuilding, including women's peace activism and the work of local organizations. Outside institutions can provide resources and expertise, but peace is not a product delivered to a passive population. Residents are the people who must live under the resulting arrangements, pay taxes, use public services and participate in political decisions. The measure of an institution's success is ultimately whether it becomes reliable and accountable to those residents, not whether an external agency can produce a favorable closing report.

Recurrence presents a further problem. A country may formally have ended a civil war while institutions remain vulnerable to the same abuses that preceded it. Barbara Walter's research on repeat civil wars emphasizes the role of institutions capable of constraining elites. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-52" class="cite">52</a>\] A useful assessment therefore looks beyond the formal existence of elections or courts to the effectiveness of legal protections. Can officials be investigated for corruption? Can a citizen obtain a remedy against the state? Can an opposition party campaign safely? These are questions about public institutions, not a checklist for predicting an exact date of renewed violence. Evidence of improvement in one area may coexist with deterioration in another.
