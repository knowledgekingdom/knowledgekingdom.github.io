Colombia's armed conflicts involved disputes over land, political exclusion, state authority in rural areas, illicit economies and the activities of multiple armed organizations. The conflict between the government and the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC-EP) was one part of a larger landscape of violence. In November 2016, the government and FARC-EP signed a final peace agreement after an earlier version had been rejected in a referendum. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-31" class="cite">31</a>\]

The agreement addressed rural reform, political participation, the end of the conflict between its signatories, illicit drugs, victims' rights and implementation. International verification and monitoring were built into the process. The UN Verification Mission was established in 2017, and its mandate developed over time to include additional aspects of the agreement. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-32" class="cite">32</a>\] This is a useful example of why monitoring is not merely counting whether a signature exists: different commitments require evidence of different kinds.

The implementation of a comprehensive agreement can vary across regions. A former conflict participant may become a civilian political actor while residents of a rural district still face insecurity, unresolved land claims or violence involving other organizations. The end of one conflict relationship is therefore not equivalent to the end of all armed violence in a country. The Colombian example illustrates the need to identify the relevant parties, the geographic scope of an agreement and the particular commitments being assessed. A peace process can be a substantial achievement without being complete.
