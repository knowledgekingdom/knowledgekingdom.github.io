Guatemala's internal armed conflict lasted from 1962 until the 1996 peace accords. Its history included unequal land ownership, political repression, Cold War intervention and the systematic exclusion of many indigenous communities. The conflict's human consequences were distributed extremely unevenly. Maya communities experienced devastating violence. The UN-supported Commission for Historical Clarification later documented extensive abuses and concluded that acts of genocide had been committed against particular Maya groups. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]

The 1996 settlement was not merely an agreement to stop fighting. It included commitments concerning human rights, indigenous peoples, institutions and social questions. The verification mission MINUGUA supported the implementation process. In later years, arguments about the fulfillment of commitments and accountability for past crimes demonstrated the difference between signing a document and changing the institutions that had permitted abuses.

A truth commission cannot restore a person who disappeared or died. It can, however, create an official evidentiary record that challenges denial, identifies institutional failures and acknowledges victims whose experiences were previously ignored. Whether its findings lead to prosecutions, reparations or institutional reforms is a separate question. When comparing countries, the important measure is not whether a truth commission existed, but what its mandate allowed, how independently it worked and what happened to its recommendations. This case also reminds us that truth-seeking must avoid assigning collective guilt to entire communities.
