The fall of Mohamed Siad Barre's government in 1991 was followed by the fragmentation of Somalia's central institutions and prolonged conflict. International humanitarian and peace operations encountered major obstacles in the absence of an effective and broadly recognized domestic political settlement. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-27" class="cite">27</a>\] The collapse of the central government did not, however, mean that Somali society ceased to organize itself.

Local political arrangements, customary institutions, business networks and community associations continued to perform various functions, although their effectiveness and inclusiveness differed. Different parts of the country experienced different political trajectories. Somaliland declared independence in 1991 and developed its own institutions; its claim to sovereignty remains contested internationally. Somalia's federal institutions have developed over subsequent decades amid ongoing security and governance challenges. The distinction between internationally recognized statehood and effective local administration is especially important here.

The case challenges an assumption sometimes found in international policy: that rebuilding a capital city's institutions automatically restores authority everywhere. A government may issue laws nationally while implementation depends on relationships with local authorities. The problem is not simply to impose uniformity but to create arrangements that provide equal protection and reliable public administration without ignoring the institutions citizens actually use. A centralized structure may be appropriate for some functions and local autonomy for others. Which arrangement is legitimate depends on constitutional choices and the participation of affected people, not on a universal template imported from abroad.
