The Spanish Civil War, fought from 1936 to 1939, arose from intense disputes over land, labor, religion, regional autonomy, political authority and the future of the Spanish Republic. A military rebellion against the Republican government led to a prolonged conflict. The Republic and its opponents contained different political factions, and political violence affected civilians in areas under both sides' authority. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

The conflict was also international. Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy supported Francisco Franco's Nationalists; the Soviet Union supported the Republic, and international volunteers participated on the Republican side. Britain and France supported a policy of non-intervention, although the policy did not prevent foreign involvement. The European diplomatic context mattered, but the war had Spanish origins that cannot be reduced to the international confrontation between fascism and communism.

Franco's victory was followed by dictatorship, not an inclusive settlement. Spain's later democratic transition occurred decades afterward, under a different domestic and international environment. This separation matters when evaluating the end of war. A military outcome can terminate a particular conflict while leaving repression, unresolved historical memory and restrictions on political participation. The consequences of the outcome require independent evaluation rather than being inferred from the absence of renewed fighting.
