James Fearon and David Laitin's 2003 study challenged the assumption that ethnic or religious diversity alone explains which countries experience civil war. Their cross-national analysis gave greater weight to conditions associated with weak state institutions and the possibility of insurgency. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\] It contributed to a major scholarly debate about whether the observed correlates of conflict reflect motivations, institutional constraints or both.

A statistical association is not a complete explanation of a historical event. Low income, for example, may indicate a shortage of administrative resources, limited economic opportunities, unequal development or the effects of earlier violence. The same measured variable can represent several mechanisms. Researchers who find a relationship between poverty and conflict still need evidence to determine which causal account is convincing.

Reverse causation is particularly important. War damages the economy, and a damaged economy may in turn make recovery more difficult. If a researcher measures income after a war starts and then treats low income as its original cause, the analysis mixes the causes and consequences of conflict. The problem resembles Chapter 7's feedback loops: the variables influence each other over time. One should not use these findings to label poor societies as inherently violent or to pretend that wealth makes a country immune from political breakdown.
