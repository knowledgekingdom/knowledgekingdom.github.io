The American Civil War of 1861–1865 followed the secession of southern states and the formation of the Confederacy. Slavery, including disputes over its extension into new territories and its political protection, was central to the crisis. Several seceding states' declarations explicitly identified slavery among their reasons for leaving the Union. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]\[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\] Constitutional disputes over the nature of the Union cannot be separated from the institution they were used to defend.

The war brought the preservation of the Union and the abolition of slavery through the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865. Reconstruction then confronted questions about citizenship, equal protection, political participation and the protection of formerly enslaved people. The Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments transformed the constitutional framework, although their practical enforcement faced organized resistance and would remain deeply contested for generations.

This example illustrates the distinction between ending hostilities and transforming institutions. A formal constitutional guarantee is a major achievement, but it does not automatically ensure that local courts, political authorities and public officials will honor that guarantee. Studying Reconstruction therefore brings us back to state capacity and legitimacy: the capacity to enforce equal citizenship, and the willingness to regard people formerly excluded from the political community as full members of it.
