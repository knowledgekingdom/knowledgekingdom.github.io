Chapter 35 examined imperial rule and African partition. Colonial governments frequently organized taxation, labor, representation and administrative boundaries around the purposes of empire rather than equal citizenship. Independence inherited many of these institutions and the inequalities they had produced. Yet historical inheritance does not mechanically dictate later outcomes. New governments made decisions about education, political participation, public investment and the distribution of authority; international pressures also changed.

A boundary can contain communities that previously participated in different trading networks, political structures or religious institutions. That does not make coexistence impossible. Most states contain diverse populations, and some borders with difficult histories have become relatively peaceful. Equally, a population sharing a language can experience profound political conflict. Historical analysis should trace specific institutions and choices rather than treat diversity as a prediction of disaster.

There is another trap: selecting only countries in which inherited inequalities led to war. To evaluate the argument, examine comparable societies where similar inequalities did not produce sustained violence. What institutions allowed grievances to be addressed without armed conflict? What external conditions differed? Comparing only the cases that experienced the outcome is like examining only collapsed bridges when trying to establish which construction methods are safe.
