Oil, minerals and other valuable commodities can support development through public revenue, employment and infrastructure. They can also become entangled in corruption and conflict where oversight is weak. The resource curse is a family of hypotheses about why some resource-dependent economies experience disappointing institutional or economic outcomes. It is not a law that makes every resource-rich country fragile. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

The difference between owning resources and managing their income fairly is crucial. A government that depends heavily on one export may experience a sudden fiscal crisis when world prices fall. If public salaries and services depend on that income, the shock can affect households far from the producing region. When institutions are already contested, disputes about public spending may become politically explosive. Conversely, transparent budgets, predictable rules and economic diversification can reduce vulnerability.

The term war economy refers to economic relationships reshaped by ongoing conflict, including the loss of normal employment, damaged infrastructure, coercive transactions and the emergence of interests tied to continuing insecurity. Such relationships can endure after a peace agreement. Rebuilding ordinary civilian economic life is therefore not a side issue. A society in which workers can rely on predictable incomes and courts can enforce ordinary contracts offers alternatives to dependence on violent or coercive institutions.
