Civil wars affect people through their age, health, social roles, economic circumstances and legal status. Women may lose access to essential services, face sexual violence, assume increased care responsibilities and participate in civic organizations. Men and boys may also face sexual violence, forced recruitment, detention or loss of family livelihoods. These experiences vary widely; no population should be reduced to a single stereotypical role.

United Nations Security Council Resolution 1325, adopted in 2000, recognizes the importance of women's participation in peace and security and the need for protection from conflict-related harms. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-40" class="cite">40</a>\] Meaningful participation requires more than the presence of a few individuals at a meeting. Participants must have opportunities to influence decisions, access information, raise concerns safely and represent a variety of experiences. The same attention to inclusion applies to other groups whose voices may be marginalized.

Children have particular protection needs. Separation from caregivers, interrupted education, recruitment and exposure to violence can have lasting effects. Recovery must focus on the best interests of the child rather than treating children associated with armed organizations as permanently defined by that experience. Civil documentation, schooling, family reunification where appropriate, and specialized psychosocial support are all important components of rehabilitation. The point is not to presume that all survivors will respond in the same way, but to recognize that rebuilding public institutions includes the obligation to protect individuals who may have little political influence.
