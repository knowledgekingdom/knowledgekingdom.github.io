Nepal's Maoist insurgency lasted from 1996 to 2006. Its background included rural deprivation, social exclusion and disputes about the role of the monarchy. The conflict overlapped with a broader political crisis and a movement for democratic change. In November 2006, the government and Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) concluded a Comprehensive Peace Agreement. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-30" class="cite">30</a>\]

The agreement connected the end of conflict to a wider political transition, including elections for a constituent assembly. Nepal became a federal democratic republic in 2008, and a new constitution was promulgated in 2015. The constitutional process was contested, with debates over federal arrangements, citizenship and the political representation of marginalized populations. The fact that a new constitution was adopted did not mean all the issues behind the conflict were resolved.

The importance of Nepal's example is that the political destination was not identical to the institutional order that existed before war. A settlement sometimes requires substantial constitutional change rather than merely restoring the former government. At the same time, a negotiated commitment to peaceful competition cannot substitute for continued protection of rights or accountability for violations committed during the conflict. Political incorporation and transitional justice are different tasks, and progress on one does not automatically complete the other.
