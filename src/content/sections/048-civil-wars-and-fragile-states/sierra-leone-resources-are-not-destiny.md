Sierra Leone's civil war began in 1991 and formally ended in 2002. The conflict became internationally associated with diamonds, but this familiar association should not obscure the importance of corruption, political exclusion, institutional decline and the regional instability surrounding Liberia. Valuable resources became entangled with the war economy; they were not a complete explanation of the origins of the war. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-25" class="cite">25</a>\]

The UN mission UNAMSIL, established in 1999, experienced serious early setbacks. Its subsequent work helped support the transition toward peace, public administration and the return of displaced people. Regional engagement and international support were also important. Later accountability efforts included a truth and reconciliation commission and the Special Court for Sierra Leone. Each addressed a different part of the country's recovery: recording the past, recognizing victims, establishing individual criminal responsibility and rebuilding the institutions of ordinary government.

The conflict generated a broader international discussion about the trade in diamonds associated with violence. The Kimberley Process, introduced in 2003, created a certification framework for trade in rough diamonds, although its definition and enforcement have been criticized. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-26" class="cite">26</a>\] A consumer label cannot by itself repair political exclusion or guarantee ethical conditions at every stage of a supply chain. The example is valuable precisely because it connects international commerce, domestic governance and conflict prevention without treating any one of them as a magical solution.
