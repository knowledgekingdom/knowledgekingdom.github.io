In a historical chronology, one event follows another: a demonstration, a harsh government response, a new wave of protests, a political rupture. This sequence may suggest a causal relationship, but sequence alone cannot establish one. Similar demonstrations may produce concessions in another country, while repression sometimes prevents open opposition without addressing the injustice that caused it.

Political scientists study processes of escalation because background indicators often change slowly while conflicts begin at particular moments. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\] The question is not only why a country was vulnerable, but also why the political process took a particular course. This requires evidence from institutions, public records, independent journalism, interviews and the study of political decisions. The interpretation of a disputed event can vary greatly between participants, so the analyst needs to separate confirmed facts from claims about motives.

Governments possess a particular responsibility to uphold rights and maintain lawful public order. Opposition organizations also have responsibilities for their own conduct. These responsibilities are not erased by assigning a label such as 'revolution', 'security operation' or 'resistance'. A causal explanation may identify the role of an abusive institution without treating every subsequent act of violence as justified. The same principle appeared in our study of international law: a claimed political purpose cannot substitute for an assessment of conduct.
