Max Weber famously associated the modern state with its claim to legitimate physical force within a territory. The emphasis is on a claim to legitimacy, not the empirical absence of every other violent actor. Even a well-functioning state experiences crime. A state may lawfully authorize private security or delegate certain functions without losing sovereignty. What distinguishes public authority is the expectation that rules and their enforcement have an institutional basis beyond the personal preferences of powerful individuals. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]

Think of the ordinary acts through which a state becomes visible: registering a birth, resolving a property dispute, maintaining roads, issuing a passport, paying a teacher, collecting taxes or responding to an epidemic. An institution must usually combine staff, money, information, legal authority and some measure of public cooperation. An office building by itself provides none of these. Nor does an impressive military parade establish that the government can administer a rural district fairly.

International legal personality and effective government are also distinct. A recognized country does not automatically cease to be a state because its government loses control of parts of its territory. A change of cabinet likewise need not alter the identity of the state. This distinction helps us understand why failed institutions do not simply erase a population's citizenship or eliminate the need for diplomatic agreements. In Lydora, replacing the broken court is a problem of effective administration, while the validity of the country's international obligations is a separate question.
