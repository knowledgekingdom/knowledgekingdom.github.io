The removal of a dictatorship, a disputed election, a constitutional crisis or the sudden death of a ruler can alter expectations about the future. People who once knew the rules of political competition may no longer trust that today's guarantees will survive tomorrow. This uncertainty can create pressure on institutions at precisely the moment when they must manage difficult decisions.

Democracy is not merely the holding of a vote. It requires rules protecting opposition, fair political competition, dependable courts and procedures for accepting and contesting electoral outcomes. A government that considers every criticism illegitimate weakens those procedures. A losing party that refuses every unfavorable result can likewise undermine the institutions needed for peaceful competition. Each case must be judged on the evidence rather than by assuming the two parties bear equal responsibility.

Transition does not inevitably cause conflict. Peaceful transitions have occurred in many countries, and authoritarian regimes can themselves produce extensive violence. The relevant questions concern the credibility of constitutional rules, treatment of dissent, continuity of public administration and the ability of diverse groups to participate. An election is a recurring mechanism of government, not a substitute for the underlying social and institutional agreement that makes losing an election tolerable.
