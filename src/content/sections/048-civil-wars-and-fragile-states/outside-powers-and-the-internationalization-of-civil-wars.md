The boundary between interstate and intrastate war is often blurred. Foreign governments may take diplomatic positions, supply economic support, impose sanctions, provide humanitarian relief or become direct participants in a conflict. The presence of external involvement does not make local actors mere puppets: each may retain independent interests and disagreements. Conversely, ignoring international involvement can conceal major causes of a conflict's persistence.

During the Cold War, many conflicts in newly independent states were interpreted through the global rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union, as Chapter 41 described. Regional powers, domestic political movements and former colonial states had their own priorities. A conflict might begin before either superpower became involved, and local disputes often survived the end of the Cold War. Calling a war a proxy war therefore identifies an important dimension, but not a complete explanation.

International engagement can also support peace. Neighboring governments may have an interest in stable trade, safe borders and the return of displaced people. Regional organizations can convene negotiations and help monitor political commitments. A foreign policy that promotes a peace process should be evaluated by its actions and effects, not only by its stated motives. The question is whether external involvement helps institutions become accountable to the people they govern or creates a new dependency.
