Syria's uprising in 2011 and the government's violent repression developed into a complex war involving domestic and external actors. The fall of the Assad regime in December 2024 opened a new political transition, as the UN Secretary-General noted at the time. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-35" class="cite">35</a>\] That milestone did not itself guarantee a stable, inclusive order. The questions of citizenship, accountable institutions, displaced people's rights and the protection of minority communities remained essential to evaluating the transition.

Myanmar presents a different historical trajectory. The country had experienced internal armed conflicts for decades before the military takeover of February 2021. The coup reversed political developments and was followed by widespread opposition, renewed and expanding armed conflict and a severe humanitarian crisis. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-36" class="cite">36</a>\] Longstanding disputes over the constitutional distribution of power and the rights of different communities cannot be explained entirely by the events of a single year.

These two cases are not interchangeable. Their institutional histories, regional environments and political actors differ profoundly. They illustrate a shared analytical principle: a change in the government is not a sufficient measure of the end of conflict or the quality of the institutions that follow. Because both situations have evolved rapidly, the discussion here is anchored to dated milestones rather than a claim about a continuously current map of authority. A reader researching their later development should consult updated UN reports, independent journalism and multiple local sources, paying attention to access limitations and differences in terminology.
