Imagine a baker in Lydora. Before the crisis, flour arrived regularly, customers used a dependable currency and the shop borrowed money from a bank. As insecurity grows, deliveries become unreliable, savings lose value and customers increasingly cannot afford bread. Even if the building remains intact, the business may close because the institutions supporting ordinary exchange no longer function.

Multiply that problem across a country's economy. Firms delay investment, banks become more cautious, public revenue falls and employees lose work. Government expenditure may shift from maintenance and education toward emergency needs. Electricity networks, telecommunications and health facilities can deteriorate together. The economic damage is not simply the market value of destroyed buildings; it includes income that would otherwise have been earned and opportunities that never materialize.

Estimates of economic losses depend on an uncertain comparison with a world in which the war did not occur. Researchers use assumptions about previous growth, comparable countries or alternative projections. These estimates are valuable for revealing the scale of disruption, but their uncertainty should be reported. A figure for the cost of war is not a complete account of its human consequences, and human rights are not contingent upon their monetary valuation. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]
