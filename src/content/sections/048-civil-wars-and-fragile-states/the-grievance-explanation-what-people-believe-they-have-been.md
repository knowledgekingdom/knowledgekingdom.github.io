Grievances can concern exclusion from public office, unequal access to land, discriminatory law, repression, loss of cultural recognition or the absence of meaningful political representation. They are not necessarily imaginary simply because they are expressed through political narratives. A person who cannot obtain equal treatment in a court experiences an institutional problem, not merely a subjective feeling.

But grievance alone is not a sufficient explanation of war. Countless people experience injustice without taking part in violence. Some organize trade unions, form political parties, petition courts, migrate or join nonviolent movements. The same injustice can produce different responses depending on the political environment and the choices available. The existence of anger does not establish that the only possible outcome was civil war.

The joint United Nations and World Bank study Pathways for Peace emphasizes the importance of exclusion from power, resources, security and justice in the prevention of violent conflict. The implication is not that all grievances must be satisfied exactly as stated, but that durable channels for legitimate participation and dispute resolution are essential. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\] Imagine Lydora's rural districts demanding access to the national courts. The government can dispute a particular proposed reform while still recognizing the residents' equal citizenship. The distinction between disagreeing with a demand and denying the right to make it is central to peaceful politics.
