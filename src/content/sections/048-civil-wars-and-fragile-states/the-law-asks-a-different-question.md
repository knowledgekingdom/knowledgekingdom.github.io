As Chapter 46 explained, international humanitarian law distinguishes international armed conflict (between states) from non-international armed conflict, in which at least one party is a non-state armed group. For the latter, the assessment generally depends on the intensity of violence and the organization of the parties. The law does not simply borrow the numerical conventions of political science. Additional Protocol II has a more specific scope than the minimum rules in Common Article 3. \[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/048-civil-wars-and-fragile-states/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]

A riot, an isolated criminal incident or a disputed election does not automatically constitute an armed conflict. Conversely, a government cannot prevent applicable humanitarian obligations simply by calling an organized armed conflict a police matter. Classification follows the facts rather than the label used by a political spokesperson. The minimum humanitarian protections include humane treatment of people who are not, or are no longer, taking part in hostilities; murder, torture, hostage-taking and humiliating treatment are prohibited under Common Article 3. Applying that article does not confer political recognition on an armed group.

Foreign intervention complicates classification. A country can contain multiple legally distinct conflicts at the same time. A dispute between a government and an organized armed group may coexist with a conflict between two states. Rather than describing an entire country's situation with one label and assuming every legal consequence follows, lawyers identify the parties and relationships. This is a practical example of Chapter 46's distinction between the legality of force and the protection of people once hostilities exist.
