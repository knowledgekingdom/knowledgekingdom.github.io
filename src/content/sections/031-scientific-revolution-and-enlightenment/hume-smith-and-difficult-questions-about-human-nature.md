David Hume investigated experience, causation, belief, and the foundations of moral judgment. His skeptical questions about induction remain influential: observing a pattern repeatedly does not yield a logically guaranteed proof that it must continue forever. Modern science still depends on judgments about evidence, probability, and explanations, not on perfect philosophical certainty.

Adam Smith studied moral sentiments, commercial society, specialization, and institutions. <em>The Theory of Moral Sentiments</em> (1759) and <em>The Wealth of Nations</em> (1776) should be read together rather than reducing Smith to a single slogan about markets. His analysis of the division of labor explored productivity while also recognizing dangers associated with repetitive work and unequal power. These ideas belong to the history of political economy, not simply a celebration of individual greed.

The Scottish Enlightenment and other regional movements demonstrated that intellectual renewal was not confined to Paris. Edinburgh, Glasgow, Königsberg, Berlin, Naples, and many other centers hosted different debates. Cross-border networks mattered, but the histories were not identical.
