The scientific and Enlightenment transformations reached southeastern Europe through merchants, clerics, schools, translators, printed books, diaspora communities, Habsburg cities, and Ottoman urban networks. Orthodox intellectual life did not vanish under Ottoman rule; institutions and opportunities varied widely, and different populations developed educational and commercial connections across borders.

Bulgarian, Greek, Serbian, Romanian, and other scholars and readers encountered new works through multiple channels. The later Bulgarian National Revival should not be projected backward as if eighteenth-century inhabitants all understood themselves through nineteenth-century national categories. Paisius of Hilendar's <em>Slav-Bulgarian History</em> (1762) belongs to a developing culture of religious, historical, and linguistic self-reflection, but it was neither a straightforward scientific manifesto nor a complete national movement by itself.

Across the Ottoman world, reform and technical knowledge also developed through local institutions, military needs, diplomacy, and translation. The point is not to impose a Paris-centered story on every region. Ideas traveled unevenly, and communities adapted them to different political and religious conditions.
