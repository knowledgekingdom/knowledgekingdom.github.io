John Locke's political works, especially the <em>Two Treatises of Government</em> (published in 1689, dated 1690), offered influential arguments about natural rights, political consent, and legitimate resistance to arbitrary government. Locke challenged the idea that a ruler's authority was automatically inherited from a patriarchal original sovereignty. For Locke, political authority required justification in relation to the rights of the governed.

These arguments helped later constitutional thinkers, but they are not a complete blueprint for modern universal democracy. Questions remain about property, majority rule, who counts as part of the consenting people, and the historical reality of societies without equal political rights. Locke's entanglement with colonial contexts further complicates easy celebrations of his legacy.

Distinguish the moral principle from the institutional arrangement. A claim that government must rest on consent tells us something about legitimacy; it does not by itself say how broad suffrage must be, how minorities are protected, or how ownership and power affect genuine freedom. Those questions would become crucial in the Atlantic revolutions and remain important today.
