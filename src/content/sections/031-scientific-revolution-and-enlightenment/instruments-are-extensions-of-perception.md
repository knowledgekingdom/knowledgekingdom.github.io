Telescopes and microscopes made previously unseen phenomena visible. Thermometers, barometers, pendulum clocks, balances, and surveying equipment made patterns more comparable. Every instrument also introduced new errors: lenses distorted images, scales needed calibration, and clocks changed performance with conditions. Reliability depended on standards, maintenance, and skilled users.

Robert Hooke's <em>Micrographia</em> (1665) offered striking images of small structures, including the compartments he called 'cells' in cork. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek reported observations of microscopic organisms using lenses that differed from those of many compound microscopes. The ability to see tiny life did not immediately yield a complete theory of infection or a modern cell theory; those developed later.

The lesson is that technological improvement and conceptual change often reinforce each other. A new device provides an unexpected observation, which demands a new explanation. The explanation then motivates improved devices and experiments. But access matters: if a machine is costly, distant, or held under institutional control, many people cannot participate in the knowledge it produces.
