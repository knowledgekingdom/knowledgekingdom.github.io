René Descartes sought secure foundations for knowledge through systematic doubt. The purpose was not to believe nothing forever, but to identify what could resist radical questioning. His philosophical program linked arguments about mind and body with hopes for a more mechanical account of the physical world. The famous <em>Discourse on Method</em> appeared in 1637.

Mechanical explanations approached nature through matter, motion, and regular relationships rather than relying solely on inherited forms or purposes. But Descartes's physical theories were not all correct. His vortex theory of planetary motion, for example, was displaced by more successful mathematical explanations. A thinker can transform the questions people ask without supplying the final answers.

The tension between rational construction and empirical investigation remained productive. Mathematical reasoning might show what follows from assumptions, but observation must test whether those assumptions apply to the world. The central lesson is not 'trust reason instead of tradition.' It is that reason also needs discipline, and apparently self-evident principles may need revision when evidence contradicts them.
