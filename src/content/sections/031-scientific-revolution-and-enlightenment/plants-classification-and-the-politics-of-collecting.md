Natural historians catalogued plants and animals, developed methods of classification, and built collections of specimens. Carl Linnaeus's eighteenth-century naming system helped standardize the communication of species names; it did not solve every problem of biological relationships, and modern taxonomy has changed. Natural history drew on gardens, field observation, illustrations, Indigenous names and ecological knowledge, commercial networks, and colonial ships.

A specimen crossing an ocean might be labeled under the name of a European collector even when local communities had known its properties for centuries. Botanical gardens could serve scientific comparison while also helping empires transport economically valuable crops. The study of nature and the pursuit of profit were intertwined.

We should ask three questions of any celebrated expedition: what did the visiting investigator learn, from whom, and under what conditions? A herbarium or museum collection contains evidence not only about biology but about the political economy of collecting. The same networks could foster genuine intellectual exchange and coercive appropriation.
