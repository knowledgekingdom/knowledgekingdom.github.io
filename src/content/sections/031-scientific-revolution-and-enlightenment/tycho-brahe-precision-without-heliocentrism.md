Tycho Brahe, working in Denmark, assembled unusually precise observations of planetary positions before the telescope. His instruments and observatory required substantial resources and labor. He studied the new star of 1572 and a comet in 1577, events that challenged aspects of the traditional picture of an unchanging celestial realm. These observations mattered even though Tycho did not accept a fully Copernican cosmos.

Tycho proposed an alternative arrangement: the Sun revolved around a stationary Earth, while the other planets revolved around the Sun. That model accommodated many apparent motions without committing to Earth's movement. It reminds us that an observation can undermine one view without uniquely deciding between all possible alternatives.

After Tycho's death, Johannes Kepler drew on the extensive measurements of Mars. The relationship between the observer who collected data and the theoretician who interpreted them became a contested part of the story. Yet the scientific consequence was extraordinary: more precise measurements narrowed the range of plausible mathematical explanations. Data were not a decorative addition to theory. They made some elegant theories impossible to preserve.
