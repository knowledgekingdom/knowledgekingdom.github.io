Frederick II of Prussia, Catherine II of Russia, and Joseph II of the Habsburg monarchy are frequently associated with 'enlightened absolutism.' They sponsored or discussed administrative rationalization, education, religious policy, legal change, and economic improvement. Yet they remained rulers with coercive power, military interests, and dynastic priorities. Reform from above could expand opportunities in one area and strengthen state control in another.

Joseph II pursued reforms affecting religious toleration and serf obligations, some of which encountered strong resistance and were modified or reversed. Catherine corresponded with philosophers while expanding imperial authority and preserving serfdom. Frederick encouraged intellectual life within a militarized state. These were political programs shaped by constraints, not laboratory experiments proving that rational advice guaranteed liberty.

To assess reform, compare an official decree with implementation. Did peasants acquire enforceable rights? Could minorities worship safely? Were courts less arbitrary? Did government improve education at the expense of new controls? The difference between announced ideals and lived experience is a recurring lesson of political history.
