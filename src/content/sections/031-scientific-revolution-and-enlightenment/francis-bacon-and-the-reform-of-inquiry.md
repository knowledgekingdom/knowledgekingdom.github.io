Francis Bacon argued that inherited systems and human habits of thought often distorted the study of nature. In works including the <em>Novum Organum</em> (1620), he encouraged organized collection of observations, comparison of cases, and practical investigation. His discussion of the 'idols' of the mind anticipated a recurring concern: human judgment can be led astray by personal inclination, language, and social authority.

Bacon is often presented as the inventor of 'the scientific method,' a misleading simplification. He did not single-handedly create experimentation, which had ancient and diverse histories; nor did practicing investigators uniformly follow his program. Mathematics, models, skilled craftsmanship, and competing philosophical approaches remained indispensable.

Bacon's importance also lay in imagining knowledge as a collective, organized enterprise, supported by institutions and directed toward practical effects. That ambition could improve public welfare through medicine and technology. Yet the idea of mastering nature also developed alongside colonial expansion and commercial exploitation. Scientific promises should be judged not only by technical success but by who receives the benefits and who bears the costs.
