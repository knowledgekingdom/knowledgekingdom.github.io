The new European institutions did not develop in an empty global space. Astronomy in the Islamic world had long combined observation, mathematical modeling, instrument-making, and criticism of earlier authorities. European scholars used knowledge and texts transmitted through Arabic and Latin networks. This history should not be reduced to a simple relay in which one civilization passes ancient wisdom unchanged to another.

Jesuit mathematicians and astronomers working in China brought European astronomical methods into conversation with Chinese calendar-making and court science. Chinese officials and scholars assessed, adopted, adapted, or rejected elements of these techniques, sometimes in the service of imperial calendrical accuracy. European merchants and intellectuals also circulated accounts of Chinese statecraft and moral philosophy, often through selective and inaccurate descriptions.

In South Asia and elsewhere, multiple mathematical, astronomical, medical, and observational traditions continued and interacted with European expansion. Comparisons require care: the existence of sophisticated knowledge in different regions does not imply that institutions operated identically, or that a single modern scientific model had already emerged everywhere. We should seek exchange, difference, and power rather than a universal ranking of civilizations.
