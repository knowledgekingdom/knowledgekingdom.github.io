By the late eighteenth century, powerful new traditions of measuring, experimenting, calculating, publishing, and criticizing had reshaped some forms of natural inquiry. Mathematical physics became more systematic. Institutions connected observers across borders. Philosophers and public writers questioned monarchy, religious authority, commerce, education, and inequality. Yet the world had not become universally scientific, equal, secular, or democratic. Many people remained excluded from formal learning, and empires claimed new territories and resources.

The Scientific Revolution's lasting achievement was not a rule saying that experts are always correct. It was the growth of practices that made difficult claims more publicly contestable through evidence, mathematics, procedures, and criticism. The Enlightenment's enduring contribution was similarly imperfect: it widened the range of institutions people could ask to justify themselves. Both processes produced knowledge and controversy, and both depended on social structures that sometimes betrayed their ideals.

Return to the two books with which we began. Newton's <em>Principia</em> represents an ambition to explain the physical world through connected principles; the <em>Encyclopédie</em> represents an ambition to gather and redistribute knowledge through public communication. The first cannot decide moral justice, and the second cannot guarantee that knowledge will be used fairly. Understanding their possibilities and limits prepares us for the revolutionary century ahead.
