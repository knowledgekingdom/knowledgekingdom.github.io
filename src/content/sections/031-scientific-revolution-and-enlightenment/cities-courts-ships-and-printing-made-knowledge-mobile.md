The expansion of printing reduced the cost of reproducing some texts and images, although the prices of scholarly books and instruments remained substantial. Printed editions helped standardize tables, diagrams, and terminology. Readers could dispute the same illustration, send corrections, and compare versions. Errors also spread efficiently: printing increased circulation, not guaranteed accuracy. Manuscripts, correspondence, lectures, and oral instruction continued alongside print.

Patrons mattered. Princes financed astronomers for calendars, prestige, astrology, and navigation. Merchants wanted accurate surveying, timekeeping, and calculation. States sponsored maps and voyages; military engineers investigated ballistics and fortification. Workshops supplied lenses, clocks, pumps, scales, and precision metalwork. Many improvements arose from cooperation between people of different education and status, even when elite publications credited only a learned author.

Global contact mattered too. European empires obtained geographical, botanical, agricultural, and medical information from Indigenous communities, Asian specialists, and Africans, often under profoundly unequal conditions. Knowledge could move voluntarily through exchange or be extracted through colonial power. A history of discovery that counts only the European name printed on a book leaves out much of the work that made the book possible.
