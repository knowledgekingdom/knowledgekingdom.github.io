The Enlightenment supplied concepts later used to criticize monarchy, inherited privilege, and unequal rights. But it did not 'cause' the American, French, or Haitian Revolutions in the way a physical force causes an object's acceleration. Political crises resulted from fiscal pressures, imperial wars, local conflicts, mobilization, and historical contingencies as well as ideas. Different actors invoked Enlightenment language for incompatible purposes.

The relationship between ideas and action is reciprocal. New concepts make some grievances easier to articulate; political struggle changes how concepts are understood. Rights language could be deployed by property-owning colonial revolutionaries, abolitionists, enslaved rebels, women petitioners, and opponents of popular democracy. The same word—liberty—might conceal different assumptions about who belongs.

Chapter 32 will follow these arguments into political upheaval. For now, the important preparation is to separate intellectual influences from sufficient causes. A book can change what people believe is possible without determining exactly when or how they act.
