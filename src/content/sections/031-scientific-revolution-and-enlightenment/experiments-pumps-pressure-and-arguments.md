Robert Boyle's work on gases helped establish a culture of controlled, repeatable experiment. Air pumps allowed investigators to study pressure and the apparent properties of a partial vacuum. Under specified conditions, the pressure and volume of a fixed amount of gas were found to vary inversely, a relationship commonly known as Boyle's law. Similar ideas were developed by Edme Mariotte and others, and the law has limits when gases depart from ideal behavior.

A public demonstration did more than show an impressive machine. Viewers needed to decide whether the apparatus worked, whether witnesses were credible, and whether the reported conditions could be reproduced elsewhere. The construction of experimental authority therefore depended on mechanical skill, written description, social trust, and sometimes exclusionary norms about who counted as a reliable observer.

Not all demonstrations were innocent. Experiments involving animals raised questions about suffering, and equipment depended on labor that might be anonymous in printed accounts. When historians speak of the rise of experimentation, they should study the people and materials that made reliable measurement possible, rather than crediting a philosopher in isolation.
