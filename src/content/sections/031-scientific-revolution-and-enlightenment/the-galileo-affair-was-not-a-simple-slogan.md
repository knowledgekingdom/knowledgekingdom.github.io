Galileo's public advocacy of Copernicanism brought him into conflict with Catholic authorities. The controversy involved scriptural interpretation, philosophical assumptions, institutional authority, the politics of patronage, and particular works and arguments. In 1616, Church authorities restricted the defense of heliocentrism as physical truth. After publication of Galileo's <em>Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems</em> in 1632, the Roman Inquisition tried him; in 1633 he was condemned and placed under house arrest.

It is wrong to present the episode as proof that all religion opposed all scientific inquiry. Many scholars, including clergy, made major contributions to mathematics and astronomy. Catholic institutions supported scientific teaching in some settings and restricted it in others. Equally, a vague statement that the conflict was 'complex' must not conceal the coercive power exercised against Galileo and the damage done when institutional authority constrained argument over nature.

This case illustrates a political question that recurs throughout Knowledge Kingdom: who has the authority to decide whether a claim may be publicly defended? Institutions can promote learning through resources and networks, then undermine it by punishing conclusions that threaten their worldview.
