Yinxu, the ruins of the late Shang capital at Anyang, supplies much of the evidence that allows scholars to study named Shang rulers. Excavations have recovered palatial and ritual areas, workshops, elite tombs and extensive collections of inscribed bones and turtle shells. The remains reveal a society capable of organizing both delicate craft production and major construction; the inscriptions reveal something of its court's concerns. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]

The modern rediscovery of oracle bones is itself an instructive historical episode. Inscribed bones entered antiquarian and medicinal markets, and scholars recognized that their markings represented ancient writing. Later archaeological work associated inscriptions with the late Shang capital. Isolated objects could be dated and interpreted much more powerfully once they were connected with excavated contexts. This is why the careful recovery of a text matters as much as the cleverness of someone who reads it. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

Royal records do not offer equal access to all people. A king's inquiry about rain, childbirth or an expedition reflects a court's priorities, not a national opinion poll. Scholars also encounter fragmentary inscriptions, competing readings of graphs and uncertainty about how to reconstruct spoken language from writing. We should celebrate the evidence without exaggerating its coverage. The <em>absence</em> of a peasant diary is not evidence that peasants had no complex thoughts about their world.
