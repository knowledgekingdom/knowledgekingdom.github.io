The king could not commission a ritual vessel without people who cultivated food and supplied materials. Northern communities grew grains such as millet and, in different settings and periods, wheat; other regions maintained substantial rice-growing traditions. People kept pigs and other domestic animals, hunted, gathered, fished and traded. Production varied geographically and socially. A dynastic narrative centered on chariots and sacrifices can conceal the farms, households, waterways and roads that sustained the political elite. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]

Imagine a hypothetical administrative problem: a palace workshop needs 120 units of grain to feed specialists through a production season, while local households require 480 and the community must reserve 100 against bad harvests. If the available store is 650 units, there is a shortfall of <strong>50 units</strong>. The mathematics does not prove a real Shang account existed with these numbers. It simply reveals the choices rulers or communities face: reduce consumption, redistribute from another settlement, delay production, obtain more grain through trade or compel people to absorb the shortage. Each option has social consequences.

Transportation matters as much as production. Bronze casting can involve copper and tin sources distant from the workshop; jade and cowrie shells also reveal connections across regions. The presence of an exotic item proves a connection of some sort, but not necessarily a single uninterrupted journey by one merchant. Goods might change hands several times. Archaeologists study source regions, chemical signatures, manufacturing styles, road and river possibilities, and the contexts in which objects ended up. A wealthy tomb is evidence of accumulated goods; it is not a transparent map of all trade routes. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]
