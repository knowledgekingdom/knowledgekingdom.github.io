Much of what people today associate with ancient Chinese philosophy arose within the political turmoil of the later Zhou centuries. Thinkers later linked with Confucian, Mohist, Daoist and Legalist traditions debated questions such as: How should rulers behave? What creates social order? When is warfare justified? What duties do families and governments owe? Can good institutions train people to act well? These traditions were internally diverse and developed through texts compiled and transmitted over time. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-22" class="cite">22</a>\]

<strong>Confucius</strong>, traditionally dated <strong>551–479 BCE</strong>, belongs to the Eastern Zhou world, far later than the earliest Shang oracle bones. It is a chronological mistake to project his teachings backward as if they had directly founded Shang political institutions. He and other thinkers responded to inherited rituals and the problems of their own age. The term <em>Hundred Schools</em> is a later umbrella label for an intellectual world that was diverse, argumentative and politically consequential.

For a reader seeking broad knowledge, the most useful bridge is not to memorize twenty philosophers immediately. Instead, recognize the question their debates inherited from the early state: <strong>what makes authority legitimate, and what institutions can make people cooperate without constant force?</strong> Religion, law, moral education, administration and punishment offered different answers. Subsequent Chinese histories—from imperial rule to local society—cannot be understood without these debates, but those ideas deserve fuller treatment in their proper later setting.
