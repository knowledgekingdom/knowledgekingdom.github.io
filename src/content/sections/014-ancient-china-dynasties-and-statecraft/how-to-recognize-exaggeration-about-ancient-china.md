Ancient China is especially vulnerable to two opposed simplifications. One turns every early archaeological object into proof of an uninterrupted five-thousand-year empire. The other treats ancient Chinese society as a single isolated civilization with no contact or internal variation. Both exaggerations flatten complex evidence. Early rice and millet farmers, Liangzhu engineers, Erlitou elites, Anyang kings and Sichuan metalworkers did not live in one simultaneously unified political system. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-7" class="cite">7</a>\]

A sound historical claim should answer at least four questions. <strong>First</strong>, what is the date and provenance of the evidence? <strong>Second</strong>, is the claim based on a contemporary inscription, excavated material or a much later account? <strong>Third</strong>, does the inference concern cultural influence, political control or something else? <strong>Fourth</strong>, what alternative explanations remain possible? If a post claims that a patterned pot 'proves the first written constitution', ask where the decipherment, context and corroborating records are. If a post says 'all of China was ruled by a Shang emperor', ask which territory and which century it means.

The same critical standard applies to familiar names. 'Xia' is an important tradition, not a universally verified list of contemporary inscriptions. 'Shang' designates a ruling house whose later phase is securely evidenced, not every bronze found within modern borders. 'Zhou' can mean the royal dynasty, a cultural order or an era in which kings gradually lost effective control. And the Mandate of Heaven describes an ideological framework whose use and meaning changed—not an automatic physical law by which nature removed bad rulers.
