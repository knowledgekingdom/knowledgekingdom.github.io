Around <strong>1046 BCE</strong>, a western regional power known as the Zhou defeated the Shang ruling house. Traditional narratives include the Battle of Muye and accounts of King Wu and his predecessors. The conquest has strong historical significance, but narrative details preserved in later sources should be evaluated separately from contemporary or near-contemporary bronze inscriptions and archaeological evidence. A new lineage had to do more than win a battle: it had to convince influential communities that its commands deserved obedience. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

The Zhou leaders inherited some practices and technologies from the Shang world while developing new ways to organize rule and justify it. They rewarded relatives and supporters, established regional centers and maintained networks of obligation linking the royal house to powerful lineages. This is sometimes compared with European 'feudalism', but the comparison must be qualified: political authority, landholding and ritual obligations were organized in specific ancient Chinese ways, not simply copies of medieval European institutions. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]

A conquering dynasty has an obvious ideological difficulty. If the Shang kings had appeared legitimate for generations, why had the Zhou been right to overthrow them? An answer emerged in the language of <strong>Heaven</strong> and the <strong>Mandate of Heaven</strong>. The idea would become one of the most enduring political concepts in Chinese historical thought.
