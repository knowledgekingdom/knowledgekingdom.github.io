Shang royal ritual centered on relations with deceased royal ancestors and other powers, including a high power often rendered as <strong>Di or Shangdi</strong> in scholarly discussions. The court made offerings, asked questions and sought favorable relations with the unseen. The precise religious meaning of ancient terms is a matter for interpretation; we should not simply translate Shangdi into a fully developed concept from later religious traditions. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

Ancestral ceremonies had a political dimension. A king could claim special responsibility for maintaining relations with the royal dead. A ruling family linked present commands to inherited authority. Ritual bronzes, food, drink and sacrifices materialized those claims; the buildings, craft workers, animals and labor they required made spiritual authority part of everyday political economy. This does not imply that all Shang subjects held the same beliefs or had equal access to the court's rituals.

Human and animal sacrifice are also present in the archaeological and written record. They are serious evidence of coercion and unequal power, not picturesque details to be romanticized. Scholars debate specific practices and their changes, but the broader existence of violent ritual is well attested. In comparing the Shang with other early states, we should not rank civilizations with a single moral label; instead, we can ask what mechanisms linked elite authority, coercion and religious obligation and whose lives were most vulnerable. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]
