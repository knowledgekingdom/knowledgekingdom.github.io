There is a danger in building a history exclusively from royal graves, ceremonial vessels and oracle-bone texts: we begin to imagine that everyone was a king, a priest or a warrior. Most ancient people spent most days obtaining food, caring for children and elders, making and repairing things, and navigating social obligations that rarely entered royal inscriptions. The best corrective is careful household archaeology. Dwelling foundations, hearths, pottery, animal bones, plant remains, wells, refuse pits and tools can reveal ordinary economic routines. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]

Even ordinary material needs interpretation. A cooking pot may have been used for several foods; the survival of a tool does not identify its owner's gender; a larger house may reflect household size as well as wealth. Excavation samples are uneven, and some organic materials decay faster than metal or fired clay. Questions about household labor require multiple lines of evidence: use-wear, spatial distribution, botanical remains and comparisons between communities. A historian cannot turn one attractive object into the entire daily experience of a civilization.

This gives our story a larger purpose. The early Chinese states were neither just kings with bronze nor only villages producing grain. They were systems of interdependence in which elite ritual, agricultural production, exchange, kinship and coercion helped shape one another. To understand why a dynasty gained and lost authority, we must look at both the highly visible center and the people whose work sustained it. That is the same principle that helped us study ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, and it will be just as necessary when we later examine empires and modern states.
