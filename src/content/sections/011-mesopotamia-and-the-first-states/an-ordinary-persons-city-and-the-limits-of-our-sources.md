The people who fill archaeological museums are often kings, priests, and wealthy tomb owners. Everyday residents are less visible. Try to imagine an ordinary working household in an early city. Some members might farm plots outside the dense center; others prepare food, weave, tend animals, transport goods, clean vessels, repair tools, or work for an institution. Their routines would depend on age, status, household circumstances, season, and the particular city. These are plausible categories drawn from economic and archaeological evidence, not a diary recovered from one household.

Households were not all alike. Alongside institutional estates, there were forms of private property, exchange, personal debts, and commercial activity, especially well documented in later periods. Social rank could affect food access, legal standing, working obligations, and burial practices. Enslaved people existed in Mesopotamian societies, although forms of dependence, conditions, and their prevalence varied across time. It would be inaccurate to treat every ancient worker as an enslaved laborer or every ration recipient as a free wage earner in a modern sense.

Why are we cautious? Texts survive unevenly. Administrative tablets record the concerns of the institution that produced them. The archaeological remains of a palace or royal cemetery attract attention because the materials are distinctive. Most ordinary belongings were made of perishable or reusable materials, and many neighborhoods remain unexcavated. The surviving record emphasizes some people and underrepresents others.

An educated history reader should ask: Who left a record? Who was named? Whose labor is represented only by a number? What was never recorded? The first states created archives, but archives are not mirrors of everyone who lived under their authority. That insight will matter when we study taxation, imperial rule, labor and social hierarchies in every subsequent civilization.
