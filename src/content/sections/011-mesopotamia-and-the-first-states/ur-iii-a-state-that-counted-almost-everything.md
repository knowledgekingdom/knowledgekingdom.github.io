After Akkadian power waned, some southern Mesopotamian rulers reasserted control. The Third Dynasty of Ur, approximately 2112–2004 BCE in a common chronology, is especially well documented through administrative texts. Kings such as Ur-Nammu and Shulgi oversaw a highly organized state with extensive accounting, official correspondence, labor obligations and public building. The abundance of tablets makes this a particularly valuable period for studying administration rather than merely royal conquest. \[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-14" class="cite">14</a>\]

The surviving records can list workers, animals, fields, deliveries, and transfers. This is not proof that every act of daily life was centrally planned. Private practices and local variation continued. It does show an extraordinary administrative capacity and a strong interest in standardizing obligations. A state can become powerful by measuring land, directing labor, inspecting officials and organizing redistribution. The ruler may appear on a monument, but innumerable clerks and workers make the system function.

Around 2100 BCE, Ur-Nammu sponsored major temple projects, including the famous ziggurat at Ur. The building's original symbolic meanings cannot be known with certainty, and some reconstruction has changed the surviving physical form. Its enormous brick mass nonetheless testifies to organized work and the importance of religious architecture in royal representation. \[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]

This is the point at which our first storehouse story acquires its larger significance. Coordination can create impressive infrastructure and sustain many specialists. It can also burden households with record-keeping, corvée or other labor obligations and hierarchical controls. A sophisticated state is not automatically a just state. When we study state capacity later, we will return to the central question: <strong>capable of doing what, for whom, and at whose expense?</strong>
