The southern alluvial plain could provide agricultural products, fish, reeds and clay, but many raw materials important to metalwork, construction, and luxury goods had to be acquired through regional connections. The archaeological record at places such as Ur reveals imports that include lapis lazuli, carnelian and metals; evidence links Mesopotamian exchange to the Gulf, Iran, regions toward Afghanistan, and the Indus world. \[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]

What motivates such exchange? Some goods are functional: timber for larger buildings or metals for tools. Others signal power, religious devotion, wealth or diplomatic ties. Exchange can be commercial, redistributive, diplomatic, tributary or coercive; a foreign object cannot on its own reveal which arrangement brought it to a city. To determine that, researchers compare distribution patterns, texts, routes, materials and chronology.

Trade creates dependencies. If a city has abundant grain but limited copper, a distant mine or a trading intermediary matters. Long-distance networks expose people to opportunities and vulnerabilities: loss during transport, political conflict, shortages, unequal bargaining power, and sudden changes in demand. They also circulate technologies and artistic styles. An object made from a faraway stone is evidence that the city's world was larger than the fields visible from its walls.

Consider a simple hypothetical exchange: a river trader offers three units of wool cloth in return for one unit of imported metal. If metal becomes scarce and the rate changes to five units of cloth per metal unit, the trader faces a <strong>relative-price change</strong>. The ratio is not a documented Mesopotamian price schedule; it teaches an idea from Chapter 3 and the economics chapters to come. The world's first cities were not modern capitalist economies, but scarcity, bargaining, specialization, and shifting relative values were already part of social life.
