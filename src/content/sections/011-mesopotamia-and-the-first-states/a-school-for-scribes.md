Writing changes a society only if people can learn, reproduce, interpret and trust it. In later Mesopotamian cities, scribal training left physical evidence: practice tablets, sign lists, copied texts, lexical exercises and mathematical problems. At the Metropolitan Museum, an Old Babylonian student tablet shows the repeated copying of a divine name. That small object reveals an institution of instruction far more immediately than a grand royal inscription does. \[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]

Imagine an apprentice practicing wedge marks under the supervision of a teacher. This is an illustrative scene, not a documented individual lesson. The learner must memorize many signs and distinguish different readings. Some signs represent whole words; others represent syllables; the appropriate interpretation depends on language and context. Written Sumerian continued to be used for scholarly and religious purposes even as Akkadian became widespread. Learning to read and write was an investment in a specialized profession, not a hobby available equally to the whole population.

Scribes could serve different institutions: temples, palaces, merchants, households, and governments. Some recorded goods; others copied literary works, prepared correspondence, or worked with legal documents. A trained scribe might enjoy advantages of status and opportunity, but the profession also required discipline and expertise. We cannot simply estimate universal literacy rates from surviving school tablets, because their survival and discovery are highly uneven.

This story also suggests why records influence power. If you can read a land document while another person cannot, your ability gives you leverage—but it also imposes obligations and can be scrutinized by others who possess similar skills. Literacy may distribute knowledge more widely over time; in its early institutional setting, it could concentrate expertise. Understanding that tension will help us interpret schools, bureaucracies, law and communication technologies throughout later history.
