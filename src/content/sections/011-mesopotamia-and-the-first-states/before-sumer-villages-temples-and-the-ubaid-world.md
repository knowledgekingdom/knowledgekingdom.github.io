The southern cities did not appear suddenly around 3000 BCE. Agriculture and settlement existed in the wider region for millennia. Archaeologists identify a long Ubaid cultural horizon, approximately the sixth through fifth millennia BCE in southern Mesopotamia, characterized in part by distinctive pottery and developing settlement networks. At sites such as Eridu, successive religious buildings and settlement layers help researchers investigate how communities organized labor and ritual before the famous cities of the Uruk period. \[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]

The label <em>Ubaid</em> refers to an archaeological culture, not to a single uniform ethnicity or political state. Pots made in a characteristic style tell us something about practices and connections; they do not by themselves reveal everyone's language or identity. Nor do larger buildings prove that a hereditary king already ruled. A shared ceremonial center might coordinate labor through leaders, households, kin groups, or institutions that later changed into something more centralized. The archaeological evidence has to carry the conclusion rather than serve a predetermined story.

An important shift was increasing settlement differentiation. Some places became larger or more institutionally prominent than their neighbors. Craftwork, farming, fishing, herding, and seasonal exchange could coexist. Monumental structures might have required substantial coordination, but cooperation can arise for reasons beyond coercion: ritual obligations, shared identity, collective prestige, practical benefit, or unequal authority. A village where people build together is not identical to a government that collects taxes from unwilling subjects. Yet the ability to organize work beyond a household can become part of the toolkit of later states.

The lesson is chronological and causal. The origins of the city belong to a long prehistory of farming, institutional experimentation, environmental management, and interregional contact. The more interesting question is not 'Who woke up and decided to build a city?' It is 'What changes allowed some settlements to grow large enough that people needed new ways of living together?'
