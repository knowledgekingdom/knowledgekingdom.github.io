The Royal Cemetery of Ur, excavated in the twentieth century, produced remarkable burials from the mid-third millennium BCE. Gold, silver, lapis lazuli, musical instruments and complex burial arrangements testify to wealth, craft skill, international connections and differentiated status. The findings are historically important because they reveal social relationships that an economic tablet about barley could scarcely express. \[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]

Rich graves are tempting because they seem to let us enter an ancient world. But they can mislead. A cemetery may give greater visibility to exceptional households than to ordinary residents. A burial assemblage may represent ideals about rank and ritual rather than the deceased person's everyday possessions. And when archaeologists encounter multiple people buried together in unusual circumstances, they must distinguish the material evidence from interpretations about ritual death, violence or political obligation. Older excavators sometimes expressed more certainty than modern researchers would accept.

Here, inequality becomes visible in a new way. Different people were not commemorated with equal quantities or qualities of material goods. Yet one cemetery does not measure an entire city's wealth distribution. What proportion of ordinary households enjoyed comfortable lives? How did access to food vary? How much social mobility existed? We cannot calculate these answers from prestigious tombs alone. Other evidence—housing, food remains, administrative records, and bioarchaeology—must be brought into conversation.

Ur also shows why trade matters to political power. Imported prestige materials were not just decorations. They could communicate access to distant networks and the ability to concentrate labor and valuables. A ruler or elite household that controls rare materials can make authority visible. But the ability to collect spectacular objects should not distract us from the people who farmed, transported, spun, smelted, carved, and maintained the society that made such displays possible.
