We use the words <em>city</em>, <em>civilization</em>, <em>state</em>, <em>kingdom</em>, and <em>empire</em> almost interchangeably in casual conversation. History becomes clearer when we separate them. A <strong>city</strong> is a relatively dense settlement where many people and activities are concentrated. A <strong>state</strong> is an enduring political organization claiming authority over a population and territory, with institutions capable of making and enforcing collective decisions. A <strong>city-state</strong> links a principal urban center to the land, villages, and political authority around it. An <strong>empire</strong> extends rule over multiple territories or peoples, often through conquest, tribute, or unequal political relationships.

No single archaeological object proves that a state exists. A big temple is not a constitution; a wall is not a census; a seal is not a tax code. Researchers look for clusters of evidence: hierarchy among settlements, durable administrative practices, public works, regulation of resources, concentration of coercive authority, or institutions surviving the individuals who lead them. Definitions of the first state remain debated, partly because early power did not resemble a modern government with ministries and mapped borders.

The key word is <em>institution</em>. A ruler can die, but an office can continue. An individual may forget an obligation, but an archive can record it. A canal overseer may be replaced, but the rules for water distribution can persist. These arrangements allow societies to coordinate through time. They also make possible enduring inequalities: people who control records, land allocations, food supplies, and armed force can concentrate advantages that do not disappear when the harvest is over.

This is why the first cities and first states belong in the same discussion without being identical events. Urban growth can encourage administrative authority; rulers can promote urban concentration; warfare can make institutional organization more valuable; religious networks can organize resources; environmental management can reward cooperation. But none is a universal one-way cause. It is better to imagine interacting processes than a ladder with a single mandatory next step.
