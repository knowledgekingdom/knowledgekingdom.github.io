The earliest Mesopotamian states did not invent every aspect of government, and no unbroken line connects their exact institutions to ours. But they make certain enduring human problems unusually visible. How can people coordinate labor beyond family networks? Who controls a shared waterway? How can a promise survive the absence or death of the person who made it? What gives a ruler authority? When does administration become oppressive? Why do city-states cooperate, compete, or submit to larger empires?

These questions outlast the cities that first left us written answers. A modern budget is not a Uruk ration tablet, and a modern court is not Hammurabi's monument. Still, both involve scarce resources, recognized procedures, records, standards, competing claims and judgments about fairness. The value of comparing them is not to erase differences, but to see that institutions solve practical problems while creating new forms of power.

Mesopotamia's story also challenges a simplistic timeline of progress. The appearance of writing did not abolish injustice. The rise of cities did not guarantee liberty. Imperial unification did not end conflict. Yet these developments helped create new possibilities for art, exchange, technical knowledge, law and collective projects. Achievement and coercion could grow together. The same society could build a magnificent temple, preserve a beautiful poem and impose difficult obligations on people who left no written account of themselves.

Carry one sentence into the next chapter: <strong>civilization is not a switch that turns on when humans learn to farm; it is a changing arrangement of institutions, technologies, beliefs, and relationships whose benefits and costs are distributed unequally.</strong> Chapter 12 will take us to the Nile Valley, where a different environment and political trajectory gave rise to one of the world's most remarkable early states.
