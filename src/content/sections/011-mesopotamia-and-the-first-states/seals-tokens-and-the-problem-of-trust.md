Before a merchant could send an electronic invoice, a state could print a form, or a letter could be signed with a pen, administrators had to find ways to authenticate objects and agreements. Mesopotamian communities developed various devices for counting, labeling, and validating transactions. Small clay tokens appear in some early contexts; stamp seals and later cylinder seals could impress distinctive designs into wet clay. The historical connection between individual token forms and later written signs is a research question, not a perfectly documented straight line. \[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]

Imagine two people agreeing to send jars of oil from one settlement to another. They could count the jars in person, but an official at the destination needs assurance that the shipment is complete and has not been opened. A piece of clay bearing a seal impression can help establish whether a container or document has been disturbed. Seals are not magical guarantees: powerful people can abuse authority, and marks can be copied or misused. Still, they are mechanisms of accountability and identity. A distinctive image stands in for a person or institution that cannot be present everywhere at once.

Cylinder seals were especially versatile. A carved stone cylinder rolled over clay could leave a repeating scene, a person's identification, or a combination of both. The objects are tiny; the systems they supported could be large. Seals are evidence of administration, artistic expression, and the way material culture connects economic relationships with political or religious symbols. When you see an ancient seal in a museum, ask not only what the figures depict but also <em>what action the impression was meant to authorize</em>.

This is a useful lesson in institutional economics. Trust can be personal, but large networks often employ standardized evidence of commitments. Receipts, signatures, delivery tracking and digital authentication are modern technologies for related problems. The similarities lie in the problems being solved, not in any claim that Mesopotamian seals were exact predecessors of every modern system.
