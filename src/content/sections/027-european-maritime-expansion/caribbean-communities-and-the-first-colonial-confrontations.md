The peoples Columbus encountered had their own settlements, political relationships, agricultural systems and maritime routes. Taíno communities in the Caribbean grew crops, made canoes, traded among islands and maintained spiritual and social traditions. European texts reveal much about the newcomers' perceptions, but their descriptions can distort Indigenous realities through misunderstanding, prejudice or self-justification. Material culture, linguistic research and Indigenous continuities provide essential additional evidence. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

Early encounters varied. People exchanged goods and information; some local actors helped foreign visitors for their own reasons. But Spanish demands for labor, tribute, land and gold rapidly generated violence and coercion. Forced labor and enslavement formed part of early colonial practice. European-introduced diseases also had devastating effects, though exact population estimates and the relative contributions of disease, warfare, displacement and work regimes remain debated in particular places. Chapter 28 will investigate those demographic and ecological processes in depth. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

Calling these events 'contact' without identifying the imbalance of power can sanitize conquest. Calling every interaction the same form of violence erases the choices and differing situations of Indigenous people. A careful account recognizes negotiation, resistance, adaptation and catastrophic disruption in the same historical field. Indigenous Caribbean cultures were transformed, but not wholly erased: descendants and cultural continuities remain present today.
