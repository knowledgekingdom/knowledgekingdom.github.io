In 1500 a Portuguese expedition commanded by Pedro Álvares Cabral, bound for the Indian Ocean, reached the coast of what is now Brazil. The circumstances and degree of planning behind the landfall are discussed by historians; it is safer to describe what the voyage documented than to announce a proven secret discovery campaign. Portuguese claims soon encountered Indigenous societies whose histories were older than European maritime projects. The territory was neither vacant nor a single political unit. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

Early Portuguese interest concentrated on valuable coastal resources, especially brazilwood, and on maintaining a position in Atlantic networks. Later settlement, sugar cultivation and territorial expansion deepened forms of colonial control. The colony did not appear fully formed in the year of Cabral's landfall. Colonization unfolded through negotiations, alliances, conflict, missions and the exploitation of Indigenous and African labor. Local geography, epidemics and Indigenous responses shaped the limits and directions of settlement. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]

An important contrast emerges with Portugal's Asian network. In parts of Asia the Portuguese initially concentrated on fortified ports, passes and sea routes. In Brazil, over time, substantial land settlement and plantation production became central. It would be wrong to turn this distinction into an absolute rule, but it helps explain why a single empire could contain very different forms of government and economic dependence.
