Prince Henry of Portugal, often called 'the Navigator,' supported and participated in the politics of fifteenth-century Atlantic ventures, though he was not personally sailing on every expedition. Popular histories sometimes imagine a formal school of navigation at Sagres, assembled under his direction with scientists and maps sufficient to plan the entire process. That neat institutional story is much more certain in patriotic legend than in the surviving evidence. Henry's patronage, dynastic ambitions and involvement with African ventures were real; a single centralized academy directing all advances is difficult to demonstrate. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-6" class="cite">6</a>\]

Why do such legends persist? They make an extended process intelligible by assigning it a recognizable founder. Historians then inherit a tidy sequence: visionary prince, new scientific techniques, heroic captains, global empire. This hides merchants seeking profit, prisoners and enslaved captives, African negotiators, shipwrights, crew members and royal successors whose decisions altered plans. It also encourages a false contrast between supposedly scientific Europeans and technically passive societies elsewhere.

A good historical biography asks what a powerful sponsor could actually do. A prince might grant privileges, supply resources, authorize voyages and advance religious or commercial aims. He could not personally determine every wind, pilotage decision or overseas encounter. The history of maritime expansion was institutional and collaborative even when individual rulers and captains became its most famous faces.
