In 1532 Francisco Pizarro's expedition intervened in the Inca imperial world during a period of dynastic conflict following struggles between rival claimants. The capture of Atahualpa at Cajamarca became a dramatic episode in European accounts, but it cannot explain the entire conquest. The Inca state stretched across difficult mountainous environments and ruled diverse populations through roads, labor obligations and local authority. Rivalries, Indigenous resistance, cooperation, disease and the difficulties of imperial succession all influenced the outcome. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

Cuzco fell to Spanish-led forces in 1533, yet Inca resistance continued, including the Neo-Inca polity centered at Vilcabamba until 1572. This prolonged history matters because the word 'conquered' can conceal decades of struggle and negotiated authority. Early Spanish claims often exceeded actual power, especially in regions far from administrative centers. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

The Andean case also demonstrates the danger of importing one explanation from Mexico without examining different political and geographical conditions. The mountains, Inca administrative system, recent succession conflict and forms of local alliance were not identical to the lake region around Tenochtitlan. In both regions, however, European forces depended on Indigenous labor and political relationships rather than succeeding through a mysterious technological superiority alone.
