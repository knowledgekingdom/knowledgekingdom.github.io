Long before sixteenth-century European ships entered the Pacific, its island communities had developed extraordinary traditions of seafaring, navigation, settlement and exchange. Austronesian-speaking voyagers populated vast areas over many centuries, using knowledge of stars, winds, swells, birds and island environments. The Pacific was not an empty gap between Europe and Asia. Its networks often operated on geographic and social scales very different from those favored by European charts. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-27" class="cite">27</a>\]

The Magellan-Elcano expedition did not immediately create stable European government across the ocean. Subsequent Spanish efforts sought a durable route connecting the Americas to Asia. In 1565 Andrés de Urdaneta identified a return route across the northern Pacific using prevailing winds, helping support the Manila-Acapulco galleon connection that developed thereafter. From 1571 Spanish Manila became a major point of commercial interaction in the Philippines. Asian merchants, especially those connected with Chinese networks, were indispensable to the exchange of silks and other goods for American silver. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-27" class="cite">27</a>\]

Even there, labels such as 'Spanish Pacific' can obscure the practical reality: indigenous polities, local communities, regional merchants, religious institutions and environmental constraints shaped the empire. A long-lasting sea route required ports, sailors, supplies, credit and alliances on both sides of the ocean. It is more revealing to picture overlapping systems than a single European line drawn across a vacant sea.
