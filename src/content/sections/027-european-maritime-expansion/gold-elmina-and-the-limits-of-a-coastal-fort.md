In 1482 the Portuguese established the fortress of São Jorge da Mina, commonly called Elmina, on the Gold Coast in what is now Ghana. The site symbolized the importance of gold commerce and the desire to protect a royal trading position. Yet a fort's walls did not automatically govern large inland territories or guarantee the compliance of every nearby merchant. Trade depended on African producers, transporters, market networks and negotiations. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]

A coastal stronghold could store goods, shelter personnel, host a garrison and project force. Its effective reach was narrower than a later map shaded with an imperial color might suggest. Competition with other Europeans and changes among regional African powers repeatedly reshaped arrangements. The building itself might endure while its economic relationships changed. Consider the difference between a customs station collecting revenue, a fortress able to threaten ships, and a state administering farms and households. These are not equivalent powers.

The contrast matters beyond Elmina. Across the early Portuguese empire, administrators held particular ports, islands or fortified positions while relying on local actors for food, intelligence and market access. Maritime empire often began as a scattered network of points rather than a continuous belt of territory. Reading a map without understanding this difference creates an exaggerated picture of political control.
