Transoceanic expansion altered the flows of commodities and precious metals. Gold, spices, sugar, timber, dyewoods and later American silver connected producers and consumers across increasingly long distances. Trade created opportunities for merchants, investors and some port communities, but profits were unevenly distributed and often depended on coerced labor. The existence of growing trade does not imply that everyone involved experienced rising welfare. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

American silver became particularly significant in the sixteenth century. Mining required labor, infrastructure, transport and fiscal institutions. Silver traveled to European markets and through Pacific connections to Asia, where it met demand shaped by existing monetary and commercial systems. This was an interaction among several economic regions, not an automatic transfer of global wealth into one European treasury. States could receive windfalls and still face debt, inflationary pressures or military overextension. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

The wider consequences belong partly to Chapters 28–31 and the economics chapters. For the present, notice the causal sequence: a navigable route permits trade; investors and rulers organize extraction; labor arrangements shape costs and human suffering; merchants distribute goods; and governments struggle to tax or control the results. A shipping route changes possibilities, not the laws of economics.
