Christian conversion was a major stated objective of Iberian overseas governments and missions. Missionaries built schools and churches, learned Indigenous languages, opposed some abuses and at times participated in cultural suppression. The outcome differed across orders, regions and individuals. Religious encounter was neither solely a cover for profit nor automatically a benign cultural exchange. It was entangled with political power, genuine convictions, coercion, adaptation and Indigenous agency. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

One famous debate concerned whether Spaniards possessed the right to conquer and govern Indigenous peoples and what obligations followed. Bartolomé de las Casas condemned abuses, while Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda defended arguments for war and domination under specified circumstances. Their sixteenth-century controversy did not resemble a modern democratic referendum over colonialism, and neither man represented every opinion held in Spain or its territories. Yet it provides evidence that empire's legitimacy was contested within the colonizing society itself. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

The key analytical distinction is between a moral ideal and its political use. A ruler might claim conversion as the purpose of occupation; an official might demand labor; a missionary might resist that demand; and Indigenous converts might use religious institutions to pursue their own interests. A historian should investigate these relationships rather than decide in advance that every actor's professed motives were sincere or insincere.
