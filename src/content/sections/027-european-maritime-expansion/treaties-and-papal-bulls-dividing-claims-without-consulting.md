In 1493 papal bulls supported Castilian claims to overseas territories, and in 1494 Portugal and Castile agreed to the Treaty of Tordesillas, establishing a line of division for their competing claims outside Europe. The dividing meridian was described in relation to the Cape Verde islands, but measurement and geographic uncertainty complicated its interpretation. The agreement later mattered for Portugal's claims in parts of South America, including Brazil. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-23" class="cite">23</a>\]

The treaty was not an agreement by the peoples living in the regions concerned. It expressed a framework of rivalry among two European crowns operating within a particular Christian diplomatic world. Other European monarchs did not necessarily accept Iberian exclusivity, while Indigenous American and African societies had their own political authority. Treating a treaty line as the creation of legitimate universal ownership would be to reproduce one side's ideology rather than explain history.

This is a case study in distinguishing normative claims from enforcement. A ruler can declare sovereignty, secure a religious endorsement and issue a map without possessing the soldiers, administrators, local alliances or economic leverage required to rule. This difference between legal entitlement as asserted by a government and practical power will become central in later chapters on colonialism and international law.
