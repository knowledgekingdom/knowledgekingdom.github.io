Our sources include royal charters, diplomatic correspondence, shipping accounts, travel narratives, early printed maps, archaeology, Indigenous texts and oral historical traditions. Each preserves some information and excludes other perspectives. Columbus's letters can tell us what he reported or wished patrons to believe; they cannot alone reconstruct the whole life of a Taíno village. A conquistador's claim that victory came from his own brilliance requires testing against Indigenous alliances and material realities. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-19" class="cite">19</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]

Some disputes are about evidence, others about language or values. Historians agree on the occurrence of major voyages while debating particular motivations, population counts or the extent of a ruler's real control. The word 'discovery' is defensible in a qualified discussion of what became known to European readers, but misleading if presented as the world's first awareness of inhabited lands. Similarly, 'exploration' describes geographic inquiry but does not neutralize conquest, enslavement or forced settlement. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

Public memory tends to swing between heroic celebration and total condemnation. A more demanding approach neither hides the technical achievements of sailors nor uses those achievements to excuse violence. It asks which outcomes are documented, who acted, who suffered, what alternatives were available and how later societies have interpreted the history. The ability to hold multiple supported truths together is one of the core aims of Knowledge Kingdom.
