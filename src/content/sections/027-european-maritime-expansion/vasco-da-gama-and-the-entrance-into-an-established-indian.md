Vasco da Gama's expedition sailed from Portugal in 1497 and reached the Malabar coast of India in 1498. Its arrival at Calicut did not constitute Europe's first contact with Asia: Asian goods and knowledge had crossed multiple commercial networks into Europe for centuries. What changed was the creation of an Atlantic-Cape route linking European vessels directly to Indian Ocean ports. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]

On the East African coast, Portuguese ships encountered established Swahili trading cities connected to Arabian, Persian and Indian merchants. The expedition depended on local navigation knowledge and negotiated contacts, sometimes amid suspicion and hostility. At Calicut, the existing commercial order did not rearrange itself to welcome an unknown royal delegation. Political authorities, traders and brokers assessed Portuguese intentions against the interests and obligations of their own port society. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]

The early voyages were neither a simple triumph of friendly commerce nor an immediate conquest of the Indian Ocean. The Portuguese attempted to use naval force to compel favorable terms, while local rulers and merchants resisted, bargained or sought alliances. Chapter 26 explored the resulting system in greater detail. Here the essential question is how one new passage altered the incentives of many actors without eliminating older routes through the Red Sea, Persian Gulf and other markets.
