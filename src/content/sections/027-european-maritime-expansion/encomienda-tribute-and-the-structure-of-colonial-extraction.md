Spanish settlers and officials devised institutions to channel Indigenous tribute and labor toward colonial interests. The encomienda granted colonists rights to receive specified tribute or labor from Indigenous communities under conditions officially framed in terms of protection and Christian instruction. It did not, in principle, mean straightforward legal ownership of all the people concerned. In practice it could support severe exploitation, compulsion and abuses. Distinctions between legal categories and actual experiences are essential. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

A crown may formally forbid certain practices while officials and settlers evade regulations far from central supervision. Colonial courts could sometimes offer arenas for Indigenous legal petitions even while the broader order remained coercive. Missionaries and officials disagreed over the treatment and status of Indigenous inhabitants, but these debates occurred within imperial projects that already involved conquest and land claims. A document announcing reform is evidence of a proposed or imposed rule, not by itself evidence of effective protection. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

Colonial production was diverse. Mines, ranches, plantations and towns relied on different mixtures of coerced and paid labor, and Indigenous communities adapted livelihoods to survive or negotiate demands. The movement of American silver into Atlantic and Pacific circuits will connect this discussion to later chapters on money, world trade and the economic effects of empire.
