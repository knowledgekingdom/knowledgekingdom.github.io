Atlantic island settlement transformed landscapes through forest clearance, crop cultivation, grazing animals and the construction of mills, ports and roads. Sugar required land, water, labor and processing equipment. Ecological transformations could increase export production while imposing costs on soil, vegetation, local food security and communities whose access to land had changed. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-11" class="cite">11</a>\]

In the Americas, European maritime expansion led to the movement of plants, animals and microorganisms across previously separated ecological systems. The consequences would be uneven and sometimes catastrophic. Wheat, cattle, horses and sugarcane entered new environments, while American crops later became important in Europe, Africa and Asia. These changes interacted with Indigenous agriculture and colonial policy rather than replacing every local practice in one step. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

Chapter 28, The Columbian Exchange, will investigate these biological and demographic transformations systematically. Here we should retain one principle: maritime transport moved organisms as well as people and merchandise. A ship could import a crop that enriched a landowner, an animal that transformed the local environment, or a pathogen that harmed communities that never chose to participate in the voyage.
