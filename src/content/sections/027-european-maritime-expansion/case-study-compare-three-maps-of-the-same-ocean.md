Picture three maps of the Atlantic around 1500. The first is a nautical chart designed for pilots. It marks coastlines, compass bearings, hazards and ports useful for navigation. The second is a royal political map that colors islands and coasts according to claims and treaties. The third is a modern historian's reconstruction of African and Indigenous American polities, settlements and exchange networks at the same date. None is automatically false, but each answers different questions. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-22" class="cite">22</a>\]

The pilot chart may be more useful for sailing than for estimating the size of a kingdom. The royal map may document an official ambition but overstate effective control. The historical reconstruction may better represent local societies yet rely on uncertain archaeological dating or uneven written evidence. By comparing them, students can see that a map is an argument about what matters as much as a picture of where things are. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]

Try applying this approach to a map of the Treaty of Tordesillas. Ask whether the line was positioned with the precision that modern cartography suggests, what areas European diplomats actually understood, and how coastal inhabitants might view its proposed authority. The result is not cynicism about maps; it is better geographic literacy.
