Accounts of early voyages often center kings, navigators and military commanders because they produced the best-preserved royal letters, maps and memoirs. Women were nonetheless involved in maritime and colonial societies as landholders, artisans, market traders, workers, spouses, political negotiators, household leaders, missionaries and enslaved persons. Their opportunities and constraints differed according to locality, status, legal position and cultural setting. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-20" class="cite">20</a>\]

Some people made the labor of shipping possible without ever boarding a transoceanic vessel. Forest workers supplied timber; rope makers and sailmakers outfitted ships; farmers and bakers supplied food. In overseas ports, interpreters, Indigenous guides, water carriers and builders could determine whether an expedition survived. In the Americas, Indigenous women's experiences of conquest included violence and exploitation but also political mediation, survival strategies and adaptation. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-18" class="cite">18</a>\]

An archive is not a population census. A captain's detailed log may include many officers but mention a local translator only briefly. The scarcity of surviving testimony from workers or captives should lead to careful reconstruction from different sources, not to the claim that such people were marginal. A mature account of maritime expansion asks who did the work and who bore the costs, not only who received honors after the ship returned home.
