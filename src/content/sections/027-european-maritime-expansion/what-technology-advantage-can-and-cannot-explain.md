European vessels carried particular combinations of sails, artillery and navigational tools. These could offer advantages in certain encounters. Cannon-equipped ships could threaten coastal targets; horses altered some battlefields; improved charts helped repeat voyages. But outcomes were never reducible to an automatic European technology advantage. European crews depended on non-European pilots, supplies, political allies and market knowledge, while opponents adapted or used different advantages. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

Geography could reverse a presumed advantage. A sea-going cannon is less useful in a mountainous interior. A large ship designed for ocean crossings may not enter a shallow local harbor. A few firearms cannot replace thousands of allied fighters, regular provisions or a governable administrative system. In the Indian Ocean, established merchant institutions persisted despite Portuguese attempts to use force. In Mexico, Indigenous alliances and epidemic conditions mattered alongside European weapons. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

The better question is conditional: which technology, used by whom, in which environment, against which institutions, and for what purpose? Avoid choosing between the equally oversimplified claims that European domination was entirely technological or that technology made no difference. Explanation requires interaction among tools, organization, information and politics.
