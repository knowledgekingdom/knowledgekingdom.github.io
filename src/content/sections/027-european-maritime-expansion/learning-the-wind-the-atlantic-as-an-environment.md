The Atlantic is not a flat blue highway. Prevailing winds, ocean currents and seasonal weather create advantages for some courses and dangers for others. Portuguese sailors gradually learned that a return trip from parts of western Africa might require steering far into the open Atlantic to catch more favorable winds before turning toward Europe. This practice is commonly called the volta do mar, the 'turn of the sea.' It exemplifies a crucial point: the shortest distance on a map is not always the fastest or safest sailing route. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]

Further west, tropical trade winds aided some Atlantic crossings, while higher-latitude westerlies could support other journeys. Knowledge came from repeated voyages, observations, missed landfalls and sometimes disaster. The ocean's geography shaped imperial geography: islands that offered freshwater or repair stations gained importance, and navigable bays became strategic sites. A sea route could be viable for one kind of vessel or one season but dangerous for another. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-5" class="cite">5</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]

European descriptions sometimes speak of 'opening' the ocean. That metaphor is useful only when we remember that people on Atlantic coasts, including African and American societies, already possessed maritime expertise in their own environments. The technological change was the creation of repeated transoceanic connections under particular European political sponsorship, not humanity's first discovery that water could be crossed.
