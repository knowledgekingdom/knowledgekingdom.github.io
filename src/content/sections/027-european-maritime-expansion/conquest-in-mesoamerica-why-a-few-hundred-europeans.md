In 1519 Hernán Cortés entered the political world of central Mexico, where the Mexica-led Triple Alliance exerted power over numerous communities. The conquest of Tenochtitlan in 1521 is often portrayed as a miraculous victory by a tiny European force over an enormous Indigenous empire. That version is deeply misleading. Cortés's forces operated with substantial Indigenous allies, including peoples who opposed Mexica tribute demands or sought advantage in local struggles. Armed confrontations were accompanied by diplomacy, intelligence gathering, siege warfare and devastating epidemic disease. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

The Mexica were neither primitive nor politically uniform. Tenochtitlan was a populous urban center, with markets, causeways, temples and a sophisticated system of lake-based transport. Its leaders responded to rapidly changing circumstances within a complex regional political order. European weapons and horses could influence encounters, but they do not by themselves explain the fall of the city. Indigenous military participation and the decisions of local elites were central. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-28" class="cite">28</a>\]

The collapse of Mexica imperial power did not mean that the thousands of communities across Mexico instantly became culturally or administratively Spanish. Indigenous languages, legal practices, towns and elites continued to shape colonial society. Later colonial records contain Indigenous petitions, maps and histories that help correct conquerors' triumphalist accounts. Historical explanation becomes stronger when it identifies multiple causal mechanisms rather than crediting a single captain's daring.
