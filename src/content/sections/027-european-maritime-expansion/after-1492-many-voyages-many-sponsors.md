Once Atlantic crossings became repeatable, exploration multiplied. Voyages associated with John Cabot under English sponsorship in 1497, Portuguese navigators in the North Atlantic, and later French expeditions sought commercial opportunities, fishing grounds and routes toward Asia. Spanish and Portuguese ventures expanded along South American coasts. Navigators were often international specialists employed by dynastic sponsors: a person's birthplace did not always match the political flag of the expedition. \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-24" class="cite">24</a>\]

The search for a northern passage to Asia became attractive because Iberian claims and difficult southern routes limited options for rivals. But a northern sailing route was not a promise of easy trade. Ice, shallow waters, uncertain currents and lack of reliable maps made voyages risky. North Atlantic fisheries and coastal commerce could prove more immediately useful than reaching China or the Moluccas. The economic returns of these journeys should be studied on their own terms rather than judged only by whether a legendary passage was found.

English and French claims increasingly challenged the idea that papal endorsements and Iberian agreements could divide the planet. Claims did not instantly produce large settlements. Many early efforts involved seasonal voyages and fragile coastal footholds. Later centuries would see more durable colonies and violent imperial rivalries, topics to which Chapters 28–29 and subsequent geopolitical chapters will return.
