In a <em>settler colony</em>, significant numbers of incoming settlers sought durable access to land and political privilege. Their interests could encourage confiscation, restrictive labor laws and racial segregation in ways that differed from colonies primarily administered for commerce or strategic advantage. European settler populations were unevenly distributed, and the term does not imply that every white resident possessed equal wealth or political authority. It identifies a broad political relationship around residence, land and governance.

In Kenya, struggles over land in the highlands and labor systems developed under British rule. In Algeria, European settler communities played major roles in politics and patterns of dispossession. In Southern Africa, settler states and mining capitalism deeply reshaped landholding and migration. Across these settings, colonial states sometimes built legal categories that denied African communities equal standing in courts, property transactions, representation or movement.

A history focused only on European settlers can still miss African choices. People challenged dispossession in courts, organized associations, migrated, adapted farming methods and preserved local claims through oral or written records. Land politics also existed among African communities independently of colonization, and not all local disputes began with a European decree. The point is not to romanticize precolonial land relations; it is to recognize how an imposed racial hierarchy and unequal coercive power changed the terms on which conflicts were resolved.
