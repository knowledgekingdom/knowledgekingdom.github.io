The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 made the eastern Mediterranean and Red Sea central to oceanic shipping between Europe and Asia. Egypt's rulers had undertaken ambitious modernization and infrastructure programs, but mounting debt and foreign financial control brought political conflicts. In 1882 Britain occupied Egypt after an uprising led by the nationalist officer Ahmad Urabi and other opponents of foreign domination and the khedival political order. Although the Ottoman-Egyptian constitutional situation was complex, Britain became the dominant occupying power for decades.

Egypt illustrates how informal financial leverage could lead to military control. Creditors and diplomats used debt servicing, administration and concern for commercial routes to justify intervention. But the country's politics cannot be reduced to foreigners reacting to financial statements. Egyptians had debates about constitutional authority, military pay, taxation, national dignity and the distribution of power. The canal mattered enormously, but the occupation also grew out of the local crisis and imperial decisions.

The Nile system connected the Egyptian question with Sudan, where a new Mahdist movement challenged Egyptian and later British ambitions. Controlling an imperial route did not necessarily entail controlling every place nearby, but European strategists often feared that rivals would use political vacuums to threaten transport or prestige. Such arguments could transform a strategic corridor into a much larger territorial commitment.
