The Scramble for Africa accelerated when changing technology, capitalist commerce, European geopolitical rivalry and local political conditions interacted. The Berlin Conference helped regulate claims without deciding every frontier. Conquest required campaigns, treaties, administrative coercion and African intermediaries. It frequently produced violence and exploitation, including genocide, but colonial systems differed greatly by region and time. African leaders and communities fought, bargained, adapted and maintained political aspirations under severe constraints.

No single cause explains every invasion, and no single category captures all African responses. The same demand for minerals or crops could lead to commercial relationships in one district, armed occupation in another and profitable African smallholder production in a third. Effective historical analysis asks who exercised power, who benefited, who carried the costs, and how much the surviving sources let us know. It rejects both imperial celebration and the opposite error of reducing African peoples to victims without political initiative.

Three questions carry this chapter forward. <strong>First</strong>, how did nationalism transform the European states competing for empire? <strong>Second</strong>, how did imperial rivalries contribute to diplomatic instability before 1914? <strong>Third</strong>, how did Africans eventually turn cultural, religious, economic and political institutions into movements for self-government? Chapters 36–41 will return to these questions from multiple directions. Remember that the map records a claim to rule; human history is the story of the people who confronted it.
