Why did so many independent African polities lose sovereignty? One explanation focuses on material asymmetries: financial resources, modern weapons, shipping, industrial production and the ability to replace losses. A second emphasizes political fragmentation: rival African rulers sometimes made alliances with invading forces to secure advantages against competitors. A third stresses international diplomacy: European states negotiated between themselves over large regions without giving African governments equal standing.

These explanations reinforce one another but have limits. Material advantages did not guarantee victories at Isandlwana or Adwa, and African leaders could be formidable administrators and tacticians. Alliances with Europeans could reflect reasonable strategies under uncertainty, not a unique African tendency toward division; European powers themselves competed and collaborated unpredictably. The denial of sovereignty in diplomacy was important, yet military occupation and ongoing local compliance still had to be won or imposed.

A more complete model treats conquest as a changing process. Early commercial contacts alter opportunities and rivalries; colonial claims invite local bargains and resistance; conflicts affect the finances and reputation of governments; armies depend on local allies and transport; settlements generate further disputes. The outcome depends on contingent decisions and feedback, not a timeless ranking of civilizations. Explaining colonial conquest is not the same as approving it.
