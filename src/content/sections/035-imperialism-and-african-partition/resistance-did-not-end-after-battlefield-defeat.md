Conquest could destroy an army and leave the underlying political conflict unresolved. Communities resisted tax collectors, evaded labor recruitment, fled to neighboring districts, retained banned rituals, challenged local collaborators or refused crop demands. Some organized large rebellions; others pursued petitions, legal claims, religious associations or newspapers. These forms should not be ranked solely by the number of soldiers involved. A boycott or an alternative school could alter colonial administration without producing a conventional battle.

Colonial records frequently called opponents 'rebels,' 'bandits' or 'troublemakers,' categories that reveal the perspective of a government seeking to claim legitimacy. But local sources may also contain factional bias. People who resisted one policy could cooperate on another; some joined colonial armies or accepted clerical employment while opposing discriminatory laws. Life under empire was made of recurring negotiations rather than permanent allegiance to only two camps.

The Maji Maji war, the struggles of the Ovaherero and Nama, the Asante campaigns and Ethiopian resistance illustrate a spectrum of political action. Survival did not depend only on armed success. Communities rebuilt institutions, adjusted political language and preserved memories that later shaped nationalist movements. The transition from conquest to anticolonial nationalism was neither immediate nor uniform; it took new organizations, global wars, changing political ideas and many different local efforts.
