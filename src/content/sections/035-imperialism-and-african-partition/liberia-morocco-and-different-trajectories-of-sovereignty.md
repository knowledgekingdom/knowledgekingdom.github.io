Liberia, established through a complex nineteenth-century history linked to settlement by free Black people from the United States and the American Colonization Society, also retained formal independence through the Scramble. Its institutions, however, were shaped by tensions between Americo-Liberian elites and Indigenous populations, foreign commercial interests, and unequal relations with powerful states. Independence on paper did not guarantee an inclusive political order or freedom from external pressure.

North Africa followed somewhat different timelines from much of sub-Saharan Africa. France invaded Algeria beginning in 1830, and military conquest, settler colonization, land confiscation and resistance unfolded over decades. Tunisia became a French protectorate in 1881, while Morocco came under French and Spanish protectorates in 1912. Ottoman connections and regional reform efforts shaped North African politics. Libya was invaded by Italy in 1911, although effective control met prolonged resistance and fluctuated after the initial coastal occupation.

These histories complicate the familiar classroom statement that only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent by 1914. As a broad description of sovereignty after the partition it is useful, but it conceals differences among protectorates, spheres of influence, colonies, nominal sovereignty and actual external control. It also risks overlooking that powerful African states were themselves sometimes hierarchical empires incorporating diverse populations. Precise terms produce a better account than a map with only two colors.
