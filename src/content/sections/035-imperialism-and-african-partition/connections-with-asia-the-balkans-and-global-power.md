Africa was central to the nineteenth-century world, but its partition cannot be understood in isolation. The same industrial networks that supported European power in Africa sustained economic and strategic competition in Asia. Control of Egypt and Suez connected Mediterranean politics to Indian Ocean routes and British interests in India. Imperial armies employed people recruited from different territories, and commodities traded through global credit systems whose pricing and finance were often arranged in European cities.

For readers familiar with the Balkans, useful comparisons exist but must be handled carefully. The Ottoman Empire's territorial retreat in southeastern Europe and European diplomatic management of the Eastern Question also involved outside powers treating local territories as strategic objects. Yet the national movements, imperial legal frameworks, religious histories and demographic patterns differed substantially from African colonial conquest. Similarity in diplomatic rivalry does not mean all imperial relationships were equivalent.

By 1914 colonial possessions formed part of the political geography of European alliance politics and economic power, though it would be inaccurate to explain the First World War solely as a struggle over colonies. Empires became sites of wartime recruitment, military campaigning and raw material extraction. Chapters on nationalism and the world wars will trace how these global relationships transformed, opening new political arguments about self-determination and the legitimacy of empire.
