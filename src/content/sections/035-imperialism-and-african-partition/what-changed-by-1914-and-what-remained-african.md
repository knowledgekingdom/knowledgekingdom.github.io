By 1914 most of Africa was formally claimed by European empires, with notable exceptions. Systems of law, taxation, labor, transport and education had been reorganized under unequal political authority. Land was seized in many regions; wars and colonial repression caused immense suffering; commodity exports and some infrastructure expanded. Maps changed quickly, but local administration remained uneven. Colonial governments often depended on the African people they attempted to subordinate.

Much also persisted. Languages, religious scholarship, farming knowledge, trade relationships and family networks adapted under new conditions. Communities retained and revised political memories, while some Africans used schools, courts, commercial networks and newspapers to challenge the legitimacy of imperial rule. These continuities matter because colonialism did not wipe away centuries of African social history. Nor should the existence of cultural resilience soften our assessment of coercion, dispossession and violence.

The political legacies extended far beyond 1914: compulsory work systems, unequal land ownership, administrative borders, segregated cities, new educated elites, organized anticolonial movements and historical claims about restitution. But the decades after independence brought new policies, conflicts and achievements. Modern African nations cannot be reduced to the circumstances of their colonization. A rigorous history explains inherited constraints while preserving the agency of later generations.
