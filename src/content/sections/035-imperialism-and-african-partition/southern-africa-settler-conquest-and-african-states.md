European domination in southern Africa had histories stretching back to Dutch settlement at the Cape in the seventeenth century and later British expansion. Land seizure, warfare, agricultural settlement, indentured labor and mineral discoveries transformed the region before the conventional dates of the Scramble. The discovery of diamonds and gold intensified struggles over land, labor recruitment and control of transport and taxation. British imperial ambitions intersected with independent Boer republics and African kingdoms, making the conflicts more complicated than a simple Europe-versus-Africa confrontation.

In 1879 the Zulu kingdom initially inflicted a major defeat on British troops at Isandlwana, demonstrating military organization and the vulnerability of invading forces. The British eventually defeated the Zulu kingdom in the same war, but later territorial arrangements, civil conflict and colonial intervention continued to reshape the region. The example exposes a familiar historical mistake: military superiority in a long campaign does not mean the winning side faced no serious reverses.

In South Africa, dispossession and labor controls eventually formed foundations for increasingly codified racial segregation, culminating in apartheid in the twentieth century. The 1913 Natives Land Act, after the Union of South Africa was formed in 1910, sharply restricted African land rights. A comprehensive history must trace these developments over time rather than imagining that all later institutions existed fully formed in 1880. African communities resisted, adapted economically, maintained political traditions and developed new organizations under mounting constraints.
