Colonial officials generated maps, tax returns, correspondence, censuses, military reports and judicial records. These documents reveal much, but they also served administration. An officer might exaggerate an enemy's numbers to claim military credit; a concession company might understate forced labor; a district report might count tax-paying households rather than the full population. Missionaries could document abuse while translating African religious practices through their own assumptions. A photograph may reveal a real injury but conceal who took it, when and why.

African sources expand and challenge this record. Oral histories preserve memories of chiefs, families, treaties and migrations, although narratives can change with later politics. Islamic manuscripts, petitions, newspapers and letters document intellectual and political activity; art, architecture, landscapes and archaeology show practices that written sources omit. Linguistic evidence can trace contacts and changing identities. Good history combines source types and openly states uncertainty.

Consider the simple claim, 'The colonial government ended slavery in this district in 1900.' A law can prove a formal prohibition was announced, but not that coercion disappeared. Court records, employment contracts, testimony and settlement patterns may reveal new forms of dependency. Similarly, a railway budget proves expenditure, but not who benefited. Knowledge Kingdom's broader lesson applies: <strong>the source is evidence for a particular question, not automatic proof of an entire narrative</strong>.
