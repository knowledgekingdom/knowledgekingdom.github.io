In Sudan, the religious leader Muhammad Ahmad declared himself the Mahdi in 1881 and mobilized a movement against Egyptian and foreign authority. His forces captured Khartoum in 1885. The Mahdist state that followed possessed its own political and military organization, religious legitimacy and internal tensions. It was not merely a gap between Egyptian and British administrations. Successive Mahdist leaders confronted famine, regional opposition and the demands of state-building while European powers reconsidered the strategic position of the Nile.

In 1898 an Anglo-Egyptian force under Horatio Kitchener defeated Mahdist forces at Omdurman, deploying heavy firepower and logistical resources. The crushing battlefield defeat does not justify reducing the entire conflict to a single moment of military technology. The Mahdist movement had already transformed a vast region, and later Anglo-Egyptian rule continued to face resistance and complex negotiations with local authorities.

Also in 1898, British and French imperial forces met at Fashoda in a diplomatic confrontation over the upper Nile. The episode illustrated how European territorial ambitions might produce a crisis between powers that had no right to assume sovereignty over the local population. France ultimately withdrew, and the two states worked toward a broader settlement of rival claims. The context explains why imperial borders were frequently shaped not by African political wishes but by European strategic bargains.
