A colonial flag did not administer a village. European states governed enormous territories with relatively few expatriate officials. In many British African colonies, administrators relied on local rulers, chiefs and courts through forms commonly called <em>indirect rule</em>. In some regions they worked through institutions with long-established legitimacy; elsewhere they appointed or strengthened individuals who had not previously held comparable authority. This could turn flexible political systems into rigid bureaucratic hierarchies.

French colonial ideologies sometimes emphasized direct administration and the possibility of assimilation into French institutions, but practice varied greatly. Chiefs, interpreters and local elites remained crucial. Portuguese rule also differed across regions and periods, with particularly extensive coercive labor arrangements in several territories. German colonial policies changed from early company rule to stronger government control in the wake of resistance. The distinction between direct and indirect rule is therefore useful but not a complete explanation.

Colonial governing coalitions included African clerks, soldiers, intermediaries, merchants and officials who could both benefit from and resist imperial structures. Their participation does not negate the coercive power imbalance. A community might comply with tax collection while quietly undermining a labor demand; an official might use a colonial court to pursue local interests; a chief might protect some dependents while exploiting others. Governance was an unstable political relationship rather than the frictionless execution of decisions made in London or Paris.
