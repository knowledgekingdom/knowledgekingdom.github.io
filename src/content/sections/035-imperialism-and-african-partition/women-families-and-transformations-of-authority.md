Women shaped trade, food production, craft manufacture, household economies, political negotiation and resistance both before and during colonial rule. Colonial administrations often represented African politics through male chiefs and household heads, sometimes narrowing women's access to formal decision-making even where older institutions had recognized female commercial or ritual authority. Gender relations were also influenced by missionary ideas, new labor migrations, taxation and changes in property law.

Colonial labor demands could separate workers from families for long periods. Migrant men in mining or plantation zones sent money and goods home, but their absence could increase the work and vulnerability of women and children. In commercial farming areas, women sometimes took on larger responsibility for food crops while male labor or income shifted toward exports. Other households used cash-crop markets and education to expand opportunities. A single label such as 'women under colonialism' conceals differences of class, region, age and legal status.

Resistance also had a gendered history. Yaa Asantewaa was a political leader in the struggle against Britain; women participated in collective protests, boycotts, food supply and religious movements elsewhere. A history that counts only battles led by male commanders underestimates the daily practices through which communities preserved autonomy or challenged authority. The more comprehensive question is who performed essential social and political work, and how colonial policy altered its value.
