Today's map of Africa owes much to the colonial partition, but modern states are not simply frozen copies of maps drawn at Berlin. Frontiers evolved through later treaties, wartime transfers, administrative revisions and post-independence political decisions. Language, religion, trade and kinship connections often crossed the resulting lines. Some border regions became arenas of smuggling, migration and political conflict; others created opportunities for trading communities that knew how to navigate several states.

After independence, many African governments sought to maintain inherited international boundaries. The Organization of African Unity, founded in 1963, endorsed respect for existing borders as part of an effort to avoid dangerous interstate territorial disputes. This choice may look contradictory if colonial borders had been imposed unjustly, but revision could generate competing claims among states with mixed populations and complex historical sovereignties. An inherited border can be criticized for its origins while retaining practical importance for peace.

Political outcomes today cannot be explained by colonial borders alone. Institutions, ecological pressures, demographic change, domestic political leadership, international markets, wars, education and many other factors contribute. Contemporary research has also complicated the idea that colonial lines were always drawn without reference to existing African states. The essential historical claim is not that every problem comes from a ruler's pencil, but that colonial partition transformed the rules through which African political communities related to land and one another. \[<a href="/035-imperialism-and-african-partition/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]
