Colonial economies were frequently reorganized around commodities demanded by distant markets: cocoa and groundnuts in parts of West Africa, cotton in several colonies, copper and gold in mining areas, rubber in central Africa, and other crops and minerals. Local societies were not passive suppliers. Farmers, merchants and workers adopted or resisted new activities, and local enterprise sometimes generated substantial wealth. Yet the structure of taxation, land access, prices and labor law shaped who could benefit.

In many places colonial authorities favored export transport corridors while giving less attention to food security, rural health or internal trade. Monoculture could increase vulnerability to weather, pests and price changes; mining could damage landscapes and create crowded labor settlements. It is nevertheless inaccurate to claim that colonization always created only one crop or that Africans never profited from trade. Economic outcomes varied with soils, access to markets, bargaining power, political institutions and whether production was coerced or voluntary.

Environmental history adds a further dimension. New crop regimes changed forest cover, grazing patterns, irrigation and disease ecologies. Colonial rules sometimes restricted access to land and forests while claiming to preserve nature, although local communities had long used sophisticated environmental management practices. What counted as 'unused' land often depended on administrative definitions that ignored seasonal use, sacred sites or shifting cultivation.
