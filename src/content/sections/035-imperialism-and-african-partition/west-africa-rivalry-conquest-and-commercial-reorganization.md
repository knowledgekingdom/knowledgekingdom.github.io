West Africa experienced multiple intersecting histories, not a single uninterrupted advance by colonial armies. French expeditions expanded from Senegal into the western Sudan, British authorities pursued claims along the coast and Niger, and merchants and rulers confronted changes in the terms of trade. Coastal and inland political actors sometimes made commercial accommodations while resisting the loss of territorial autonomy. The Niger River was especially significant as a transport artery, commercial network and symbol of claimed sovereignty.

The Royal Niger Company, chartered by Britain in 1886, made treaties and asserted a sphere of influence before British government administration replaced much company rule around the turn of the century. Britain's conquest of the Sokoto Caliphate's core territories in 1903 did not erase local Islamic scholarship or authority, but it reorganized power through colonial institutions. British rule also relied on different arrangements in coastal Lagos and other regions, so 'Nigeria' was not a single pre-existing political unit awaiting an administrator.

In French-controlled territories, military figures, administrators and local rulers pursued different strategies. The French model often proclaimed assimilation and republican ideals but in practice used hierarchy, appointed local intermediaries, coercive taxation and legal distinctions. The structure of power was shaped by bargaining, military budgets and the practical difficulty of governing vast territories. For each territory, distinguish the date of a European claim from the date of effective conquest and the everyday realities of colonial government.
