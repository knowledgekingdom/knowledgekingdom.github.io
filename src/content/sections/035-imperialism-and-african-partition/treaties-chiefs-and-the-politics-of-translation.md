European agents often collected written 'treaties' with African rulers as evidence of sovereignty. A document might use phrases such as protection, trade friendship, land sale or cession of sovereign rights. But the meaning of these terms was not always shared. Interpreters could reshape messages; officials might exploit differences between written and oral political cultures; rulers might see a treaty as a limited alliance or permission to trade rather than the abandonment of independence.

Yet African leaders were not uniformly deceived or powerless. Some deliberately used European partners against local rivals, sought weapons and guarantees, or attempted to preserve autonomy by accepting a protectorate relationship. Others refused agreements, organized coalitions or repudiated terms when their consequences became clear. Rulers operated in constrained circumstances, facing rivals and uncertain information while trying to safeguard their communities. Local leaders could also bargain for advantages that harmed competing African groups.

The evidential problem is substantial. If we have only an official European text and a later colonial interpretation, how do we recover what African participants thought they had agreed to? Historians examine alternative translations, testimony, oral histories, subsequent diplomatic conduct and patterns of resistance. A treaty is an important source, but a signature alone does not prove informed consent to everything later claimed in its name.
