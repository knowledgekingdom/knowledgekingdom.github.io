Missionary activity expanded in many regions during the nineteenth century, though Christian communities had existed in parts of Africa for many centuries. Schools, printing, clinics and new literate publics accompanied some missions. African converts became teachers, catechists, journalists and organizers with their own priorities. They could use Christian teachings to criticize colonial injustice as well as support reforms that missionaries themselves hesitated to endorse. African religious life was never simply a choice between 'European Christianity' and an unchanged precolonial tradition.

Islamic scholarship and institutions also shaped colonial politics. Sufi networks, reform movements, Muslim jurists and local educational centers had long histories across North, West and East Africa. Some Muslim leaders fought conquest; others bargained with imperial administrators, and still others maintained community autonomy through religious courts or education. Colonial governments could attempt to supervise Muslim elites because they viewed them as politically influential, sometimes strengthening particular authorities while weakening others.

Education created new possibilities and inequalities. Literacy in European languages could give access to clerical jobs, newspapers and later nationalist politics, yet opportunities were limited, uneven and gendered. Many colonial systems invested far less in mass education than in the administrative personnel needed to sustain rule. It is important to judge schooling through its actual reach and curriculum rather than assuming that the existence of a school proves a generous public policy.
