The powers attempted negotiations in the winter of 1876–1877, including the Constantinople Conference, to address the crisis and propose reforms. Proposed territorial and administrative arrangements reflected the interests of the negotiators and competing interpretations of local populations. Ottoman constitutional developments complicated diplomacy without resolving the demands. After negotiations failed and tensions remained high, Russia declared war on the Ottoman Empire in April 1877.

Russia's decisions reflected both humanitarian rhetoric and imperial calculation. Bulgaria was not simply conquered by an isolated foreign army acting without local participants. Bulgarian volunteers, local networks and other Balkan forces played roles; Romanian participation proved militarily significant, especially around Plevna. Ottoman officers and soldiers offered determined resistance, and the war took place across difficult terrain under winter conditions.

The period illustrates how diplomacy and war are connected but distinct instruments. When proposed reforms cannot be agreed or credibly implemented, governments may escalate to force.
