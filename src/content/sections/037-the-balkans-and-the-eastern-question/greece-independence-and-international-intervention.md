The Greek War of Independence began in 1821 against Ottoman authority, drawing on networks of merchants, intellectuals, clergy and secret societies such as the Filiki Eteria. The conflict was prolonged and brutal; insurgent and Ottoman forces committed violence against civilians, while local factions argued over control of the revolutionary movement. The struggle attracted sympathy in parts of Europe, including a wave of philhellenism that romanticized ancient Greece and contributed to fundraising and volunteers.

Britain, France and Russia intervened at critical moments. Their naval forces defeated an Ottoman-Egyptian fleet at Navarino in 1827; subsequent war and diplomacy led toward recognition of an independent Greek state in 1830. But the new kingdom did not include every Greek-speaking or Orthodox community. The gap between an existing state and imagined national territory remained part of the 'Greek question,' influencing later ambitions toward the Aegean, Epirus, Macedonia and Crete.

This is not merely a story of foreign rescuers. Greek fighters, sailors, financiers and local communities created and sustained the movement. Yet foreign powers influenced boundaries and government arrangements.
