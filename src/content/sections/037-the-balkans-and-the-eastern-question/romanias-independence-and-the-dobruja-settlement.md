Romania's internationally recognized independence in 1878 came with a territorial bargain it had not sought on those exact terms. Russia recovered southern Bessarabia, while Romania received northern Dobruja from the Ottoman Empire. The region contained Romanian, Bulgarian, Turkish, Tatar and other communities; its people did not form a single uniform bloc. New state institutions therefore faced questions of citizenship, landholding, language and administration.

Romanian political leaders often viewed the loss of southern Bessarabia as a disappointment despite battlefield contributions against the Ottomans. From the perspective of foreign diplomacy, the exchange was part of a greater package. These perspectives were not identical. Romania's evolving relations with Russia and Austria-Hungary reflected the need to balance strategic security against memories of the settlement.

Dobruja also shows why border changes must be followed by social history. Which officials registered land? Which schools received support? How did communities maintain religious institutions? Did newcomers obtain rights comparable to long-term residents?
