Relations among the former allies collapsed over disputed territory, especially Macedonia. In June 1913 Bulgarian forces attacked Serbian and Greek positions, setting off the Second Balkan War. Romania and the Ottoman Empire also entered the conflict against Bulgaria. The resulting settlement was unfavorable to Bulgaria: the Treaty of Bucharest in August 1913 transferred southern Dobruja to Romania and apportioned most of Macedonia among Serbia and Greece; separate arrangements with the Ottoman Empire established their frontier in Thrace.

No single treaty suddenly resolved every grievance. Serbia expanded significantly; Greece acquired Thessaloniki and other territories; Albania's frontiers remained internationally contested; Bulgaria's disappointment became a major influence on its later diplomatic choices. The Ottoman Empire retained or recovered a small but strategically significant European area around Edirne. Population movements, reprisals and disputes over schools, language and church property continued across the region.

The immediate political consequence was the reshaping of military and diplomatic expectations. Governments remembered who had supported or opposed their claims. Yet it would be a mistake to say that a world war was therefore unavoidable.
