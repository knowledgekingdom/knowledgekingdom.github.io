The changing Balkan political map was produced by interacting forces: Ottoman reform and military setbacks, the growth of national movements, regional economic change, international finance, European diplomacy, warfare and mass displacement. Different actors sought different things. A Bulgarian schoolteacher, a Muslim farmer in Thrace, an Ottoman constitutionalist, a Serbian minister, an Albanian organizer and an Austrian general did not confront the same 'question,' even when they occupied the same geopolitical theater.

Three conclusions deserve retention. First, a treaty settles legal claims among its signatories but cannot automatically settle identities or grievances among populations. Second, a national movement may pursue self-determination while also making claims that another community regards as domination. Third, great-power support is a resource that can become a constraint: patrons help create new states but also seek influence over their choices. The most useful history explains these tensions without treating any one national map as the natural ending to centuries of regional life.

In Chapter 38 we will examine the outbreak of the First World War. Sarajevo, the alliance systems, imperial competition and military planning will matter, but none can be understood well without the relationships and grievances developed across the preceding century. The Eastern Question was not a puzzle finally 'solved' in 1878 or 1913.
