There was no single document called the Eastern Question, and no fixed list of problems. The phrase changed with circumstances. In the eighteenth century it could describe conflicts between the Ottoman and Russian empires; in the nineteenth it came to include Greek independence, the future of Ottoman provinces, access to the Straits between the Black and Mediterranean seas, the security of the Danube, and the balance among European great powers. The phrase itself reveals a perspective: it treats an inhabited region as a strategic question to be answered by cabinets elsewhere.

Three layers must be kept distinct. First was the Ottoman state's capacity to reform and defend itself. Second were the aspirations of populations and political movements within or adjacent to Ottoman territories. Third was the competition among states—notably Russia, Britain, Austria (later Austria-Hungary), France and eventually Germany—over what would happen if Ottoman rule weakened. A diplomatic agreement might quiet the third layer without meeting demands in the second. A successful provincial reform might strengthen the first but disappoint nationalist activists demanding independence.

Calling the Ottoman Empire simply a 'sick man' is an inadequate explanation. The expression captures a nineteenth-century stereotype and some real fiscal and military vulnerabilities, but the empire could still mobilize armies, negotiate alliances, carry out administrative reforms and develop railways. Its European provinces did not decline in one straight line.
