The Ottoman Empire's territorial difficulties did not begin in 1876. Conflicts with Habsburg and Russian forces in the eighteenth century, the challenges of provincial autonomy, and the changing requirements of warfare all pushed Ottoman rulers toward military and administrative reform. Reform could be threatening to established elites, provincial power brokers and groups whose privileges depended on old arrangements. Stronger central control could also be experienced locally as more intrusive taxation and conscription.

The early nineteenth century brought struggles over military organization and the Janissary corps; Sultan Mahmud II destroyed the corps in 1826 and sought a new army. These transformations did not guarantee success. Central officials still faced enormous distances, uneven information and fiscal constraints. Regional leaders sometimes exercised substantial power and negotiated their place within imperial structures. European loans and commercial ties later increased external influence over Ottoman finances.

Rather than treating each defeat as inevitable evidence that an empire was dying, consider the problem of reform under pressure. A government attempting to improve taxation may confront resistance before new revenue arrives. It needs a modern army but may not yet possess the factories or finance to sustain one. A revolution in a province can invite foreign intervention, which then reduces the empire's control over its own program.
