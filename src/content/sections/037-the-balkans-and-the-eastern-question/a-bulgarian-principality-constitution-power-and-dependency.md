After 1878 the Bulgarian principality developed institutions in a setting of strong Russian influence and formal Ottoman suzerainty. The Tarnovo Constitution, adopted in 1879, established a constitutional framework with representative institutions and protections that were significant for a new state. Prince Alexander of Battenberg became its first ruler. Political life nevertheless contained disputes between liberals, conservatives, the prince, Russian representatives and local elites over the distribution of power.

The new state's challenges were practical. It needed a functioning administration, courts, schools, roads, taxes, armed forces and a civil service. Officials faced questions of property redistribution and the consequences of wartime population displacement. State building also involved choices about whose language and historical narrative would become official. A constitutional text could promise rights without guaranteeing that every resident—particularly members of religious or linguistic minorities—experienced equal security.

Russia had helped secure Bulgarian autonomy but expected influence over the principality's institutions and diplomacy. Bulgarian leaders were grateful to Russian military sacrifice yet increasingly contested the scope of Russian interference. This distinction helps explain why liberation did not generate permanent political alignment.
