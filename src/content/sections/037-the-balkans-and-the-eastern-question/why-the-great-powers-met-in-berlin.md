The Congress of Berlin met in June and July 1878 under Bismarck's chairmanship. Its purpose was to revise the San Stefano arrangements in a form other great powers could tolerate. Bismarck presented himself as a mediator, but the participants negotiated within the realities of military power and rival alliances. The Ottoman Empire was present in formal diplomacy, yet the victors and stronger European states exercised disproportionate influence over the settlement.

The Congress's priorities can be expressed as a dilemma. If Russia's war gains were accepted almost without revision, Britain and Austria-Hungary feared a radical shift in influence. If all gains were reversed, Russia and its allies might reject the outcome. If territory were distributed purely according to one group's nationalist claims, other populations and states would dispute the definition of that group and the legitimacy of the chosen boundary. The resulting treaty compromised among the powers rather than solving every claim by an agreed standard.

One must not say that 'the European powers created every Balkan state at Berlin.' Serbian, Romanian, Greek, Bulgarian and Albanian movements had their own histories and internal social forces. Yet outside recognition, armed intervention and treaty boundaries mattered enormously.
