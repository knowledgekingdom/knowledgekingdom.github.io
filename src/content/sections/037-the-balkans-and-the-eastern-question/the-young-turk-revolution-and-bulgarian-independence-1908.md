The Young Turk Revolution of 1908 restored the Ottoman constitution and parliament. Its supporters hoped that constitutional government might renew the state and reconcile its diverse population under an Ottoman political identity. Initially many groups greeted the revolution with optimism; disagreements over centralization, language, representation and autonomy soon exposed conflicting priorities. The Committee of Union and Progress became increasingly influential, but the movement was not ideologically uniform.

In the same year, Bulgaria declared full independence from Ottoman suzerainty, and Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia and Herzegovina. The timing was not coincidental: constitutional upheaval in the Ottoman Empire and competing diplomatic opportunities encouraged states to revise arrangements left by Berlin. Bulgaria's ruler Ferdinand assumed the title tsar, and the principality became a kingdom in international terms. Financial and diplomatic negotiations followed to regularize the change.

A lesson here is that political reform can be interpreted differently across an empire. Some subjects may view a restored constitution as a path to equality; others may see centralization and conclude that national independence is safer. The events of 1908 did not demonstrate that constitutionalism was impossible.
