International recognition in 1878 did not settle every Serbian and Montenegrin aspiration. Both states expanded or adjusted their frontiers, built central institutions and pursued ambitions shaped by neighboring populations and great-power diplomacy. Serbia's rulers faced internal constitutional and dynastic struggles, as well as commercial and diplomatic dependence on Austria-Hungary. Montenegro, whose military and political history differed from Serbia's, had to navigate its terrain, small resources, coastal ambitions and relationships with larger patrons.

A foreign-policy choice could conflict with national rhetoric. A Serbian government might rely commercially on Vienna even while Serbian nationalists looked toward Slavic-speaking communities under Habsburg administration. Montenegro could seek expansion without always possessing resources to integrate new territory quickly. Political independence did not guarantee independence from markets, debt or diplomatic leverage.

After the change of dynasty in Serbia in 1903, its foreign policy shifted, raising tensions with Austria-Hungary. Later crises—especially the customs dispute commonly known as the Pig War and the Bosnian annexation crisis—illustrated the connection between economics, sovereignty and nationalist ambition.
