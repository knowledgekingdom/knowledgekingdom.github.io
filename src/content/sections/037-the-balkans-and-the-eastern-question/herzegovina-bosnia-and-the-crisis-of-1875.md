An uprising that began in Herzegovina in 1875 spread into a wider crisis. Heavy fiscal burdens, local grievances, agrarian conflict and political organization interacted; simplistic explanations in which religion alone caused rebellion miss the social setting. Neighboring Serbia and Montenegro became entangled in the conflict, and refugees, volunteers and foreign sympathizers crossed borders. As violence spread, Ottoman forces faced the challenge of reasserting order amid growing external scrutiny.

Bosnia and Herzegovina was home to Muslim, Orthodox and Catholic communities whose identities were not interchangeable with fixed modern national labels. Local social relations involved landowners, peasants, towns, religious establishments and imperial officials. People could disagree about Ottoman reform, autonomy, union with a neighboring principality, or the desirability of any outside administration.

The uprising helped trigger a chain of international interventions and wars. It is important not to read this chain backward, as if participants in 1875 knew the Treaty of Berlin was inevitable.
