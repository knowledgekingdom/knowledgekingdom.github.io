The Crimean War of 1853–1856 arose from disputes involving the Ottoman Empire, Russia and the status of Christian holy places, within a much broader struggle over influence and security. Britain and France joined the Ottoman side; fighting and suffering extended beyond the Crimean Peninsula. The siege of Sevastopol became a symbol of industrial-age warfare and medical inadequacies. The conflict also revealed how logistics, disease, transport and public opinion could shape modern armies.

The Treaty of Paris in 1856 constrained Russia's military position in the Black Sea and reaffirmed a form of European commitment to Ottoman territorial integrity. It also influenced the Danubian principalities and arrangements for navigation. But constraints were not permanent. Russia later repudiated key Black Sea provisions, and the 1870–1871 diplomatic changes showed how a system of treaties depended on the broader balance of power.

For Balkan national movements, the lesson was ambiguous. Great-power rivalry could weaken one patron in the short term while creating new diplomatic openings in the long term.
