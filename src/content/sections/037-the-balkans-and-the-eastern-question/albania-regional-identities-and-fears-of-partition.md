Albanian-speaking populations lived across several Ottoman districts, encompassing Muslims of different traditions as well as Catholics and Orthodox Christians. Their social institutions and political loyalties were varied. Some Albanian elites served in the Ottoman administration; others resisted local reforms or sought greater autonomy. A simple opposition of 'Albanians versus the Ottoman Empire' misses these shifting relationships.

As neighboring national movements expanded territorial claims, Albanian activists increasingly feared that lands they regarded as Albanian would be divided among Serbia, Montenegro, Greece or other states. The League of Prizren, formed in 1878, brought together regional interests and developed demands shaped by the outcomes of the Russo-Turkish War and Berlin diplomacy. Its participants did not all initially seek the same constitutional destination; positions ranged from defense of territory within the empire toward more autonomous projects.

The Albanian example complicates religiously defined maps: national movements might unite people across religious boundaries, while rulers and diplomats sometimes assumed such divisions made a coherent nation impossible. Questions of Albanian autonomy and statehood grew especially urgent in the early twentieth century, culminating in the declaration of independence in 1912 amid the Balkan Wars. \[<a href="/037-the-balkans-and-the-eastern-question/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]\[<a href="/037-the-balkans-and-the-eastern-question/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]
