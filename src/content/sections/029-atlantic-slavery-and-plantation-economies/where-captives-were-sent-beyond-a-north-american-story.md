Popular presentations sometimes imply that most captives were taken directly to what became the United States. In fact, Brazil and the Caribbean received far larger shares of transatlantic arrivals. Portuguese and Brazilian operators, British vessels, French traders, Dutch merchants, Spanish American networks and others played different roles across time. Many voyages were organized from American or European ports; the flag, investor, ship owner and destination could represent different political affiliations.

Brazilian plantations and mines demanded labor over centuries. Caribbean colonies built dense sugar-producing societies in which enslaved people often made up large majorities of the population. Continental North America had a smaller share of direct Atlantic disembarkations, even as slavery expanded dramatically through births, inland transfers and domestic trade. By the nineteenth century, slaveholding economies in the United States were tied to cotton production for domestic and overseas textile industries.

A map that places one pin at every landing port would illuminate a strikingly unequal geography. But maps can also mislead: arrivals tell us where captives entered documented systems, not every place they lived or the entire scale of internal displacement. Distinguishing first landings, later forced movements and the growth of local enslaved populations is essential to understanding the Americas.
