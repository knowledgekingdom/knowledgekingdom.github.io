Imagine an estate producing a commodity worth 100 units in annual sales. Its records list 30 units of equipment and transport costs, 20 units of provisions and administration, and 15 units of interest and other expenses. The owner reports a surplus of 35 units. This is a hypothetical exercise. It does not imply that any such estate truly produced those amounts or that human beings can be priced as ordinary inputs.

If the accounting system treats forced labor as a property holding and excludes its human costs, the apparent surplus tells us little about the enterprise's social value. A welfare analysis must consider the suffering and lost autonomy of enslaved workers, the removal of communities, political violence and alternative uses of resources. Some costs defy credible monetary valuation; their exclusion is itself a limitation.

The distinction between private returns and social cost is applicable far beyond slavery. An institution can enrich owners by shifting harm onto others. That observation does not erase the distinct extremity of slaveholding, but it provides a useful conceptual bridge to later economics chapters on externalities, institutions and inequality.
