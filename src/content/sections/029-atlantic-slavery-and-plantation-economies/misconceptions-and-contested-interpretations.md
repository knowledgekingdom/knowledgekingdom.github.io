Misconception one: 'Slavery existed everywhere, so Atlantic slavery was ordinary.' Earlier bondage systems existed, but Atlantic racial chattel slavery had historically specific laws, markets and consequences. Misconception two: 'All African societies cooperated equally.' Their roles differed by region and century, and resistance was widespread. Misconception three: 'The triangle explains all routes.' It omits direct and intra-American traffic. Misconception four: 'Most African captives were sent to the United States.' Brazil and the Caribbean received far larger shares of direct Atlantic arrivals.

Misconception five: 'Abolishing the trade abolished slavery.' Trade restrictions and emancipation laws frequently occurred decades apart. Misconception six: 'Enslaved people contributed only labor.' They brought skills, knowledge, cultures, institutions and resistance into the making of Atlantic societies. Misconception seven: 'One economic theory explains the entire legacy.' Quantification and causation remain contested, especially when comparing centuries of unequal development.

A central discipline is to distinguish a historical fact from an interpretation. The existence of a law is usually established. How effectively it worked can be debated with evidence. The profitability of a particular plantation can sometimes be measured. The total contribution of slavery to national industrialization requires broader causal analysis. Neither certainty nor uncertainty should be exaggerated.
