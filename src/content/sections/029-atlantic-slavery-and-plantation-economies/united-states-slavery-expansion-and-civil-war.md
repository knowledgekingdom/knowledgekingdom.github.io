In the United States, slavery became politically central as cotton production expanded westward and new territories were organized through conquest and Indigenous dispossession. Enslaved people were sold internally, sometimes over great distances, breaking families and creating economic ties between older slaveholding districts and newer cotton regions. The abolition of overseas importation in 1808 did not end domestic enslavement or prevent slavery from becoming more deeply embedded in the national economy.

Antislavery organizing, enslaved people's resistance, legal disputes, sectional political conflict and the movement to restrict slavery's territorial expansion contributed to the crisis that led to civil war in 1861. The causes of the war included constitutional debates and power struggles, but the protection and expansion of slavery were central to the seceding states' political projects. Enslaved people accelerated emancipation through escape, intelligence-gathering, military service and pressure on wartime policy.

The Emancipation Proclamation in 1863 was a wartime measure applying to areas in rebellion, not a single law ending slavery everywhere immediately. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in December 1865, abolished slavery in the United States subject to its punishment-for-crime exception. Freedpeople then faced the enormous struggle to secure land, education, family unity, citizenship and political rights in the face of organized resistance.
