Imagine three statements: 'Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807,' 'Britain abolished slavery in 1833,' and 'everyone in the British Caribbean became fully free in 1833.' The first is broadly correct regarding Britain's trade. The second names legislation enacted in 1833, largely implemented in 1834. The third is misleading: apprenticeship delayed full freedom in much of the British Caribbean until 1838. The statements differ because the object and implementation of each law are different.

To analyze any abolition milestone, ask four questions. What activity was prohibited—importation, sale, ownership, compulsory labor? To whom and where did the law apply? How was it enforced? What happened to people previously held in bondage? An answer that specifies these dimensions is more useful than a date memorized without context.

The same method applies to Brazil, Cuba, the United States and regions beyond the Atlantic world. Law can be a decisive instrument of change, but legal status, labor markets, political power and personal security often move at different speeds.
