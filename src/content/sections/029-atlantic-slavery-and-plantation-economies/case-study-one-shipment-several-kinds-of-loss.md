Suppose a hypothetical ship carries 300 captives away from an African port and 258 survive to disembark. The loss during the measured crossing is 42 people, or 14 percent of those embarked. This arithmetic is straightforward and the scenario is illustrative, not a claim about an identified voyage. It says nothing precise about how many people died before embarkation or after landing. It also cannot describe the loss of homes, languages, relatives or freedom endured by survivors.

Now suppose a document reports only 258 arrivals and not the 300 departures. Unless the embarkation count is found elsewhere, dividing missing lives by arrivals would be impossible. It would be methodologically wrong to assume every voyage shared the same mortality rate. Historical estimates model missing information using observed records, patterns and assumptions, and they should disclose uncertainty.

The case exposes the dual character of numbers: indispensable for measuring scale, incapable of capturing the full moral and social reality. Quantitative reasoning must be paired with evidence about institutions and lived experience rather than treated as a substitute for it.
