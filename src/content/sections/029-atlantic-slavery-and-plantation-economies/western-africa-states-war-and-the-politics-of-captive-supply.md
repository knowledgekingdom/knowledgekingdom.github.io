The Gold Coast, the Bight of Benin and neighboring regions were influenced by changing African states and trading centers. Asante power grew in the eighteenth century in a political environment shaped by warfare, gold production and commerce. Dahomey, centered in the Bight of Benin, at times participated actively in the export of captives, but its relations with neighbors and foreign merchants evolved over time. The Oyo Empire and many smaller powers also shaped regional routes and conflicts.

Captives could be acquired through warfare, raids, kidnapping, judicial proceedings or debt-related mechanisms; the proportion of each varied by region and period. Commercial incentives could transform practices that had previously operated under narrower circumstances. A war might have causes independent of Atlantic trade, but the possibility of selling prisoners could increase its material rewards. Conversely, some communities changed settlement patterns, formed defensive alliances or migrated to avoid raiding.

A responsible account therefore asks specific questions: Which ruler or community? At what date? What goods and political pressures? Who records the event? A European fort's documents offer evidence about coastal transactions but rarely reveal the full conditions in which captives were taken inland. The task is to connect African political history to Atlantic demand without treating either as a complete explanation on its own.
