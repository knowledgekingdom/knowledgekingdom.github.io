Retain several connected ideas. Atlantic slavery was an institution linking state power, racial law, coercive labor, maritime trade and plantation production. Its enormous scale can be studied with quantitative evidence, but records often obscure individuals' experiences. African societies responded in diverse ways; colonial consumers and financiers were linked to the system; and enslaved people continually resisted, survived and created new political and cultural worlds. Emancipation was an essential legal rupture, not an instant cure for unequal land ownership, economic vulnerability or racism.

The most important historical question is not merely how many ships crossed the ocean. It is how so many legal, political and economic decisions normalized human domination—and how people contested that normalization. Chapter 27 established the routes of maritime expansion. Chapter 28 explained biological and ecological transformations. This chapter adds the labor systems and resistance that made Atlantic empires both lucrative and violently unequal.

Chapter 30 will turn toward the Qing, Mughal and Ottoman worlds. That shift matters because the Atlantic was not the whole early modern world: enormous Asian and Eurasian states maintained their own political orders, commercial systems and cultural institutions. A globally balanced history must follow both histories without imagining that European expansion instantly became the only force shaping the world.
