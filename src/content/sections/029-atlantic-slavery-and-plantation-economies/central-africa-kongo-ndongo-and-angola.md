West Central Africa became a crucial source of enslaved captives, often through ports associated with Luanda and Benguela. Portuguese contact with the Kingdom of Kongo began in the late fifteenth century. Kongo rulers and elites made their own diplomatic and religious choices, including engagement with Christianity, but the growing trade in captives generated severe disputes. Kings and advisers were not simply powerless subjects of a European state, yet the possibilities for regulating trafficking narrowed as outside demand and internal political divisions intensified.

In the region associated with Ndongo and Matamba, Queen Njinga (often called Nzinga) navigated warfare, diplomacy and shifting alliances during the seventeenth century. Her career cannot be compressed into either a pure antislavery legend or a story of collaboration. She pursued sovereignty and political survival in a landscape shaped by Portuguese expansion, regional conflict, captive taking and changing economic interests. Remembering her complexity is more respectful to African history than assigning her a modern label without qualification.

This regional case also shows why a ship's embarkation point is not necessarily a person's birthplace. A captive landed at Luanda might have been seized or sold far inland, transported through several markets and recorded under a coastal identity. Archives preserve the terminal stage of a much longer rupture.
