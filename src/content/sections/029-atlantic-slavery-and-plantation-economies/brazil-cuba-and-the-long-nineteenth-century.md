The nineteenth century was not simply an epilogue to Atlantic slave trading. Large volumes of coerced migration continued after British abolition in 1807, and plantation exports expanded in some regions. Brazil received a major share of transatlantic captives over the history of the trade; coffee and sugar production remained connected to enslavement. Cuba's sugar economy also flourished in an era when antislavery laws and their enforcement varied across empires.

Brazil's 1850 Eusébio de Queirós Law helped suppress the transatlantic import trade more decisively than earlier restrictions, while slavery itself endured until the Lei Áurea (Golden Law) in 1888. In Cuba, the process of abolition unfolded through stages, with slavery formally ending in 1886. Such dates matter, but they do not mean every locality experienced change at the same moment or that former owners ceased to command land and capital.

Abolition timelines should never be presented as a race in which one nation wins moral credit. They require attention to coercion that persisted after legislation, the agency of enslaved and freed people, political struggles within states, and the international commercial context sustaining labor demand.
