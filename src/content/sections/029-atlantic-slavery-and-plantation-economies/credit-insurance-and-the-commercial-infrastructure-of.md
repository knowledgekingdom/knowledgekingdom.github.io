An oceanic business required lenders, merchants, shipbuilders, insurers, warehouses, brokers and courts. Voyages were expensive, risky and long. Investors might spread their risk among multiple ships and destinations; insurance and maritime law could transform events involving people into claims about insured cargo. Plantation credit might be secured against land, future crops or enslaved people, embedding claims to human property inside financial arrangements.

Documents created by merchants reveal how an atrocity could be normalized through routine procedures. A contract might specify prices, delivery dates and risk-sharing while never questioning the original seizure of freedom. Accounting records therefore need ethical and contextual interpretation: accurate bookkeeping does not certify legitimate exchange.

One especially disturbing case was the 1781 voyage of the Zong, during which the crew threw captives overboard and litigation followed over insurance. Historians debate aspects of the surviving evidence, and the case should not be treated as representative of every voyage. It vividly illustrates a system in which legal and commercial categories could turn murderous decisions into disputes over financial losses. An archive can reveal both economic infrastructure and moral blindness.
