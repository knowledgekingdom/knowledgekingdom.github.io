A textbook map may show a clean route from Liverpool to the West African coast, then to Jamaica, then home. It is an intelligible introduction to certain British circuits. But suppose records reveal a vessel financed in Bahia, sailing to Luanda and returning directly to Brazil, or a trader moving captives between Caribbean islands after their Atlantic landing. Neither fits the iconic diagram, yet both belong within the economic history of Atlantic slavery.

To test a network description, compare the diagram with shipping records, port charges, financing arrangements and commodity flows. Ask whether the model describes one typical journey, the main flows of a period, or an entire economic system. A schematic can be useful even when incomplete, provided the omission is acknowledged.

The analogy carries into modern economics. A global supply-chain map may highlight factories, shipping lanes and retail markets while obscuring subcontractors, workers' conditions or environmental costs. Recognizing the hidden links is a general skill, not merely a historical detail.
