After Britain ended its own transatlantic slave trade, naval patrols and international treaties sought to suppress shipping by other nations. Some intercepted vessels and liberated captives, but enforcement was uneven, expensive and complicated by diplomatic interests. The campaign also gave European states opportunities to increase their maritime and political influence along African coasts. An antislavery claim could coexist with imperial ambition; historians must assess particular interventions rather than assuming a single motive.

Restrictions did not immediately end trafficking. Illegal voyages, false paperwork, changes in flags and new routes continued, especially while slave labor remained profitable in Brazil, Cuba and elsewhere. The period of abolition therefore produced new forms of surveillance and smuggling as well as genuinely important changes in international law and public opinion.

Nor did opposition to the ocean trade always translate into opposition to every form of unfree labor. Colonial governments sometimes supported indentured labor systems after emancipation, recruiting workers from India and elsewhere under contracts whose freedom and enforceability varied greatly. Indenture was not identical to chattel slavery, but it could involve harsh restrictions and coercion. The historical lesson is to examine actual labor conditions rather than assume a humane transformation merely because legal vocabulary changed.
