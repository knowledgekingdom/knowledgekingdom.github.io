Sugar dominated some Atlantic colonies, but enslaved labor supported multiple commodities. Tobacco production in the Chesapeake depended on plantation labor and land expansion. Caribbean and Brazilian coffee estates expanded in different periods, transforming highland and tropical landscapes. Rice cultivation in parts of coastal North America and the Caribbean drew on specialized knowledge, including agricultural expertise possessed by enslaved Africans from rice-growing regions. Indigo dye production tied agricultural labor to manufacturing and consumer fashion.

Each crop created different production challenges. Rice required understanding of water and drainage; tobacco needed careful planting, curing and inspection; coffee involved harvesting and processing; indigo required controlled fermentation or extraction. Enslaved workers contributed significant technical skill. Describing them as unskilled laborers erases knowledge that plantation operators relied upon.

Commodity choice shaped landscape, settlement and political conflict. Some systems encouraged frontier expansion; others concentrated capital in processing installations. The broader theme is not that every product required the same plantation model, but that Atlantic slavery became compatible with multiple kinds of profitable enterprise.
