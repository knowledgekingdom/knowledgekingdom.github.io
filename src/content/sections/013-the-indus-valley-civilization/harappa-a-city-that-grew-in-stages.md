Harappa, in Punjab in present-day Pakistan, gave its name to the civilization because its excavated material helped researchers identify the widespread shared cultural tradition. The site was occupied over a long span and did not maintain one unchanging plan. Different mounds and sectors reveal changing walls, housing, activity areas, craft production and movement through the site. During the mature urban phase, it became one of the major regional centers in a network of towns, villages and other large cities. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]

A dense settlement needs continuous coordination, even if we cannot identify every office in its government. Streets require space, walls require maintenance, grain must move through storage and distribution systems, and some crafts need fuel, tools and access to raw materials. At Harappa, archaeologists have documented specialized production involving beads, shells, stones, metals and other materials. Shared standards suggest broad networks of interaction, although the existence of similar objects does not by itself prove a single empire directing every workshop. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]

Imagine a merchant receiving an unfamiliar shipment. The goods might be weighed, the package marked, and an identifying impression made in clay. We know that the Harappan world used seals and standardized weights; the exact detailed transaction just described is an illustrative possibility. It is reasonable to infer systems of verification and recognition, but not reasonable to translate a seal into an invented bill of sale. That distinction between reconstructed function and imagined content should guide every scene in this chapter.

Harappa also warns against treating urban archaeology as a photograph of one moment. A wall in a later occupation can cut through an earlier layout. An abandoned drain may later be cleaned and reused. The shape of the site that an excavator exposes today is a time-layered palimpsest: a landscape rewritten repeatedly by its inhabitants.
