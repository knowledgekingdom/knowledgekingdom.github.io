Dholavira, on Khadir Island in the Rann of Kutch region of present-day Gujarat, India, shows why the phrase 'the Indus city' can be misleading. Here water management had to confront a semi-arid environment with seasonal availability and strong variation across years. UNESCO describes planned urban spaces, fortification features, water harvesting and reservoirs, and sophisticated use of construction materials. Dholavira's organization is therefore not simply a replica of Mohenjo-daro's streets and wells. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]

The site's elaborate storage and management of water show that urban planning was partly an answer to specific environmental constraints. A ruler, council or other authority might have organized the work; the reservoirs themselves do not identify which arrangement existed. Similarly, walls might help with defense, demarcation, controlled movement, flood management or symbolic authority, depending on context. A historian should resist forcing a single explanation from one architectural feature.

Dholavira is also associated with a remarkable sign-bearing display often discussed as a large inscription. Its existence is intriguing, but, like the small seals, it has not yielded a universally accepted reading. We can say the signs were sufficiently important to display prominently; we cannot responsibly claim to know the message. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-12" class="cite">12</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

The comparison of Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and Dholavira exposes an important historical pattern: integration did not require architectural uniformity. Similar practices and cultural items could circulate over a huge area while settlements made locally adapted choices about water, materials, defenses and public buildings. A civilization is not one standardized town copied endlessly across a map.
