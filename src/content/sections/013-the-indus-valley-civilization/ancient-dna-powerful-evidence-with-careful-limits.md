Genetic research provides another line of evidence for investigating ancient populations, but it does not translate inscriptions or reveal a person's spoken language. A much-discussed study of an individual from Rakhigarhi recovered ancient genomic information relevant to population history in South Asia. Broader studies have modeled ancestry connections and changes across South and Central Asia. These findings are important because they can test some claims about population mixture, but they are not a complete census of the Indus civilization. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-22" class="cite">22</a>\]

One ancient skeleton represents one person, with a particular date, burial context and history of preservation. Even when researchers compare many samples, the map of excavated and successfully sequenced remains is uneven. Genetic ancestry does not determine an individual's religion, political identity, social position or home language. A person can inherit genetic ancestry from multiple populations while belonging to a community with an identity that cannot be read from DNA. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-21" class="cite">21</a>\]

This distinction matters for contemporary discussions. The desire to assign ancient civilizations to one exclusive modern nation, ethnicity or religion creates pressure to overinterpret fragmentary evidence. The archaeological landscape of the Harappan world straddles the borders of modern countries, while the human past extends across much longer and more fluid historical processes. An intellectually responsible account separates what genetics can test from questions requiring language, texts, archaeology and cultural evidence.

A sensible summary is modest but meaningful: ancient DNA has expanded what we can study about movements and mixture in South Asia; it has not supplied a translation of the Indus script, a list of political rulers or a simple map from prehistoric individuals to present-day identities.
