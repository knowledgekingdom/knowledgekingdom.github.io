Harappan cities display large construction projects and shared conventions, so it is reasonable to infer forms of organized authority. The harder question is what kind. Archaeologists have not identified a securely documented sequence of kings comparable to Egyptian king lists or Mesopotamian royal inscriptions. Monumental palaces and royal tombs of the familiar Near Eastern types are not clearly demonstrated across the Harappan sites. Yet the absence of an identifiable palace does not prove a society without inequality, competition or people exercising coercive power. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-10" class="cite">10</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]

Several explanatory models remain in discussion. One emphasizes influential urban elites and local political centers. Another suggests a more distributed or heterarchical arrangement: several institutions—merchant groups, neighborhoods, ritual communities or craft authorities—could coordinate without all decisions flowing from one pyramid of command. Still other interpretations emphasize distinct regional centers rather than a single territorial state. We can debate these models because archaeological evidence bears on aspects of them, but the debate is not settled by selecting whichever arrangement sounds most attractive today. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]

Compare two hypotheses. Under a strongly centralized empire, we might expect patterns of elite display, standardization, extraction, military power and authority extending over a broad territory. Under a network of allied cities or shared institutions, we might still expect standards, exchange and coordinated construction, but more diversity in local administrative centers and political symbols. Both models require careful evaluation of what has survived, and neither can be confirmed by a single seal or street. The challenge is to identify a combination of features that one model explains better than its rivals.

Political power may also have changed over time. Harappa in one century need not have been governed like Dholavira in another. Historians studying modern federations, empires and trade networks know that authority can be layered and negotiated. Ancient politics was not constrained to the modern choice between 'one king' and 'no government.' The Indus case teaches us to enlarge the possibilities rather than substitute an unsupported certainty.
