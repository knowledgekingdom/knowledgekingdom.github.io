Most people in a civilization do not spend their days posing for royal art. They cook, fetch water, repair things, care for children and older people, argue with neighbors, work, rest, and sometimes move. Domestic archaeology provides an imperfect way to recover those activities. At Harappan sites, houses, hearths, wells, bathing areas, pottery, animal bones, tools and waste deposits help archaeologists reconstruct routines. But an arrangement of rooms does not automatically tell us how a household defined kinship, marriage or gender roles. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-9" class="cite">9</a>\]

Differences among residences can suggest varied access to space, materials and resources. Yet archaeologists must distinguish building size from actual wealth and long-term occupation from one moment of use. Households could contain multiple generations, tenants, workers or changing configurations; a large home might also serve an institutional or commercial purpose. The expression 'ordinary people' hides many differences in age, position and work.

Diet can be investigated through plants, animal bones, residues and human remains where preservation and ethical research permit. A fragment of barley tells us a crop was present, not exactly how much every resident consumed. Dental disease or skeletal stress can reveal aspects of health and physical activity, but human remains are unevenly preserved and represent particular individuals in particular burial contexts. When researchers make population-level claims, sampling and uncertainties matter. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]

The presence of water infrastructure deserves emphasis, but it should not be romanticized into proof of universal public health. Even today, a city's health depends on waste disposal, diet, crowding, infection, infrastructure upkeep and social inequality. Archaeological drains demonstrate capabilities and choices, not a modern epidemiological survey. We can admire builders without claiming ancient urban residents lived in a disease-free society.
