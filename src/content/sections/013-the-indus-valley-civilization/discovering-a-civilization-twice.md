The Harappan world existed long before the modern countries and archaeological departments that now investigate it. Its ancient bricks were visible in mounds and reused in later construction. Nineteenth-century travelers and officials noticed ruins and collected objects, but systematic recognition that sites such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro belonged to a major Bronze Age urban tradition emerged through archaeological work in the early twentieth century. Researchers including Daya Ram Sahni and Rakhaldas Bandyopadhyay made important excavations, and John Marshall announced the significance of the discoveries in the 1920s. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-8" class="cite">8</a>\]

The discovery changed textbooks because South Asia's early history could no longer be described as though urban life had to be imported fully formed from a later outside civilization. Yet archaeology itself has a history. Early trenching practices, interests in dramatic finds and the movement of objects into museums influenced what could be studied and how interpretations formed. Later excavations used more precise stratigraphic recording, laboratory methods and regional surveys. For Mohenjo-daro especially, conservation problems created by water, salts, weather and past intervention complicate both tourism and research. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

Political borders also complicate modern research. Harappa and Mohenjo-daro lie in Pakistan; Dholavira and Rakhigarhi lie in India. Scholars working in different countries may use different institutional collections, excavation permits and research priorities. A careful history takes the evidence seriously on both sides of today's border without projecting twentieth-century nation-states into the third millennium BCE.

One of the most useful skills you can bring to an archaeological site is to ask what was recorded <em>in place</em> before an object was removed. A seal photographed in a storeroom is informative. The same seal documented in a precisely dated layer next to production waste, tablets or a doorway can support far richer questions about how it was used. Provenance is not a decorative detail: it is part of what makes an object historical evidence.
