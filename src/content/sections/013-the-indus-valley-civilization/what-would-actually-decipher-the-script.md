Suppose tomorrow an excavator discovered a group of long inscriptions clearly matched with a second, independently understood language. That could provide a transformative test of proposed sign values. Or imagine new archaeological finds revealing the same long sequence in multiple contexts, along with reliable evidence connecting signs to names or quantities. Researchers could compare predicted readings across the corpus. A decipherment is convincing when it makes reproducible, risky predictions that other specialists can test—not simply when its author finds a few symbols resembling modern letters. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-15" class="cite">15</a>\]

This thought experiment clarifies the role of missing evidence. Historians can be hopeful about what future excavations may reveal while remaining skeptical of premature claims. The inability to read the script is not proof that its makers had nothing important to say. It means the surviving marks have not yet yielded a securely supported interpretation. New evidence might transform our understanding of authority, language, trade, belief or social life, but we must not write that future book before the discoveries exist.

It also sharpens a practical rule for evaluating online 'breakthroughs.' Ask whether the proposal explains the full variety of signs and inscriptions rather than just a selected example; whether it works on unseen material; whether independent experts can reproduce it; and whether the claimed ancient language and archaeological context fit. Dramatic confidence and colorful diagrams are not substitutes for a testable result.
