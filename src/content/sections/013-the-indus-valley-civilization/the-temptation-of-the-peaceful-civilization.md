Compared with the lavish royal battle records of some neighboring societies, Indus finds do not yield an obvious archive of military conquest. This has encouraged a popular picture of a uniquely peaceful, egalitarian civilization. The picture is attractive, but it makes an unjustified inference: fewer surviving glorifications of war are not the same as proof that war or coercion did not exist. Some sites have walls, weapons or traces of interpersonal violence, although interpreting their functions and scale requires context. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-16" class="cite">16</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-17" class="cite">17</a>\]

A wall may defend a city, keep floods away, regulate access or mark an important precinct; it might do more than one of these. A skeletal injury may reflect an accident or conflict and cannot necessarily be assigned to a battle. An absence of monuments depicting a conquering king could reflect different forms of political display, lost materials, local artistic preferences, or a genuinely different distribution of authority. We must not choose among these possibilities solely because we prefer one political ideal.

The opposite exaggeration is also wrong. The Indus was not necessarily a militarized empire hiding under our feet. Historians should not invent armies, dynasties or mass conquests merely to make its history resemble Mesopotamia. The most defensible position is to recognize that the organization and intensity of warfare remain uncertain, as does the relationship between local power and broader cultural integration.

This uncertainty provides a conversation lesson. When someone claims 'the Indus had no rulers and no wars,' a good response is not simply 'That is false.' It is: 'What evidence would distinguish a peaceful network from a society whose political and military records are missing?' You can disagree firmly while still being precise about what the evidence demonstrates.
