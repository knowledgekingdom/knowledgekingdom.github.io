Imagine Rome in the tense days of 44 BCE. Julius Caesar has been killed in a meeting of the Senate. His assassins claim that they have saved liberty from a man who would rule like a king. They hope that the institutions of the Republic can recover if an unusually powerful individual is removed. Yet Rome is not a simple contest between a tyrant and an oppressed population. Caesar's veterans expect rewards; ordinary Romans fear renewed disorder; senators defend different interests; commanders control armies far from the capital. The killing of Caesar does not restore political agreement. It begins another struggle for power. This opening is a narrative reconstruction grounded in the broad sequence of events, not a record of particular individuals' thoughts.

The word <em>republic</em> comes from the Latin <em>res publica</em>: roughly, the public affair or commonwealth. Romans used it to describe their political community and its institutions, though its meaning changed across centuries. The Roman Republic had elections, magistrates, legal procedures and a Senate. It also had powerful aristocratic families, severe social exclusions, enslaved workers and citizens whose opportunities depended on status, wealth and networks of obligation. Rome's leaders could praise freedom while conquering neighboring peoples and treating captives as property.

The central paradox of this chapter is that Rome grew extraordinarily successful at projecting military and political power while its old institutions struggled to manage the consequences of that success. The emperors who followed did not abolish the language of the Republic. They appropriated it, combined it with concentrated authority, and reimagined the relationship between the ruler and the governed. And the date many students memorize as the 'fall of Rome,' 476 CE, marks a major change in the western imperial court, not the disappearance of Roman government from the earth. We will follow a much longer story: <strong>formation, expansion, crisis, adaptation and afterlives</strong>.
