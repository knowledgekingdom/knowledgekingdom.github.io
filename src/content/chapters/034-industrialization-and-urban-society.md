Imagine entering a cotton mill near Manchester in the early nineteenth century. The building is tall because rows of equipment must share power transmitted through shafts and belts. Machines turn in rhythms that cannot be slowed to accommodate each worker. Damp air may help fibers behave, but it makes an already uncomfortable room harder to endure. A child carries materials between frames, women monitor spinning or weaving, and a mechanic listens for the noise that signals a failing gear. Outside, carts bring raw cotton and coal. Along a canal, merchants dispatch cloth to consumers whom the workers will never meet. This is a composite teaching scene, not a description of one particular factory.

For much of human history, most households devoted their labor to agriculture and domestic production. Cities existed, long-distance commerce flourished, and sophisticated workshops manufactured goods in large quantities, but sustained increases in output per person were difficult to maintain across whole economies. Beginning in eighteenth-century Britain, an interconnected set of innovations began to change the relationship among human labor, machinery, energy and production. Over succeeding generations, factories, mines, railways, industrial towns and commercial institutions remade societies well beyond Britain.

The term <em>Industrial Revolution</em> can mislead if it suggests a single sudden event. Change unfolded unevenly, industry by industry and region by region. Many people continued to farm or work by hand long after machines became famous. Yet its consequences eventually altered the physical scale of production and the organization of ordinary life. The central question is therefore two-sided: <strong>How did societies become capable of producing so much more, and why did the new capacity initially coexist with dangerous work, crowded housing, pollution and severe inequality?</strong>
