Imagine standing in a square in a German-speaking city in the spring of 1848. A procession advances beneath black, red and gold banners. Speakers demand constitutions, representative government and some form of German unity. Yet the people below the flags do not agree about the country they want. Some imagine a constitutional federation; others want a centralized republic. Some would include Austrian territories; others fear that the Habsburg Empire's many non-German communities would make that impossible. A few worry less about national unity than about wages, bread and the next harvest. This is a composite scene grounded in the political circumstances of 1848, not a description of a particular demonstration.

Two decades later a German Empire would be declared, but not through the parliamentary route envisaged by many liberals. Prussian armies, monarchical bargaining, commercial integration and nationalist appeals would all play parts. The flag could remain a symbol of shared hopes while the constitution and power structure differed from what its first advocates intended. <strong>Nationalism does not prescribe a single kind of state.</strong> It is a claim about belonging and political legitimacy that can be attached to competing institutions and moral projects.

The central puzzle of this chapter is therefore not simply why Germany and Italy unified. It is why millions began to think that a political state ought to represent a particular people—and why the attempt to implement this idea unsettled empires, transformed citizenship, and made certain minorities less secure.
