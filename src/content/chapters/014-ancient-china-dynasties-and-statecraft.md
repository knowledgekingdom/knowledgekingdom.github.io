Imagine a late Shang diviner holding the shoulder blade of an ox. The scene is an evidence-based reconstruction, not a surviving eyewitness account. The bone has been prepared, and a question concerns the king: will a coming harvest be favorable? Will an expedition succeed? Should an ancestor receive an offering? The bone is heated until a crack forms. Someone interprets the crack, and a scribe may record a question, date, prognosis or outcome in characters cut into its surface. Thousands of years later, archaeologists find those inscriptions at the ancient capital near today's Anyang. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

It is an extraordinary meeting between two kinds of evidence. The cracks and inscriptions are physical objects. The questions give us access—imperfectly—to royal anxieties, procedures and beliefs. The Shang king appears as a ruler who fought, sacrificed, inspected harvests and treated the ancestral world as politically significant. The inscriptions make that world seem surprisingly close; their narrow viewpoint makes it distant again. They do not tell us what every farmer or craft worker believed. They are largely records of the people who could command scribes and diviners. \[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/014-ancient-china-dynasties-and-statecraft/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

We have already studied Mesopotamia, Egypt and the Indus world. Now ask a new comparative question: <strong>what did it take for a ruling house to claim authority over land, labor and distant communities—and what happened when another house declared that authority had been lost?</strong> This is the problem of early Chinese statecraft. We will move from diverse farming regions to dynastic rule, from archaeological mounds to inscribed bones, and from ancestor-centered kingship to the Zhou claim that a ruler's conduct affected his legitimacy. The chapter ends where the political world becomes more competitive, preparing us for later Chinese philosophy and imperial unification.
