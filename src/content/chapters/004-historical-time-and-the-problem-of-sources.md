Imagine entering a great library to see one of the most famous documents in English history. You expect <em>the</em> Magna Carta: a single heroic parchment on which a king surrendered his unlimited power and modern freedom was born. Instead, the curator explains that four original 1215 exemplifications survive. Two belong to the British Library, one is at Salisbury Cathedral, and one at Lincoln. They were produced by different scribes. Their physical differences matter to specialists, and there is no sound reason to picture one unique, authoritative sheet from which every other surviving version was copied. What you thought was one document turns out to be a family of documents and a long, complicated political story.

That is only the first surprise. Magna Carta was issued amid a crisis between King John and a group of barons in 1215. It was not a declaration of equal civil rights for everybody in the modern sense. Much of its language concerned specific grievances, feudal obligations, justice, church liberties, and limits on arbitrary royal conduct. The original settlement quickly failed as a peace agreement. Later reissues, interpretations, political disputes, and generations of lawyers transformed the charter's significance. Its eventual place in constitutional history is real; the simple origin myth is not.

The historian's task is to hold those two truths together. A document can matter enormously without meaning to its original authors what it means to us. It can survive in several versions, carry different audiences, and become powerful for reasons its creators could not foresee. If we want to understand it, we must ask what existed in 1215, how it was made, who could read it, why it was preserved, and how later generations used it. We are not allowed to replace those questions with a dramatic caption on a museum wall.

Now zoom out. The same difficulties appear everywhere. Ancient royal inscriptions tell us how rulers wished to be remembered. Archaeological ruins reveal communities that left few written accounts. A photograph of a twentieth-century protest tells us what a camera recorded but not what everyone there believed. A national holiday remembers something important while compressing years of diplomacy and conflict into one day. A history book tells an intelligible story, yet may hide the uncertain steps behind its narrative. How can we know what happened when we cannot visit the past and ask it to repeat itself?

This chapter is your toolbox for answering that question. We will learn to locate events in time; distinguish evidence from interpretation; read texts against their authors' interests; reconstruct lives from objects, buildings, and landscapes; understand dating methods; and investigate why historians disagree. We will then put the tools to work across Europe, Africa, and the Balkans. You do not need to memorize every date. You need to learn what a date or a document is actually doing in a historical argument.
