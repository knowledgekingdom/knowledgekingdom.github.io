Imagine arriving in Baghdad in the ninth century. Boats unload grain and textiles along the Tigris. Officials inspect accounts; a merchant bargains in several languages; students debate legal questions; a translator compares a Greek treatise with an Arabic version. A visitor may encounter Muslims, Christians, Jews, and members of other communities, each with different rights and opportunities under the ruling order. This is a <strong>composite scene</strong>, not a description of a particular recorded day. Its point is to show how far the world had changed in scarcely two and a half centuries.

Around 600 CE, there was no Muslim caliphate or Baghdad. The Arabian Peninsula contained trading settlements, pastoral communities, agricultural towns, pilgrimage sites and competing networks of allegiance. By 900, Arabic was a major language of scholarship and government across an immense region. Islam had become a world religion, while the rulers who claimed to lead Muslims faced growing political competition. Yet the transformation was not a simple march from one victorious battle to the next. It required preaching and persuasion, conflict and negotiation, inheritance from older empires, merchant journeys, local collaboration, and generations of religious and legal discussion.

This chapter asks five connected questions. What made the rise of Islam possible in its seventh-century setting? How did Muhammad's message become the basis of a new community and political order? Why did early Muslim armies conquer enormous territories so quickly? Why did disagreement about leadership become a lasting question in Islamic history? And how did an empire turn into a family of diverse Muslim societies whose religious connections outlived any one dynasty? These questions lead naturally back to Chapters 11, 12, 15 and 17: the new order arose among peoples shaped by older Mediterranean, Persian, African and Arabian histories.
