Imagine a small hospital near the border between two countries at war. The staff have spent days treating frightened families. One morning a wounded soldier from the invading army arrives, no longer able to fight. Soon afterwards a soldier from the defending army needs the same doctor. The hospital has one operating theatre and cannot treat both immediately. Which patient should receive care first? The answer under humanitarian law is not that the doctor must identify the more just political cause. Medical urgency, not nationality or allegiance, must guide the allocation of care. The scene is fictional, but the principle behind it has real legal force. \[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/046-international-law-and-armed-conflict/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

Outside the hospital, the families may be arguing about who began the war. One government says it is defending itself; the other invokes a threat to national security. These are serious questions, and international law has rules for answering them. But they are different from the question facing the doctor. If we allowed the protections of wounded people to depend on a verdict about the justice of the war, each belligerent would have an incentive to declare its opponents unworthy of protection. The victims would have to wait for a political dispute to be resolved before they could receive treatment.

Chapter 42 asked why wars begin. Chapter 44 examined the organization of military power, and Chapter 45 studied the diplomacy through which states cooperate and compete. We now examine something more difficult: how obligations survive disagreement, how law constrains conduct even when diplomacy fails, and why accountability requires evidence rather than merely outrage. The central puzzle is whether a world without a supreme government can nevertheless have meaningful legal rules.
