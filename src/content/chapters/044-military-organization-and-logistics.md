Imagine two countries separated by a mountain frontier. The larger one publishes a report boasting that its armed forces have twice as many personnel as its neighbour. Television presenters study photographs of impressive machinery and conclude that the outcome of any conflict is obvious. Yet another set of documents would tell a rather different story: maintenance records, recruitment figures, hospital staffing, the availability of spare parts, the condition of transport networks and the government's ability to make decisions in a crisis. These two countries are fictional. Their predicament is not.

A large military establishment is not necessarily a large usable military force. People may be registered as reservists but have spent years away from training. Machinery may belong to a military inventory while awaiting repairs. A government may possess abundant money and find that the specialised industrial goods it wishes to purchase cannot be delivered quickly. A minister can announce an ambitious commitment long before the institutions responsible for fulfilling it have assessed its implications.

The central puzzle of this chapter is how states convert their resources into organised capacity, and why that capacity does not automatically produce political success. Chapter 42 asked why wars begin. Chapter 43 examined how geography shapes power. We now examine the institutions that stand between a country's resources and the consequences of its decisions. Military history becomes more intelligible when an army is understood not as a collection of dramatic encounters, but as a complicated, expensive and very human social institution. \[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/044-military-organization-and-logistics/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]
