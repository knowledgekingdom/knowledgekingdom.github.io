Imagine a coastal settlement in the Caribbean in 1492. Families fish, cultivate crops, maintain relationships with neighboring communities, and understand currents, reefs and seasonal weather. Canoes travel between islands. News of unfamiliar ships arrives along networks of communication that existed long before a European navigator crossed the Atlantic. The residents do not think of themselves as living at the edge of history. They live within it. Across the ocean, a Castilian-backed expedition searches for an approach to Asia that its commander expects to be shorter than the eastward routes known to merchants and rulers. Two geographic understandings are about to collide.

This scene is an illustrative composite, not a reconstruction of one documented encounter. It reveals the central challenge of this chapter. Histories long described the period as an 'Age of Discovery.' For European mapmakers, Atlantic expeditions did disclose coastlines and routes previously absent from their charts. But societies encountered in the Americas, Africa and Asia were not undiscovered. They possessed names for places, political institutions, histories, languages and practical knowledge. Discovery must always be qualified: discovered by whom, for what purpose, and with what consequences? \[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]\[<a href="/027-european-maritime-expansion/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]

European maritime expansion developed through experiments that started well before Columbus and continued long after his death. Portugal tested Atlantic currents, settled or seized island territories, traded and fought along African coasts, then reached the Indian Ocean. Castile and subsequently the Spanish monarchy entered a westward Atlantic contest, conquering and colonizing parts of the Americas. Other European powers would follow. The same ships carried merchants, priests, soldiers, interpreters, technical specialists, goods and coercive ambitions. Their voyages brought new information and exchanges, but also conquest, captivity and unequal systems of extraction. Our question is not whether European sailors were courageous or violent in some abstract sense. It is how their particular capacities, circumstances, allies and opponents created consequences far beyond what their sponsors anticipated.
