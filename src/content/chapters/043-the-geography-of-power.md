Imagine that a newly elected prime minister receives three maps. The first is a physical map, showing mountains to the south, a river to the north and one broad plain extending toward the capital. The second shows roads, railways, ports, electric transmission lines and fibre-optic cables. The third is political: borders, disputed districts, military allies and trade agreements. Each map describes the same place. Yet each suggests a different answer to a question such as: Is this country secure?

A mountain range may slow an invading force, but it may also isolate villages from hospitals. A river may define a border, yet the bridges on it can unite two economies or become vulnerable points in a crisis. A coastal port may open distant markets, while a government that neglects maintenance or customs administration can squander the advantage. Even the political map changes how the other two are read: a border between friendly partners functions differently from a fortified border between rivals.

The central lesson of this chapter is that geography is neither decoration nor destiny. It supplies opportunities, obstacles and uneven costs. Political institutions, technology, markets and human choices determine what happens next. Our task is to learn how to read the interaction rather than repeat a slogan such as 'countries must always fight for warm-water ports.'
