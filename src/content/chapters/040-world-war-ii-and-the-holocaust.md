In the spring of 1945 a railway station somewhere in central Europe might hold people moving in every direction: liberated prisoners searching for relatives, soldiers sent toward a collapsing front, families fleeing advancing armies, and workers trying to find bread. A ruined platform could serve as a temporary hospital or shelter. The scene is an illustrative composite, not a description of one documented station. Its purpose is to restore human scale to a conflict often told entirely through maps, weapons and leaders. For many people the end of combat did not mean a return home; home might have been destroyed, occupied by someone else, or situated behind a new border.

World War II is conventionally dated from Germany's invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939 to Japan's formal surrender on 2 September 1945. Those boundaries are useful, but the Asian story began earlier: full-scale war between Japan and China was under way from 1937, building on Japanese imperial expansion since 1931. The conflict drew in states, colonies and civilians across nearly every inhabited continent. Its consequences included the destruction of European empires, the emergence of the United States and Soviet Union as superpowers, and new institutions meant to prevent another catastrophe.
