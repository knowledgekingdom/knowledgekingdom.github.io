In 1808, a municipal official in Spanish America might receive extraordinary news from across the ocean: French armies had entered Spain, the Bourbon royal family had been forced into abdication, and Napoleon had placed his brother Joseph on the Spanish throne. In cities such as Mexico City, Caracas, Bogotá, Buenos Aires, and Quito, this news sharpened a question that had once seemed theoretical. If the recognized king could not govern, who could legally command the king's subjects? A royal bureaucrat might demand obedience to existing institutions; a city council might claim that power returned temporarily to the community; a trader might fear that any dispute would interrupt credit and shipping.

The story did not begin with a settled desire for dozens of independent republics. Many early political actors insisted they were defending the legitimate king Ferdinand VII, even as they created assemblies that could act without him. Others wanted more local autonomy, reform of imperial commerce, or an entirely different social order. As the wars developed, choices hardened and old loyalties broke. The political map familiar today was not waiting fully formed to emerge from a crumbling empire. It had to be constructed by conflict, negotiation, institutions, and sometimes sheer contingency.

This chapter asks two different questions. <strong>Why did Spanish and Portuguese rule come under pressure?</strong> And <strong>what happened after people gained independence?</strong> The second question matters as much as the first. A victorious army might remove a royal governor, but it could not instantly create dependable tax revenues, agreement about provincial boundaries, representative government, courts trusted by every community, or a military willing to obey civilian authority.
