Imagine three countries sharing a sea. The first is a wealthy trading state with a powerful navy. The second is a continental state with a large population. The third is a small country whose ports serve both. A photograph appears in the newspapers: the leaders of the trading state and the continental state are shaking hands. Commentators declare that their rivalry is over. A month later, the two governments disagree over a regional election; meanwhile the small country signs an agreement with both. Was the handshake a lie? Not necessarily. It might have represented agreement on shipping, a temporary reduction of tensions, or simply a commitment to keep speaking when interests diverge. The scene is fictional, but the puzzle is familiar.

Diplomacy begins from the fact that political communities cannot choose all their neighbours, trading partners or problems. Even governments that despise each other may need to exchange prisoners, discuss borders, manage diseases, protect travellers or prevent an accident from becoming a war. Even close allies can disagree about who should pay for their security. A relationship may contain cooperation, rivalry and genuine hostility simultaneously. Calling two countries either friends or enemies can hide the actual structure of their relationship.

Chapter 42 examined why wars begin; Chapter 43 examined how geography shapes power; Chapter 44 asked how resources become military capacity. We now examine the political institutions through which governments try to change outcomes without necessarily fighting. The central question is not whether power or diplomacy matters more. It is how power, promises, rules, perceptions and domestic choices interact, and why arrangements that provide security today can create insecurity tomorrow.
