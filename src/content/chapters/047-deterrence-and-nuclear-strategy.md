Imagine two countries separated by an ocean. Their governments distrust one another, maintain powerful armed forces and interpret the other's diplomatic statements with suspicion. Yet for decades neither attacks the other's homeland. A newspaper announces that the absence of war proves the success of the terrible weapons both possess. A peace activist responds that the two countries have survived in spite of these weapons, not because of them. A historian points to trade, alliances, public opinion and several occasions on which diplomacy prevented a confrontation. All three are attempting to explain an event that never occurred.

This is the central intellectual problem of nuclear deterrence. Prevention is unusually difficult to observe. If a bridge collapses, investigators can examine the wreckage. If it does not collapse, they may have to distinguish good engineering from mild weather, low traffic or simple fortune. The absence of a nuclear war does not reveal what would have happened in a world without nuclear weapons. Equally, the existence of terrifying risks does not establish that the risks produced no restraint. The argument requires counterfactual reasoning, a careful reading of historical decisions, and an honest account of uncertainty.

There is a second problem. The people who make decisions about nuclear weapons do not bear all the consequences. A farmer in a distant neutral country, a child in a city or a community living beside a former test site may be affected by decisions in which they have no voice. A national security policy can create an international danger.
