Imagine a family grocery shop in a small town. A new apartment building opens nearby, and customer visits surge. At first, the owners celebrate: sales are climbing, the shop is becoming better known, and more customers are recommending it. But a month later the shop has empty shelves, employees are exhausted, and customers are leaving negative reviews. The manager orders extra stock. Deliveries begin to clog the narrow storeroom; some products expire before they reach the shelves. Hiring another worker does not immediately help, because the new employee needs training and the checkout area has room for only one cashier. Revenue has risen, yet the operation feels less successful than before.

You could blame the supplier, the manager, the employees or the new customers. Each may play a part. But looking for one guilty person misses the more useful question: <strong>what structure makes this pattern likely?</strong> Demand increases faster than restocking; congestion slows replenishment; poor availability disappoints customers; disappointment changes demand; rushed purchasing increases waste; and training new staff takes time. Several causes circle back to change the very conditions that produced them. The shop is not a pile of separate problems. It is a system.

This is an invented example, but its logic appears in real businesses, hospitals, transport networks, governments and ecosystems. Systems thinking does not mean that everything is connected to everything in a mystical way. It means that important outcomes often arise from <strong>interactions</strong>, not from the properties of individual parts taken alone. The challenge is to recognize the boundaries, relationships, delays and incentives that generate the behavior we observe.

Earlier chapters taught us to question claims, test arguments, read probabilities, examine historical evidence and interpret data. This chapter adds a different skill: the ability to ask why sensible actions, taken one after another, can produce an outcome that nobody wanted. That is one of the most powerful ideas you can carry into the later chapters on empires, wars, economic crises, climate, public health and technological change.
