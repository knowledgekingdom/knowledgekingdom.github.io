Imagine Athens in 415 BCE. A great assembly has been called to debate whether the city should send a powerful military expedition across the sea to Sicily. Supporters picture new allies, strategic advantage and the prestige of victory. Skeptics warn that the Athenians already face dangerous enemies nearer home. The men listening can interrupt, cheer and eventually vote. Some are farmers, some craftspeople, some wealthy estate owners, some sailors who know exactly what a long campaign might demand. The women, enslaved people and resident foreigners whose lives might also be changed are not there as equal political voters. This opening is a historically informed reconstruction, not a transcript of any one person's thoughts.

We know a great deal about this debate because the historian Thucydides preserved an influential narrative of competing speeches. He did not give us a sound recording. He composed speeches to express what he judged appropriate to each occasion and the overall sense of what was said. This is a perfect beginning for a book about reasoning and history. The assembly represents a startling form of citizen power, but its evidence is mediated by a historian, its membership is limited, and its decisions can be catastrophically wrong. Athens voted to proceed. Its Sicilian expedition ended in a disastrous defeat.

Greek history is frequently told as the birth of Western civilization, a phrase that can conceal more than it explains. Greek communities learned from Egypt, the Levant, Anatolia and other neighbors; Greek-speaking people lived beyond the borders of modern Greece; and their political systems differed dramatically from one another. Athens was not Greece, Sparta was not simply Athens with fewer arguments, and the world of Homer was not the world of Socrates. Instead of celebrating an inevitable march toward democracy, we will study a sequence of contested <strong>political experiments</strong>. What made a citizen? Was law superior to a powerful individual? Could the many govern themselves? What happened when a democracy became an empire?
