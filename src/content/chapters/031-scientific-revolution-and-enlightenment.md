Imagine a reader opening Isaac Newton's <em>Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy</em> in 1687. Its pages do not offer a collection of amusing discoveries. They build demanding mathematical arguments about bodies in motion and a force that reaches across the heavens. A reader who accepts the reasoning is being invited to see the fall of an apple and the movement of the Moon as related phenomena, not separate mysteries governed by unrelated rules. This is a composite teaching scene, not a record of a particular reader or of Newton watching a falling apple.

Now move to Paris in the middle of the eighteenth century. The editors of a new <em>Encyclopédie</em> seek to gather learning about philosophy, science, manufacturing, and ordinary crafts. A reader can move from questions about political authority to engravings of tools and workshops. This work, too, is controversial. To some authorities, sharing such knowledge promises improvement; to others, the editors are challenging established religious and political hierarchies.

These books mark different, overlapping ambitions. The Scientific Revolution is a historical label for changing explanations of the natural world, especially in Europe during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The Enlightenment describes varied eighteenth-century movements that subjected institutions, customs, and claims of authority to investigation and criticism. Neither was a clean break in which everyone suddenly became rational. Both developed within unequal societies where religious belief, monarchy, empire, and slavery remained powerful.
