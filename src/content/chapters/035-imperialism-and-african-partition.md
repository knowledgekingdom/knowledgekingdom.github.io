Imagine a clerk in a European foreign ministry, late in the nineteenth century, carefully marking the course of a river on a map. On the page, an ink line appears to separate two colonial possessions. On the ground, it may cross fields, seasonal grazing routes, market paths and the jurisdiction of a ruler whose representatives have not seen the map. The official's pen does not immediately create a government in the countryside; soldiers, local interpreters, taxes, treaties and years of resistance may follow. This scene is a composite teaching device, not a documented meeting. It introduces the central problem of this chapter: <strong>how did diplomatic claims turn into attempts to control real places and people?</strong>

By the First World War, European powers controlled or claimed nearly the entire African continent, even though in much of Africa direct European territorial rule had been limited only a few decades earlier. Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Italy and Spain built empires under competing legal, commercial and political arrangements. Algeria had come under French invasion in 1830; southern Africa had a longer history of European settlement and conflict. The so-called <em>Scramble for Africa</em> was not one campaign or a single conference. It was a collection of processes that accelerated greatly from the late 1870s to 1914.

The conquest was neither inevitable nor universally successful. African states could deploy armies, conduct diplomacy, mobilize religious movements and make strategic bargains. Ethiopia retained its sovereignty after defeating Italy in 1896, though its own empire expanded over other peoples. In other places conquest imposed forced labor, land alienation and racial hierarchies. A serious explanation must connect the political economy of Europe with the independent histories of Africa, without turning Africans into passive figures in a story about European choices.
