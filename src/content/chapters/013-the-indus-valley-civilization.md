Imagine approaching the city now called Mohenjo-daro, in the lowlands of the Indus basin, during the third millennium BCE. The approach is an imagined reconstruction, not a quotation from an ancient traveler's diary. You see substantial brick buildings, carefully placed streets and structures built for handling water. You may pass a craft worker drilling a tiny stone bead or a merchant carrying a square seal. Other people are drawing water, repairing drains, preparing food and tending animals. The city is busy with decisions that archaeologists can only partly recover. What can a ruler demand here? How is property transferred? Who settles arguments over a failed delivery? We do not have a deciphered archive capable of answering those questions directly. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

This is the paradox at the heart of the Indus civilization. In Mesopotamia, clay tablets allow us to hear administrators counting barley, rulers boasting of victories, families negotiating obligations and scribes copying literature. In Egypt, hieroglyphic and cursive records reveal a recognizable world of kingship, temples and diplomacy. In the Indus region, there are inscriptions and impressive physical remains, but no generally accepted decipherment of its signs. We can observe the infrastructure of a major urban tradition without confidently naming its kings, reading its contracts or repeating its inhabitants' prayers. \[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-3" class="cite">3</a>\]\[<a href="/013-the-indus-valley-civilization/sources/#source-4" class="cite">4</a>\]

That silence should not tempt us to invent the missing information. Modern accounts have sometimes turned the civilization into an ideal peaceful society, a rigid priestly state, a mysterious lost culture, or the direct and uncontested ancestor of a preferred present-day identity. All these stories say more than the surviving evidence can securely establish. Our task is harder and more rewarding: start with what excavators found, ask what it reasonably implies, test rival explanations, and distinguish strong patterns from guesses. By the end of this chapter, you should be able to explain why the Indus civilization deserves a place beside Egypt and Mesopotamia—and why it must be understood on its own terms rather than as a deficient version of either.
