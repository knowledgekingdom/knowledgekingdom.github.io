In November 1989 crowds gathered around the Berlin Wall, a fortified division that had separated East and West Berlin for nearly three decades. Some demanded permission to travel, others climbed barriers, and guards confronted instructions that had suddenly become ambiguous. The scene was dramatic, but it did not mean that the Cold War had ended everywhere at once. Soviet institutions still existed; wars continued in Afghanistan and other places; governments in Africa and Asia faced struggles that did not originate in Berlin. The wall's opening mattered because it exposed the rapid loss of coercive authority in one important European state and accelerated changes already underway.

The world that followed the Second World War had been shaped by two extraordinary and intertwined processes: rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union, and the dismantling of many European colonial empires. Neither can be understood as a simple morality play or as a succession of leaders' biographies. Ordinary people lived through economic development, surveillance, nuclear fear, national liberation, land redistribution, forced migration, and demands for political freedom. The central question of this chapter is how these large forces interacted—and why the disappearance of one superpower did not eliminate violence, inequality, or geopolitical competition.
