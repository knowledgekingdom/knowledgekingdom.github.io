Imagine a port clerk in eighteenth-century Bahia, on the coast of Portuguese America. A ship has arrived from the other side of the Atlantic. The clerk has columns for a vessel, an owner, a date and a count of people landed. He can calculate customs duties and record a sale. Yet the ledger cannot tell us what an arriving woman called herself, which language she spoke, whom she had lost, or what she intended to do to survive. This is a constructed teaching scene, not a documented encounter. Its imbalance captures a central problem of slavery's history: abundant records of commercial transactions coexist with fragmented evidence of human lives.

Across the Atlantic world, people treated as property built towns, cultivated fields, raised children, practiced religions, maintained languages, carried knowledge, and resisted domination. Many died during capture, confinement, transport or forced labor. Others formed communities and made claims to dignity and freedom that survived the institutions designed to suppress them. No adequate history can treat them only as entries in a trade table. But to understand the scale and mechanisms of the system, historians must learn to read those tables—and recognize their silences.

This chapter asks how slavery became embedded in Atlantic political economies, who benefited, who was coerced, how Africans and their descendants shaped events, and why emancipation did not instantly remove centuries of inequality. It extends Chapter 27's maritime expansion and Chapter 28's biological exchanges into a history of labor, law, power and struggle.
