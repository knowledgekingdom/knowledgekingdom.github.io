Imagine a worker arriving at a storehouse near the Euphrates more than five thousand years ago. A gatekeeper watches as baskets of barley are unloaded. The grain will support people who do not grow it themselves: builders, craftworkers, perhaps temple personnel. One basket appears to be missing. Was it stolen, lost in transit, retained by a household, or never loaded at all? The question matters because food is both sustenance and an obligation. Someone has promised to deliver an amount, someone else depends on receiving it, and the difference can affect tomorrow's work.

Our storehouse and its workers are imagined, not the reconstruction of a particular recovered tablet. But the problem is historically grounded. Some of the earliest tablets found at Uruk record quantities of goods and workers' rations. Long before anyone wrote a novel on paper, people were making durable marks to remember what an institution owed, stored, and distributed. \[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/011-mesopotamia-and-the-first-states/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]

To a modern reader, a grain account may seem an underwhelming beginning for civilization. Why begin our chapter with bookkeeping rather than a king and his army? Because the institution that knows how much grain it has, where it came from, and whom it must feed possesses a new kind of power. When hundreds or thousands of people depend on arrangements that extend beyond their personal acquaintances, memory alone becomes harder to use as the only system. People invent routines, offices, seals, records, and ways to settle disputes. Power grows from coordination as well as force.

Mesopotamia was not the place where all of human civilization was invented. Complex societies developed in other regions too, by different routes and on their own timelines. It was, however, home to some of the earliest large cities, surviving administrative texts, city-states, and territorial empires. The evidence is rich enough to let us watch cities and states taking shape—but fragmentary enough to require humility. By the end of this chapter, you should be able to explain why cities arose in southern Mesopotamia, distinguish a city from a state, trace the development of writing and political authority, and connect the region's institutions to later questions about law, war, economic life, and empire.
