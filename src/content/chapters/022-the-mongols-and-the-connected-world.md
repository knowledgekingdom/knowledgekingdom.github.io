In the 1240s, a traveler approaching a Mongol court might encounter several worlds within one camp. There were carts loaded with felt tents, herds of horses and sheep, armed guards, interpreters, envoys bringing tribute, and clerks who knew how to record orders in more than one language. A ruler might receive delegates from eastern Europe while officials discussed supplies for campaigns in China. A monastery, a mosque and the household of a steppe aristocrat could fall under the authority of the same imperial dynasty. <strong>This is a composite scene</strong>, assembled from characteristics described in contemporary sources, not an account of one witnessed meeting. It exposes the apparent puzzle of Mongol power: the conquerors depended on mobile pastoral traditions, yet ruled populations whose economies and institutions were strikingly different.

The empire created under <strong>Temüjin</strong>, known to history as <strong>Chinggis Khan</strong> (often spelled Genghis Khan), grew from political struggles in Mongolia around 1200 into a vast Eurasian power. By the later thirteenth century, related Mongol dynasties held or claimed lands stretching from eastern Asia to the Black Sea. Their armies besieged capitals, toppled governments and killed people on an appalling scale. They also recruited engineers and administrators, expanded relay routes, patronized religious institutions, and helped goods and ideas travel across existing networks. Both features belong to the history. Connections built by conquest were not gifts that erase the suffering that preceded them.

The key question is not whether the Mongols were simply <em>barbaric destroyers</em> or <em>agents of globalization</em>. Those two labels hide more than they explain. We need to ask <strong>how conquest, coercion, cooperation, administration and geography interacted</strong>, and whether a claimed imperial policy actually reached the people who lived under it. This chapter follows the empire from its founding in 1206 through its fourteenth-century transformations, while keeping the changing perspectives of Chinese, Persian, Central Asian, Russian and other societies in view.
