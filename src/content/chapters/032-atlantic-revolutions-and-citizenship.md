Imagine three gatherings separated by the Atlantic. In Philadelphia in 1776, delegates declare that the British colonies are independent and that legitimate government rests on consent. In Paris in 1789, representatives announce rights they describe as natural and common to human beings, challenging a society organized through legally privileged estates. In the French Caribbean colony of Saint-Domingue, beginning in 1791, enslaved people transform the meaning of freedom by taking up arms against plantation slavery. The settings differ: an imperial rebellion, a crisis in an old monarchy, and a struggle to destroy a system of racialized bondage.

All three movements spoke some language of rights, sovereignty, and political change. They did not want exactly the same things, and they did not produce the same result. An American planter who demanded independence from Britain could remain an enslaver. A French revolutionary who defended equality before the law could still accept restrictions on voting or hesitate to extend freedom to colonies. A formerly enslaved soldier in Saint-Domingue might judge both revolutions by the practical question of whether emancipation would survive. The word <em>liberty</em> united political slogans more easily than it settled conflicts over power.

This chapter follows an interconnected set of revolutions rather than a procession of national heroes. We will study governments, wars, documents, workplaces, and households; consider who could act as a citizen; and ask why universal promises were made in societies with sharp hierarchies. The goal is not to declare one revolution the most successful. It is to understand how competing answers to the same question—<strong>who has the right to rule, and who counts as the people?</strong>—reshaped the modern world.
