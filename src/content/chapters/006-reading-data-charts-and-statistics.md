Imagine two local news websites publishing reports about the same fictional town. The first headline reads: <em>Serious traffic collisions have doubled.</em> The second reads: <em>Road safety has improved dramatically.</em> It is tempting to assume one publication must be lying. Yet both could report true statements. The first might compare four recorded collisions last year with eight this year. The second might show that collisions per million journeys have fallen because the town's population, traffic, and reporting arrangements have changed. Four becoming eight is a doubling. A falling collision rate measures something else. Neither headline alone explains whether an ordinary journey has become safer.

Suppose last year there were four serious collisions across one million journeys, while this year there were eight across four million journeys. The count has doubled, but the rate has changed from four collisions per million journeys to two per million. This is a <em>hypothetical example</em>, not a claim about an actual town. Its lesson is not that rates always matter more than counts: emergency departments must still prepare for eight injured groups rather than four. It is that different questions require different denominators. A road engineer, an individual driver, and a hospital administrator may each reasonably ask about a different quantity.

Statistics is sometimes described as the science of collecting, analyzing and interpreting data. That is correct, but incomplete as a guide to life. Statistics is also a way of protecting yourself against the seduction of a neat number. A precise figure does not automatically represent a precise understanding. Every number is produced by a chain of choices: a definition, an observation, a recording method, an inclusion rule, an averaging rule, and usually a decision about what to display. When any link is weak, even flawless arithmetic can produce an unreliable conclusion.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to inspect a news chart, an economic claim, a scientific study, a survey, or a small-business report and ask competent questions before accepting its message. We will use fictional but realistic datasets so the arithmetic is transparent. In later chapters, these same skills will help us read unemployment figures, population pyramids, election polls, health research, climate records and the economic history of entire countries.
