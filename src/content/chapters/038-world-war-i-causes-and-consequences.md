Imagine opening a newspaper in a European capital at the end of June 1914. A prominent Habsburg heir and his wife have been shot while visiting Sarajevo. It is alarming news, but readers have witnessed diplomatic disputes and regional wars before. A railway clerk in Vienna, a shopkeeper in Belgrade, a French reservist and an Indian student in London have little reason to know that their lives may soon be altered by a much larger conflict. Within weeks, however, governments will mobilize armies numbering in the millions, and the movement of trains will become as urgent to generals as the movement of diplomats' messages.

This is an illustrative composite scene, not the description of one actual newspaper or household. Its point is the difference between a shocking event and the decisions that turn it into catastrophe. On 28 June 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand and Sophie were assassinated in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb connected to revolutionary nationalist networks. On 28 July Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia. By early August Germany, Russia, France, Britain and other states were fighting. The assassination did not itself trigger automatic declarations by treaty: officials made choices under pressure, often with poor information and grave assumptions about what their rivals would do. \[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/038-world-war-i-causes-and-consequences/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]
