Imagine a family sitting down to dinner in a modern European town. On the table are potatoes, a tomato salad, peppers and bread made from wheat. A cup of hot chocolate might follow. Nothing about this meal seems unusual. Yet a cook in Europe in 1400 could not have assembled it from those ingredients. Wheat was well established there; potatoes, tomatoes, cacao and the peppers we now call chiles were domesticated in the Americas. Their journeys into European kitchens began after sustained Atlantic contact.

Reverse the scene. A household in the Andes before Columbus might have cultivated potatoes and maize, tended llamas, and used sophisticated ways to preserve tubers. It would not have known domesticated cattle, pigs or horses brought from Eurasia. Across the Atlantic, plants and animals that had developed in different ecological and cultural settings began to meet. Some would flourish, others struggle; many would be transformed by new farming and eating traditions. These changes spread into Africa and Asia, not just between Europe and America.

The exchange was never simply a cheerful swap of useful foods. It also brought pathogens against which many communities had little prior exposure, and it unfolded within wars, seizure of land, slavery and forced migration.
