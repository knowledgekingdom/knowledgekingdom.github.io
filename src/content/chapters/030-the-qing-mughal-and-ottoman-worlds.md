Imagine three dispatches reaching different imperial courts around 1700. In Beijing, an official reports troubles along an Inner Asian frontier and discusses arrangements for soldiers and their families. At a Mughal court in the Indian subcontinent, a revenue officer argues over the income assigned to a military noble and the obligations of cultivators. In Istanbul, a provincial administrator petitions for confirmation of an arrangement to collect taxes and maintain local order. These are composite scenes, not transcriptions of surviving letters. They illustrate a shared question: how could rulers command immense territories when most people lived far from the capital and when armies, communications and taxation depended on intermediaries?

The Qing dynasty governed China and a vast Inner Asian empire. The Mughals ruled much of South Asia, although their power changed sharply across the period. The Ottomans linked southeastern Europe, Anatolia, the eastern Mediterranean, Arab provinces and North Africa. Their rulers claimed distinct kinds of legitimacy, used different military systems, and confronted different geographical limits. Yet all three depended upon food producers, merchants, local elites, written records and negotiated obedience. None was simply a ruler issuing orders to a passive population.

This chapter principally follows developments from roughly 1500 to 1800, reaching beyond these dates where necessary. The three empires were not founded together: the Ottoman state was older, the Mughal dynasty began in 1526, and Qing rule over Beijing began in 1644. Comparing them therefore requires attention to chronology. Their histories also challenge a familiar distortion: Europeans did not arrive in Asia to find politically empty or economically stagnant worlds. European commercial and military influence expanded within already powerful, connected societies. The eventual outcomes were not predetermined.
