Somewhere in the landscape of what is now northern Tanzania, a small group of early human relatives crossed wet volcanic ash. Their footprints remained where their feet had pressed into the ground. More ash eventually covered them. The tracks hardened; geological time passed; erosion and investigation finally returned them to human attention. At Laetoli, researchers identified a trail made about <strong>3.6 million years ago</strong>, probably by members of <em>Australopithecus afarensis</em>. Whatever they thought about their surroundings, those walkers could not have imagined a world of cities, passports, telescopes, and museums—or that later humans would debate their footprints in classrooms. \[<a href="/009-human-evolution-and-the-first-migrations/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]

The scene is worth lingering over. The footprints belong to creatures that walked upright long before the emergence of our species. They were not modern humans taking an unusually ancient stroll. They belonged to a different part of a branching evolutionary family. Their discovery demonstrates something remarkable: a behavior can leave evidence far older than anything written, yet the evidence does not tell us everything. The tracks can show that individuals moved on two feet; they cannot reveal the names they used, whether they told stories, or what they intended to do after crossing the ash.

This distinction—between what the evidence establishes and what we might imagine—is the foundation of the whole chapter. Human origins is a scientific and historical subject, not a collection of heroic certainties. Archaeologists and paleoanthropologists work with fragmentary remains, complicated dating methods, models of past environments, and sometimes competing interpretations. A newly discovered fossil can redraw parts of the family tree without making everything previously learned worthless. That is what good evidence does: it improves the picture, even when the picture becomes less simple.

Think of the last time you traveled to a place you had never been. Perhaps you followed a road, a river, or a bus route. You carried knowledge—directions, language, tools, expectations—from people who had lived before you. Now remove the roads, the cities, the country names, even the modern coastline. Imagine many generations moving through landscapes they knew only in pieces. That is closer to the history of early migrations than the familiar image of a single arrow leaving Africa and marching toward a modern destination.

The story begins long before our species existed. It ends, for this chapter, at the threshold of food production and farming. Along the way we will learn why Africa is central to human origins; why evolution is a branching process rather than a ladder; how researchers date ancient finds; why several human species shared the planet; how people moved across continents and oceans; and why even the most famous migration dates remain open to revision.
