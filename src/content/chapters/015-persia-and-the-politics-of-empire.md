Imagine a messenger leaving Sardis, a prosperous city in western Anatolia, carrying news meant for the royal court. Between him and the king lie mountains, rivers, guarded crossings, languages he does not speak, and provinces whose rulers may be as powerful locally as the king's own officers. The message might concern a border revolt, a tax shipment, a Greek fleet or a dispute over water. If it arrives too late, the king's decision may become useless. If it is altered, it could start a war. The distance between a throne and a village was not simply measured in miles: it was measured in the reliability of an entire political system.

From the middle of the sixth century BCE, the Achaemenid Persian Empire linked territories stretching from parts of southeastern Europe and North Africa across the Middle East to Central Asia and the northwestern Indian subcontinent. Its rulers governed peoples who planted different crops, worshipped different gods, obeyed different traditions and kept records in different scripts. In previous chapters, we met the small city-states of southern Mesopotamia, the Nile's durable kingdoms and the complicated archaeological landscapes of the Indus and early China. Persia now poses a bigger question: how does a government turn the conquest of many societies into a political order that can survive for generations?

The answer was neither an entirely new civilization nor a single administrative invention. Persian kings adopted older Mesopotamian and Egyptian practices, relied on local families, created monumental statements about their right to rule, moved people and goods through networks of roads, and applied force when cooperation failed. They also faced a difficult contradiction: the more distant their subjects were, the more they needed trusted intermediaries, and the stronger those intermediaries could become. This chapter treats the empire as a changing system, not as a list of kings or a contest between 'Persian despotism' and 'Greek freedom.' Both slogans tell us more about later storytellers than about the complexity of antiquity.
