Imagine a town in the fictional country of Lydora. A clerk still stamps documents bearing the national seal. A local council keeps the school open, although it receives little money from the capital. Families settle some disputes through neighborhood elders because the ordinary court rarely sits. The national flag has not disappeared, the country is still represented at the United Nations, and most residents would describe themselves as citizens of Lydora. Yet three different institutions exercise pieces of what an observer might call public authority. Who governs the town?

A crisis begins after an election. The losing party disputes the results; demonstrations spread; police abuse deepens mistrust. Eventually organized violence divides the country. A family that previously worried about school fees must decide whether to leave its home. A hospital loses its supply of essential medicines. An accountant whose political opinions have never mattered discovers that an identity recorded on a document can become a source of fear. Different inhabitants of the same town experience different wars.

This composite fictional story introduces three questions. What makes a state capable of governing? Why does political competition sometimes become sustained armed conflict? And how can institutions recover enough trust that disagreements again become matters for elections, courts and public debate rather than violence? We shall approach these questions through history, economics, political science and law. No one explanation will account for all conflicts, and explanation does not excuse a perpetrator's choices.
