Imagine a railway station in 1923. A demobilized soldier steps onto the platform carrying a small bag and a military coat that has lost its usefulness. His country is officially at peace, yet he returns to a town altered by inflation, disabled veterans, absent relatives, political demonstrations, and families who no longer agree about what the war meant. Shop signs are familiar, but the price of bread is not. An election poster promises national revival. Another demands land for peasants and justice for workers. A third insists that the previous government betrayed the nation. This is an illustrative composite, not a record of one particular traveler, but such experiences help explain why peace treaties alone did not restore ordinary confidence.

The years between the armistices of 1918 and the European war of 1939 are called the <em>interwar period</em>. The label is convenient, but dangerous. It makes the era sound like a pause between two inevitable wars. People living in 1924 could not know that another global war would begin. Some believed international arbitration, disarmament, social reform, and economic cooperation could produce a genuinely different future. Others feared revolution, sought revenge for lost territory, or embraced political movements that promised order through exclusion and violence. The central question is not simply why peace failed, but why several plausible futures coexisted and why particular decisions eventually closed them off. \[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-1" class="cite">1</a>\]\[<a href="/039-the-interwar-world/sources/#source-2" class="cite">2</a>\]
